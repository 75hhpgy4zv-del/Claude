## Chapter Twenty-Three: The Gap

The interview was for a podcast she had been on twice before, a longform thing, the kind that asked real questions and gave you room to answer them properly. She liked the host. She liked that the audience was the kind that listened rather than clipped. She had said yes immediately when they asked.

It was recorded on a Wednesday afternoon in a studio in Burbank, good equipment, good light, the kind of setup that made it easy to forget you were being recorded and just talk. That was what she did best, just talk.

The machine ran cleanly in rooms like this, well-oiled, the words arriving in the right order, the real things and the shaped things in their correct proportions.

They talked for two hours. Industry trajectory, the early years, the particular loneliness of scale. She said things she had said before and things she hadn't, the conversation finding its own level the way good conversations did. She talked about the stream room. She talked about what it cost to be visible and what it cost not to be. She talked, at one point, naturally, in passing, the way you mention something that is simply part of your life, about Derek.

She didn't plan it. Someone asked about the version of her that existed offline, the self that the camera didn't reach, and she said: I've been thinking about that more lately. I'm with someone who lives pretty far outside this world. A teacher. He does a film podcast, it's really good, eighty-seven listeners. And being with someone who has no stake in any of this, who isn't watching the numbers, who doesn't know what a brand deal is, who just thinks I'm a person. It does something. It reminds you the person is still there.

She said it warmly. She meant it warmly. She moved on.

She drove home thinking about other parts of the conversation. She was almost home when the Derek part arrived back — the teacher, eighty- seven listeners, he just thinks I'm a person — and something in her chest shifted in a way she did not immediately identify. She parked. She went upstairs.

She cleaned the apartment. Not because it needed it. She wiped the counters and folded the throw and moved things that were already in their correct place to more precisely their correct place, and last she ran a cloth over the grandmother's teapot until it caught the light. Then she sat at the island and looked at her phone and thought about what she had said and whether it had been his to hear first, and did not yet know the answer.

* * *

The episode went up the following Tuesday.

She had forgotten the Derek part by then, not deliberately, just the way you forget the parts of a conversation that felt like connective tissue rather than content. She was thinking about the industry trajectory section, which she wanted to clip. She was thinking about the stream room section, which had said something she hadn't said before. She was already somewhere else when the DMs started coming in.

Not a flood. Just a steady current. People who had listened. People who had thoughts about the eighty-seven listeners. People who found the podcast and were now subscribed. A few people who said: I didn't know you were seeing someone, that's really nice, he seems great.

She thought: oh. Right.

She texted Derek that afternoon: I mentioned you in an interview. It went up today. Just wanted you to hear it from me first.

The last part wasn't accurate. He hadn't heard it from her first. He had been in the interview without knowing he was going to be in the interview, and the interview had been up for six hours, and she was telling him now.

She was aware of this. She noted it briefly and then the day continued.

* * *

He called that evening.

She knew from the first word that it was going to be one of those calls. Not the glass calls; he was present, temperature was right. But there was a precision in his voice, the careful voice, the one he used when he had been thinking about something for longer than the conversation had been going.

"The interview," he said.

"I know. I should have told you before it went up — " 

"You should have told me before you said it."

She was quiet for a moment. "I was talking about us. It wasn't — I wasn't making you content. I was just saying something true about my life."

"I know you were."

"So I don't understand what I did wrong."

"You made a decision," he said finally, "about how I appear in your world. Without asking me."

"I mentioned you. I said something kind about you. I said your podcast was good."

"I know what you said."

"Then — " "Maya." The particular patience of him, the precision. "Eleven million people now know I exist. They know what I do, approximately, and that I'm with you. I didn't choose that. You chose it. The interview chose it. And it happened and I found out from a text six hours after the fact."

She felt the tight thing in her chest, the defensive shape of it. "I talk about my life. That's the whole thing — that's what I do, I'm honest about my life, that's why people — " "I know that's what you do."

"So you knew this was part of it. Being with me means occasionally being — " "Being what?" Quiet. Level. Waiting.

She stopped. "Being adjacent to my world," she said. Carefully.

"Adjacent." He let the word sit. "Yeah."

"I don't know what you want from me. I can't pretend you don't exist. I'm not going to hide you."

"I'm not asking you to hide me."

"Then what are you asking?"

"I'm asking to be part of the decision. Not approval. Not veto. Just a conversation first. Just: I'm thinking of mentioning you, is that okay. That's the whole ask."

"I don't do that. I don't run my content past people before — " 

"I'm not people."

The sentence landed. She was quiet.

"I know you're not," she said. "I'm sorry. I said it wrong."

"Can I say the full thing?" he said.

"You can always say the thing."

"I feel like I don't have any autonomy in your world. Not because you're taking it, just the world moves and I'm in it and the decisions get made and I find out after. At the gala I was in the frame before I understood I was in the frame. In the interview I was in the episode before I knew there was an episode. And each time it's warm, it's kind, you mean it well, and each time I'm the last to know I've been somewhere." A pause. "That is a pattern. I have been watching it for months and giving it the benefit of the doubt and calling it the terms we set. And last night I ran out of ways to explain it to myself."

The weight of it landed differently than the rest. Not an individual complaint, a conclusion. She heard the months in it.

"I feel like sometimes you keep score in a way you don't tell me about," she said. "And then the score comes up and I didn't know there was a game."

"The gala wasn't score-keeping." No heat. Which was almost harder than heat. "At the gala the glass was starting and the conversation had nowhere to go, and pushing it would have made a mess of something I didn't have the right words for yet. So I didn't. I was protecting what we had."

"And then things got better and you decided to let it go."

"I thought maybe I'd been wrong about what it meant. Genuinely. And then the interview."

"So I don't get credit for the gala being okay."

"You get credit. I'm saying the interview didn't come from nowhere. It came from a pattern, and I didn't say anything the first time because I was trying to protect us both from a conversation we weren't ready to have.

That's different from keeping score."

She sat with that. She could feel the accuracy of it and she did not know what to do with the feeling. He had been reading her correctly at the gala, had known she couldn't receive it, had been right about her. The account being even would have been easier.

"I don't know how to be in your world," he said, "without your world deciding what I am in it first. And I don't think you know there's a difference between including me and knowing me. In your world those are the same thing. I don't think they are."

Silence. She was sitting with it. He could hear her sitting with it.

"I'm not an island," she said. Not the arguing register, something quieter, the voice from underneath the argument. "I know what I have to figure out. I know the machine is real and I know what it does and I know there are things I owe you that I haven't worked out how to give yet. But I am not an island. And sometimes I feel like you are standing on the shore deciding whether the water is safe, and I am here, I have been here, and the water is fine, and you will not come in."

He heard it. He felt the weight of it. And because he felt it and could not sit with it, he reached for the record, because the record was real and he needed her to know it was real.

"I have shown up," he said. Not the defensive shape — the precise shape, the one he used when he needed something to land accurately. "I came back from the cold days. Every time. I told you about Priya at the table, the whole of it, not the managed version. When Vijay left I was at the worst I'd been in two years and I called you and I let you come over and I let you see it. I have given you the actual weight. That is not nothing. That is the most I have given anyone since the divorce and I need you to know I know that it's real."

A pause. Not her dismissing it. Her holding it.

"I know it's real," she said. Still that quiet register, which made what came next worse than if she had raised her voice. "I know every one of those things. I was there for all of them." A pause. "I was there before you handed them to me."

He said nothing.

"The Vijay visit," she said. "I knew before you called. I had been watching the texts for two days, the response times, the way you went flat. I had already adjusted what I was doing, cleared the afternoon, had the food ordered before I was in my car. You called and I said I'm coming and you said you don't have to and I said I know and I came." She paused. "You thought you were letting me in. I was already there. I had been standing there for two days and when you finally opened the door you felt brave for opening it and I was glad, I was so glad, I said the right things because I meant them. But I had already crossed toward you. You did not know I had moved."

The room was very quiet.

"That's not me saying your courage wasn't real," she said. "It was. I watched it and I counted it. Every time. I'm saying I was already on the other side of the door when you opened it and you didn't know I was there and I have been carrying that alone because you were not ready to know it yet and I did not want to cost you something you weren't ready to spend." A pause, and her voice when it came back was exhausted rather than angry. "I have been very careful with you. And I need to know if the glass actually comes down. Or whether it just gets thinner sometimes and I'm supposed to be grateful for the view."

The sentence sat in the room between them.

He had come into this call with the full weight of the gala, the interview, the pattern finally named. He still had all of it. She had not taken any of it. She was still wrong about the interview and he was still right about the machine. And she had just reached through all of it and found the thing underneath and named it out loud, and he had nothing — not because his case was wrong but because hers was true alongside it, and the two truths together were the gap, and he was standing in it, and there was no answer available that addressed both things at once.

"I need to think," he said.

"I know."

"That's not me putting this down."

"I know what it is," she said. He recognized his own words, from the car after the gala, and could not tell if she meant it that way.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay."

Neither of them said goodbye. They just ran out of things and the call ended.

* * *

He stood in his kitchen for a long time without moving.

The glass was at two and settling. But underneath it was something else, not the glass, not anger, not the tiredness of a hard conversation. It was the specific sensation of a person who has been precise about someone else for a long time and has just had the precision turned around.

She already knew.

He had told her about the depression like it was an act of trust. He had been quietly proud of it, the difficult thing, finally done. He had told himself: I gave her this, I let her in, I took the real risk. And she had said the right things and he had felt the glass go thin with the relief of it.

She had already known. She had noticed, adjusted, waited, managed the whole weight of it without him, and then he had arrived weeks later with the information she had already processed and offered it like a gift.

And she had received it gently, because she was paying attention, because she had been paying attention from the beginning, and he had not seen the paying-attention as evidence of anything. He had seen it as a passing grade.

He thought about the threshold. The one he had never told her existed. The proof he had been waiting for, moving every time she reached it. He had told himself it was caution. He had told himself it was the reasonable care of a man who had been burned. He had been quietly proud, even, of how fair he was being, how he had not closed the door, how he was watching and giving her the chance.

And she had been on the other side of it. Having already crossed toward him without being asked, handling the things he hadn't trusted her with, and waiting for him to notice she had moved.

He thought about Rajan. Not the careful that protects you. The careful that decides it won't work so you don't have to find out.

He had believed he was not doing that. He was not sure he believed it anymore.

She was right about the glass. He was right about the machine.

Neither cancelled the other. Both of them at the gap. Both waiting for the other to jump first. Both calling it the other person's fault.

He went to bed. He lay in the dark with the understanding, which was not comfortable and did not resolve. He had been asking her to cross a distance he had also been maintaining. He had never given her the real weight to hold. And she had found the edges of what he had offered and stayed anyway, and he had received her staying as insufficient proof, and moved the threshold again.

He did not fall asleep for a long time.

In her apartment across the city, Maya made tea she didn't finish and sat at the kitchen island.

She had said it. The thing that had been sitting in her for weeks, the pattern she had felt but not named, the glass she could feel but not touch, the sense that she was being watched for evidence of something she didn't know she was supposed to provide. She had said it plainly and it had landed and she did not regret saying it.

She was also aware that she had said other things tonight that she was going to have to sit with. He had been right about the interview. He had been right about the gala, the pattern, the last-to-know quality of it. The score quote had stung because it was partly true, not the whole of it, but a corner of it. She had been so focused on the accuracy of her own case that she hadn't let herself feel the accuracy of his, and she could feel it now, in the kitchen, with the tea going cold. The machine was real. The thing he'd named in the room of six hundred people was real. She had done it and she had not known she was doing it and not knowing was not the same as not doing.

But. She had been there. She had figured it out before he told her.

She had held things he hadn't handed her and handled them without making a thing of the handling. She had done that and he had not seen it, or had seen it and filed it as a passing grade on a test rather than evidence of a person who was paying attention, and she needed him to know the difference.

She needed to know if the glass came down.

She rinsed her mug. She went to bed. She lay in the dark and did not try to resolve the conversation or process it into something shapeable, just let it be what it was, unfinished, both of them right and both of them owing something, the gap between them real and also, she thought, possibly crossable.

She did not know who was going to have to jump first.

She fell asleep slowly, and it was not a comfortable sleep, and she did not expect it to be.


## Chapter Twenty-Four: The First Take

He didn't go to bed.

He sat in the dark for a while and then he got up and went to his desk and sat in front of the microphone, not because he had decided to record but because the desk was there and the microphone was there and the alternative was the bedroom, which at two in the morning had a quality of accusation he was not equipped to negotiate with tonight.

He hadn't planned this. He had been circling the Ferris Bueller episode for weeks, the one that kept arriving in the margins of his thoughts and retreating when he looked at it directly. It was too close. That was the problem. He had been waiting until it was less close, until he could hold it at the analytical distance that made his best episodes. He had not been waiting to record it in the dark at two in the morning after the worst conversation he'd had in two years.

He set the recording level. He clicked the light on. He talked.

He talked about Cameron the way he always talked about things when no one was listening — without the slight self-monitoring that even his most honest episodes carried, the awareness of the eventual audience that shaped the sentences toward the receivable. He was not thinking about an audience. He was in the dark in his apartment at two in the morning and the glass was at two and the conversation was still in the room with him, both of them right and neither of them able to cross to the other side of it, and he talked.

He talked about Cameron at the window. About what it meant to organize your entire interior life around the conviction that the world was not safe for the full version of you — not because anyone had told you so in any single decisive moment, but because the evidence had accumulated slowly, the way water gets into a foundation. The small hesitations. The patience that was visible as patience. The learning to minimize before the minimization was requested. He talked about how that kind of person builds things — good things, real things, the podcast, the classroom, the apartment full of chosen objects — and how the building is genuine and matters and is not the same as letting someone in.

He was talking about Cameron.

He was also, and he could hear it happening in his own voice and could not stop it, talking about himself.

He talked about the moment Cameron kicks the car. About what it requires, that particular act of destruction. Not rage. The decision, finally, that what you have been protecting was never worth protecting in the first place. He talked about what Cameron was protecting: not the car. The relationship to the car. The arrangement he had made with his own life, the window that had become the room. You stop seeing furniture after a while.

You just live in it.

He stopped the recording.

He sat in the dark for a moment. His heart was doing something. Not dramatically — just the physical register of a thing said that had not been said before, the body catching up with the mouth.

He put the headphones on.

This was his practice — he always listened back before he slept on it, caught the threads that went sideways, noted where the argument needed shoring up. He was good at the clinical part of it, the self-editing, the slight remove of the craftsman examining the work. He put the headphones on expecting to do that.

He got through the first ten minutes. He was tracking the argument — it was good, actually, better than he expected from a two-in-the-morning take, the Cameron analysis moving cleanly from the window to the institution to the particular weight of being loved by someone who sees your potential and not your cost. He was tracking it with the evaluative part of his brain, and then somewhere in the middle of it his own voice said something and he stopped tracking.

He had been talking about what it meant to wait. The specific quality of waiting that is not patience but its disguise. Cameron in his room.

Cameron in his car. Cameron performing the symptoms of illness to stay home from a life he was afraid was not, at its core, built for him. And his own voice, in the recording, had said, with the quiet of something finally arrived at: I think I've been waiting my whole life for someone to want the actual information. Not the edited version. Not the version I've decided is acceptable to hand someone. The actual weight of the thing. And I think someone does. And I think the reason I can't cross to her right now is that I'm still in the window. Still protecting the arrangement. Still convincing myself that the window is the room.

He had not planned to say that.

He could hear in the recording that he had not planned to say it — the slight surprise in his own voice, the pause after, the way he had then moved back to Cameron as though he could retroactively make it about Cameron.

He took the headphones off. He sat at the desk and the glass, which had been at two when he sat down, was not at two anymore.

He did a quiet internal survey. He knew how to do this, had been doing it for years, the careful read of his own interior the way you check a building for structural damage after something has moved the ground. The world was arriving at a distance. Not outside him — he was outside it. The coffee mug on the desk was a correct object but it was not reaching him at full temperature. The apartment was the apartment and it was also faintly and persistently behind glass.

He was at a four.

He had not been at a four since the winter after the divorce.

He knew, with the flat precision of long familiarity, that this was not the fight's fault. The fight had not done this. The recording had. He had said the true thing into the microphone and the true thing had done what true things did when you had been managing them for a long time: it had arrived at full size, finally, and the size was a four.

He saved the file. He labeled it: ferris_bueller_DELETE.

He did not delete it.

He went to the bedroom. He lay on top of the covers and did not turn off the lamp and looked at the ceiling. Outside the city continued its indifferent business, the freeway a low surf, the occasional car. The glass was at four and the ceiling was the ceiling and somewhere across the city she was asleep.

He was aware of two things simultaneously: that he had been right about what she'd done, and that the recording had just shown him, in his own voice, what he had been doing in return. The window. The arrangement. Two people each on their side of a thing they had both been too afraid to cross.

He was not okay.

He was also, somewhere under the not-okay, more honest than he had been before he sat down.

He fell asleep with the lamp on.

He went to the kitchen. He put water in the kettle. The kettle boiled.

He made coffee. He drank it standing at the counter. He put the mug in the sink.

He texted the school: not coming in today. He texted Maya: not a great morning. give me a few days.

She responded in four minutes: okay. I'm here.

He put the phone on the nightstand.

He sat at the desk. The file was on the desktop, labeled DELETE. He did not open it.

He put the Coltrane on.

The afternoon came. He was in the apartment when it arrived and he was in the apartment when it left. He ate something at some point. He replied to two emails from students, both replies correct, both complete, neither of them followed by anything.

He went to bed.

In the morning he got up.

He went to the kitchen. He put water in the kettle.

* * *

On the second evening he opened the file.

He put the headphones on. His own voice came through them. It talked about Cameron. It talked about the window. It said: I think someone does. I think the reason I can't cross to her right now is that I'm still in the window.

He listened to it twice.

He took the headphones off.

He went to bed.

* * *

On the third morning the coffee had a taste.

Not much. A suggestion of one. But it arrived and he registered it as something beyond temperature, and he stood at the counter and finished the cup and the standing had a quality that the standing the day before had not had, something faint and early and not yet nameable.

By evening it was there. Not all the way. But enough.

Then Wednesday. He woke up and the light through the blinds was the light through the blinds, the particular Wednesday-morning lines of it, and he lay there and watched them move as the clouds moved outside and he was there for the watching. Present. The ceiling not just a surface but his ceiling, the one he had looked at for three years, the one that was particular and known.

He made eggs. Properly. Butter, low flame, patience. He ate them at the table and the eggs were good and the Coltrane was on and the Coltrane was the Coltrane.

He texted Maya: I'm back. Really back.

She responded in two minutes: I know. You sound like yourself.

He held the phone. She had been on the other side of the glass this whole time and she had not knocked on it and she had not required anything from the other side of it and she had said I'm here and meant it and waited. He had not responded to the I'm here. He had not been able to.

She had not made a thing of that.

He put the phone down. He was hungry in the real way. He picked up his mug. Outside the city was doing its ordinary thing and he was in it, the temperature of it reaching him without translation, and he sat with that for a while before he did anything else.


## Chapter Twenty-Five: Three Days

She didn't post for three days.

Derek had texted two days after the gap fight: not a great morning.

give me a few days. She had said okay. She had said I'm here. She had meant both.

This was not a statement, or she told herself it wasn't. She had not decided to be silent. She simply didn't post, because the things she would have posted felt wrong in a way she was having trouble locating, felt like taking something that needed to stay in the room and putting it outside. She had learned enough about the cost of that reflex to be cautious. That was what she told herself.

What she didn't tell herself was the other thing: that the not-posting was also easier than the alternative. That the alternative was to sit with what had happened at the gala, really sit with it, without making it into content, without reaching for the version of it that was communicable and interesting and fourteen minutes long. That was harder. The three days were not courage. They were also, partly, avoidance of the harder thing.

She was getting better at noticing when she was doing this. She was not yet always able to stop.

Her community noticed. Not with alarm. They had seen her take breaks before, knew she was human in the way they always claimed to know she was human while also requiring her to be consistently available in ways that humans are not. The comments on her last video accumulated at their usual pace and she read them from a slight remove, as though through a window, which was not how she usually read them. She usually read them from inside the warmth of it, inside the reciprocal thing she had built, the eleven million people who had at some point decided she was worth watching and who collectively represented, in their aggregate attention, the closest thing she had to a community in the old sense of the word.

From outside the window they looked different. Not worse. More legible, maybe. She could see the architecture of it: the affection that was real, the projection that was also real, the people who were watching her and the people who were watching the version of her they had assembled from seven years of content, and the gap between those two audiences which was not vast but was not nothing.

She thought about what Derek had said, that the curated version would have made that into a video, and she thought about all the things she had made into videos over the years, all the real things that had passed through the frame and come out the other side as content, cleaned up and lit correctly and given a thumbnail, and she was not ashamed of any of it, she had never been dishonest, she had always meant what she said. But she was thinking, for the first time, about what stayed on the other side of the frame. What didn't make it through. What she had protected by keeping it dark.

On the second day her editor texted asking about the upload schedule.

She said she needed a few more days. He said okay and she could hear in the okay his professional concern, the slight calibration of a person who knew her well enough to read the difference, and well enough not to ask.

On the second night she almost called Jess. She got as far as opening the contact and then she sat there with the phone in her hand for a long time.

The thing about Jess was that Jess would listen and ask the right questions and she would talk and the talking would shape the thing into something coherent and by the end of the call she would understand what had happened at the gala the way you understand something that has been given a good edit: clear, legible, with a beginning and a middle and a provisional end. And then she would feel better. She would feel the particular relief of the processed thing, the thing that had been brought out of the interior and given a form.

She put the phone down.

She wanted to sit with the unprocessed thing. The actual thing, still warm, still without a shape. She had been giving everything a shape for seven years. She wanted to know what it felt like to let something stay shapeless for a night.

She sat in the quiet apartment and let it stay shapeless. It was uncomfortable. It did not improve with time. By midnight it was still shapeless and uncomfortable and she had not called Jess and had not made a video and had not done anything with it at all, and she went to bed still carrying it and woke up still carrying it and by the third morning it had changed in the way that unprocessed things change when they've been held long enough: it had become something she could look at directly. Not resolved. Just visible. Just hers.

She ate well. She slept adequately. She went for a walk in the morning with the hat and the sunglasses and the particular pleasure of being unremarkable in the world, just a person on a sidewalk in the early Los Angeles light, and the walk was good and she did not document it.

* * *

Jess showed up on the third day.

No text first. Maya heard the knock and looked at the door for a moment before she opened it, the particular delay of someone who has been alone long enough that another person feels like a category change rather than just a person.

Jess had coffee. Two cups, the place on Hyperion, the specific order Maya had not asked for and did not need to ask for because Jess had known it for four years. She held one out. Maya took it. She stepped back and Jess came in.

She looked around the apartment the way she always looked around the apartment, the quick survey, cataloguing. Maya watched her do it. She knew what Jess was seeing: the particular tidiness of a space whose occupant had been managing it carefully for three days. Not dirty — never dirty. Clean in the specific way that meant someone had been cleaning because cleaning was the thing available to do.

Jess sat on the couch. She did not ask how Maya was doing.

Maya sat in the chair across from her, the one she always sat in, and held the coffee with both hands. Outside the afternoon was doing its Los Angeles thing. Neither of them said anything for a moment.

"You didn't text back," Jess said. Not an accusation. Just a fact set down on the table.

"I know. I needed some time."

"I know you did." Jess looked at her. "How long have you been in the apartment?"

A pause. "Since Tuesday."

"That's three days."

"I know how many days it is."

Jess nodded. She was quiet for a moment, her eyes moving around the room in the way that meant she was thinking rather than looking. Maya recognized it and waited.

"Can I say something?" Jess said.

"You're going to say it anyway."

"Yes," Jess said. "But I wanted to ask first."

Maya looked at her over the coffee. "Okay."

"I've been watching you do this for seven years," Jess said. Not unkindly. With the specific gentleness of someone who has chosen to say a hard thing and has decided the gentleness is the cost of the saying, not a way to soften it. "Not the camera thing. Not the content thing. I know about those, we've talked about those, that's a different conversation. I mean this." She gestured, slightly, at the apartment. At Maya in the chair. At the afternoon. "The going quiet. The systems. The apartment that's so clean I could eat off the floor. The three days of not calling anyone back and calling it taking space."

Maya held the coffee. She did not say anything.

"You've been doing it since before I had language for it," Jess said.

"Since before you had language for what Derek has. And I've been calling it Maya needs to decompress, Maya takes space, that's just how she processes. Because on the other side of it you were always fine and the fine was real and it seemed unkind to push on something that resolved itself." A pause. "I've been letting you call it space because you seemed okay."

"I am okay," Maya said. The words came out the way words came out when they had been said too many times already, slightly worn at the edges.

Jess looked at her. Just looked at her, with the dark eyes that missed nothing, with the seven years of knowing.

"Maya," she said.

"I am. I'm — it's been a hard week. I'm processing."

"I know you're processing. That's not what I'm saying." Jess set her coffee down on the table. "I'm saying the apartment is very clean and you haven't called me back and you haven't posted anything and you've been alone in here for three days and those things together mean something specific and I think you know what they mean."

The quiet in the room changed quality.

Maya looked at the window. Outside the afternoon was gold and indifferent. She was aware of Jess on the couch, not moving, not filling the silence, just there. The specific quality of Jess being there, which was different from the quality of the apartment being there, which was different from the quality of the last three days.

"I know," Jess said.

"You knew before he did."

"Yes."

"And you didn't say anything."

Jess was quiet for a moment. "I said things. You heard them as things about Derek. You weren't ready to hear them as things about you." She paused. "That's not a criticism. That's just how it went."

Maya put the coffee down. She looked at her hands. The apartment was clean and quiet and the afternoon was outside and she had been in this chair for three days and Jess was on the couch and the thing Jess was saying was landing somewhere she had not given it permission to land.

"The scary part," Maya said slowly, "is that I didn't know I was doing it. The machine thing. I genuinely didn't know."

"I know you didn't."

"Which means I don't know what else I'm doing that I don't know I'm doing."

Jess nodded. Not to comfort her. Just to confirm that yes, that was the thing, that was exactly the thing.

"You were," Jess said. "And."

Maya looked at her.

"Both," Jess said. "You were doing the right thing and you were also three days into an apartment you haven't left, and the plant in the kitchen is overwatered because you've been standing there doing something with your hands and not thinking about the plant, and the clean is the kind of clean that happens when you're managing something instead of feeling it." She paused. "I'm not saying the space was wrong. I'm saying there's a version of taking space that's actually taking space and there's a version that's the quiet the apartment gives you when you need to not be inside yourself for a while. And those look identical from the outside and I've been letting you tell me they're the same thing for seven years because I didn't want to be the person who made you feel like your way of being in the world was a problem."

The room was very quiet.

Maya felt something shift, not a thought, not an articulation, something lower than that, something in the body, the specific physical sensation of a thing that has been held in a particular position for a long time and is finally being allowed to move.

"I overwatered the plant?" she said.

Jess smiled, small and real. "Three days of standing at the kitchen counter. Yeah."

Maya laughed. Not the stream laugh. Not the sideways one that arrived without warning. Something smaller and more ragged, the kind of laugh that happens when you've been somewhere difficult and someone hands you a door back into the ordinary and it turns out the door was right there the whole time.

She felt her eyes go hot. She looked at the window. She was not going to cry in front of Jess. She was definitely going to cry in front of Jess.

"I don't know how to fix it," she said. "The Derek thing. I know what's wrong. I can see it. I just don't know how to be different in the ways that matter."

"You already are," Jess said. "You've been different for months. You just keep waiting to have proof before you'll count it."

"That's a Derek thing to say."

"It's a true thing to say." Jess picked up her coffee. "He's not wrong about you, Maya. He's also not only right about you. Both those things are true and you're going to have to go back into that conversation holding both of them."

"I know."

"And you're going to stop letting me give you the clean version of your own feelings. That's the other thing." The slight shift in her voice, the directness that was Jess at her most herself. "I'm good at it. You let me do it because it's faster and it comes out coherent. But coherent isn't always the point. Sometimes the point is the mess."

Maya sat with that. The afternoon was still doing its thing outside.

The plant in the kitchen had too much water. The apartment was clean in the specific way.

"Jess," she said.

"Yeah."

"How long have you been waiting to say that."

A pause. "Which part."

"Any of it."

Jess looked at her across the room, with the dark eyes, with the four years of knowing, and said: "A while. I was waiting for it to matter enough.

For there to be something real enough to lose that you'd actually hear it."

The thing she'd said landed and stayed.

Maya nodded. Once. The Jess nod, she realized — the same nod her mother used, the economy of it, the I-have-said-the-necessary-thing. She had apparently been absorbing Jess for four years without knowing she was doing it.

"Okay," Maya said.

"Okay," Jess said.

They sat in the apartment for another hour, not talking about Derek, not talking about the gap fight, not shaping anything into anything. Jess asked about the plant and Maya got up and moved it to the windowsill where it would get more light and less water and they talked about Jess's thing at work, the situation with the client that had been running since January, and Maya listened and asked the right questions and it was ordinary and warm and the afternoon went gold and then started to go amber.

When Jess left she held on a beat longer than strictly necessary at the door.

Maya stood in the hallway afterward. The apartment was behind her, clean and quiet and full of the things she had chosen. It was a good apartment. It had been a very good place to be alone.

She thought about calling Derek.

She wasn't ready yet. But the not-ready felt different now — not the glass kind of not-ready, not the three-days-alone kind. Just: I need one more night. And then.

She went back inside. She watered the other plants, correctly this time. She made dinner, a real one, not the automatic competence version, but slowly, with attention, the way he made dal. She ate at the table instead of the island. It was a small thing. She wasn't sure why it felt significant.

She sat at the table in the warm apartment and ate the dinner she had made and let the evening be what it was, and she thought: I am not okay and I am going to be okay and those are not the same thing and both of them are true.

She did not try to make it into a sentence that was better than that.

* * *

On the third night she called him.

She did not plan what she was going to say. She had been planning things her whole professional life — not scripts, just the sense of what you were building toward, the conversation you intended to have. She was tired of it. She dialed and the phone rang twice and he picked up.

She could hear it in the single syllable of his hello — not the full version, not the warm thread-following version, but not the glass version either. Something in between. The glass thinning, the world starting to arrive at temperature again. She recognized it because she had learned the sound of it.

Neither of them said anything for a moment.

It was not the uncomfortable silence. The other kind, the kind that opens when two people have both been carrying the same weight in their separate rooms and have arrived, finally, in the same space, and the weight doesn't require immediate verbal processing, it just requires the acknowledgment that you are both there.

He said: "Hi."

She said: "Hi."

And then, after another moment: "I think I do the content thing," she said, "because it's the only place I know how to be honest without the honesty costing me something. Inside the frame, I can say the true thing and the community receives it and there's warmth and there's recognition and the cost is very low. There's no consequence. Nobody leaves. Nobody looks at me differently in the morning."

He didn't say anything. She knew he wouldn't yet. She knew he was listening, actually listening, the way he did, and she continued because the listening was load-bearing.

"Outside the frame, the true thing costs. It lands on a specific person.

It changes things. And I've been." She stopped. Found the more accurate version. "I've been managing that by keeping most of the true things inside the frame. Where they're safe. Where they don't actually touch anyone."

"I know," he said.

"And what I did the other week with the video wasn't a betrayal. I know you said you weren't upset and I believe you. But it was the reflex.

The automatic thing. Something real happened and I processed it into content before I'd even decided to, and when I watched it back I thought: I didn't give myself a chance to hold it. To let it be mine for a while."

"You're getting better at that," he said.

"The three days," she said.

"The three days."

She pulled her knees up on the couch. The grandmother's pot was on the shelf, the diffused city light was coming through the curtains, the apartment was warm and quiet and hers.

"I almost managed you on the phone," she said. "When you said the thing about not knowing where you fit. I heard the tone in my own voice and I knew what I was doing."

"I heard it too," he said. "And I called it wrong. You weren't managing me. You were trying to help and I was in too much of a bad place to receive it and I turned it into the wrong thing."

"You weren't wrong that I was calibrating."

"You weren't wrong that I was keeping score." A pause. "I keep score in my head and then I act surprised when the score comes out and no one else knew the game was being played. My therapist has mentioned this."

"You have a therapist."

"Had. For two years after the divorce. She was very good and very patient and she told me approximately four times that I was running a private accounting system for grievances and I thanked her for the insight and then continued doing it."

Maya laughed. The real one. In her apartment alone at ten-thirty on a Thursday it arrived sideways, the way it always did, and she heard him laugh too, a small dry sound, the laugh of a man who finds himself genuinely funny and is slightly annoyed by it.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay," he said.

"The thing you said, about not knowing where you fit. I want to come back to that. Not tonight. But I want to come back to it properly."

"I know. I want to come back to it properly too."

"It's a real thing. It's not a solvable thing, I think, but it's real."

"Most of the real things aren't solvable," he said. "That's sort of the definition."

She sat for a moment. Outside It had no interest in her or her arguments or the three days she had spent sitting with something unprocessed. It just continued.

"Can I tell you something that's not related to any of this?" she said.

"Yes."

"I went for a walk this morning and there was a dog on the street, one of those enormous fluffy ones, like an ambulatory cloud, and it looked at me with complete indifference. It didn't recognize me. It was just doing dog things and I was just a person on the sidewalk and I stood there for a minute watching it and it was really good."

He was quiet for a moment. "That's a genuinely good thing," he said.

"I thought so."

"Did you document it?"

"I thought about it," she said. "Then I just watched the dog."

"Yeah," he said. And something in the yeah had warmth in it, the specific warmth of approval from someone who means it and isn't performing it. "Yeah, that's good."

They talked for another hour. Not about any of the things that needed to be talked about. Those were still there, in the queue, real and unresolved.

But other things, the everyday texture of two people who have reestablished that they like each other's company and are using the conversation to confirm that the ground is still solid. She told him about the walk in more detail. He told her about a student who had written something about Gatsby that had made him put the paper down and stare at the wall for a minute because it was so unexpectedly right. She said she wanted to read it. He said he would ask the student.

At midnight she said she needed to sleep.

"I know," he said. "Me too."

"I'll post something tomorrow," she said. Not to him, exactly. More to herself, resolving it in his hearing.

"When it feels right," he said.

"It might not feel right. It might just be time."

"That counts too."

"Goodnight, Derek."

"Goodnight."

She put the phone down. The apartment was quiet. She sat for another minute just being in it, the quiet, the warm, the room that asked nothing, and then she went to bed, and she slept well, and in the morning she posted something small and true and lit it correctly and gave it a thumbnail and sent it into the world, and the world received it the way it always did, with the warmth that was real and the projection that was also real, and she sat with both of those things simultaneously and found that she could.

She was getting better at the sitting.


## Chapter Twenty-Six: The Mango

Four days of short texts. She had been counting, not because she was counting but because the rhythm of it was impossible not to track: the response times lengthening, the answers arriving complete and grammatically correct and without the lateral reach that was one of his signatures, the way he normally followed a thread wherever it went, which was a quality she had come to love and which was now absent, leaving his texts with the quality of a room with the furniture still in it but the lamp switched off.

He had told her the glass was up. He had told her he would be fine.

She believed both things and she also had her own relationship with what fine looked like when you were the one saying it, and the texts on day three had the quality of fine in the way she recognized it: careful, correct, nothing actually wrong, nothing actually present.

She drove to Silver Lake on day four without telling him she was coming. She considered telling him and decided against it because she had a sense, not a certainty, that the anticipation of being visited would require a version of preparation that she did not want to cost him. She wanted to arrive before he could prepare. Before he could arrange himself into the version of himself that was okay.

She brought food from the Taiwanese place. She ordered before she got in the car, picked it up on the way, and arrived at his building with the bag in one hand and her phone in the other, texting: I'm outside. You don't have to come down. But I brought food if you want it.

A long pause. She stood on the sidewalk. The late afternoon was doing its Los Angeles thing, gold and indifferent, and she waited.

Then: buzzing up.

He opened the door in a gray t-shirt and sweatpants she had not seen before, the domestic clothes, the ones that existed in the version of his life that had no audience. He looked like himself and he looked like the other version simultaneously, the way she had learned to read him: the body was right, the eyes were present in the technical sense, but the temperature was off. The slight lag. The world arriving at him through something.

She did not say: are you okay. She had learned not to ask that. She had learned it from him, from watching the way he received questions that demanded a performance of okayness in response, the way the demand itself cost something.

She said: "I brought the scallion pancakes."

He stepped back. She came in.

The apartment had the low-level entropy of a place where the person living in it had been doing the minimum. She took it in without making it visible that she was taking it in, the way you observe things you don't want the other person to feel observed doing. The dishes were clean, he always did the dishes, the small automatic order that persisted through everything, but there were papers on the desk in a stack that had the look of things waiting, the folder open to the same page it had been open to for days. The steelbooks on the shelf were slightly wrong. Not dramatically wrong. Just a few spines out of sequence, the alphabetical order she had come to know disrupted by a few inches, which was the kind of thing you would not notice if you did not know the shelf, and she knew the shelf.

She knew the shelf.

She put the food on the kitchen table. She got two plates from the cabinet to the left of the sink, the one she had known for months, and divided everything out without ceremony and she sat down and she said: "Sit."

He sat.

She did not talk much. This was deliberate. She had thought about it on the drive over, had thought about what the wrong version of this visit looked like, and the wrong version was performing warmth at someone who could not currently receive warmth, flooding him with energy he did not have, requiring him to engage with her engagement. The right version was quieter than that. The right version was just: here is food. Here is a person.

Neither of us needs to be anything right now.

They ate. She ate with the focused attention she always gave to food she liked, the pleasure of the scallion pancakes which she was right about and had always been right about. He ate slowly, with the deliberateness of someone who is eating because the body has asserted its need and the minimum response is sufficient. But he ate. She watched, peripherally, the way the first few bites produced nothing, and then something. Not much, not a return. Just the faintest signal of a person registering that something tastes like something.

Food. It tastes like food.

She knew that sound, the particular quiet sound of someone coming back from a distance by increments.

"Thank you for coming," he said. The first full sentence he had offered since she arrived.

"I wanted to," she said.

They watched a film after. She had looked at his shelves while he was in the bathroom and she had chosen something she had not seen, something she would genuinely want to watch, because the alternative was choosing something to manage the atmosphere and she was done managing atmospheres. She put on a Criterion she'd seen him mention, an older Japanese film, quiet and precise, the kind of thing that asked very little of you in terms of tracking and gave back an enormous amount in terms of feeling. She had not told him she was choosing it because she knew his preference from the podcast. She had just chosen it.

He noticed. She could tell by the way he looked at the case when she held it up, the small shift in his eyes that was recognition and something under it, something that was grateful without being performed.

They sat on the couch. The film was on. The room was warm.

Halfway through the film his breathing changed. The specific shift, the slowing, the way the body releases when it has been held for a long time and is finally somewhere safe enough to put itself down. She looked at him from the corner of her eye. He had fallen asleep sitting up, slightly listing toward the armrest, his face soft in the way that faces are soft only in sleep, without the vigilance that even the gentlest waking faces maintain.

She watched the rest of the film alone. She did not wake him. She pulled the throw blanket from the back of the couch, the gray one she had noticed weeks ago, good quality, chosen with the same care he gave all the things in the apartment, and put it over him the way you do with someone you do not want to disturb, carefully without weight, and she watched the second half of the film in the warm room with the sleeping man beside her and the city outside.

She was not watching the film. She was aware of the film. Mostly she was watching him in the peripheral way you watch someone sleeping when you do not want to be caught watching, the slight tilt of attention, the monitoring that was not monitoring so much as a kind of staying, a quiet confirmation every few minutes that he was still there and still asleep and still the thing she was sitting beside.

She had been in this apartment many times now. She knew the shelf and the steelbooks and the ring dish and the hook by the door. She knew the drawer where he kept the takeout menus and the mug he always used and the tea he kept for her on the second shelf. She knew, from seven visits and three overnights and one very bad week, the specific quality of the apartment when he was fully present in it and the specific quality when he was not. She had learned to read the room the way you learn to read a face you have studied.

The film ended. She sat in the quiet.

She looked at his face in sleep, which was the same face and also different, the way all faces are different in sleep: the vigilance gone, the arrangement gone, just the person underneath the arrangement. She had a habit, she had noticed herself developing it over the last two months, of watching for the person underneath the arrangement. She was not sure what she was looking for. She thought she might be looking for proof that there was a self there that was not the podcast voice and not the teacher voice and not the careful precision of him in a hard conversation. She thought she might be looking for the version he saved for no one.

She was looking at it.

She thought: there you are.

She sat with that for a long time. Not the cleaned-up version of the thought, not the version she would have put into words for Jess or for the camera, but the raw one, the one without a good sentence attached to it.

The one that was just: here, in this room, the specific person, the actual person, asleep in his apartment with a blanket she put on him, and she was still here, and she wanted to still be here, and she did not fully know what to do with the wanting.

It was unfamiliar. Not love; she had felt love before, or something she had called love, though she was less sure now of what she had meant by the word in those other cases. This was specific. This was this person, this room, this Tuesday in April, him listing slightly against the armrest with his face soft. It was very specific and it was also, in its specificity, the largest feeling she had been near in a long time.

She did not try to make it into something. She just sat there and felt it, the actual size of it, without the frame.

She ordered an Uber. She got her jacket from the hook by the door, which had become her hook over months, unremarked upon, just a fact of the space, and looked back at him from the doorway. He was still asleep.

The blanket was around him. The room was warm and full of the things he had chosen and the film was over and the television had gone dark.

She closed the door quietly.

In the Uber she texted: I let myself out. You were asleep. The food is in the fridge if you're hungry when you wake up.

She looked at the city going past the windows. The Los Angeles night, lit and indifferent and full of its private narratives. She thought about him waking up in the morning, oriented wrong by the angle of the couch, the blanket around him. She thought about him going to the kitchen and finding the leftover food she had put in containers with the systematic tidiness that was one of her own private qualities, a thing she did automatically for herself and had now apparently started doing for other people.

For him, specifically. For this specific person.

She thought about the refrigerator. She thought about the mango on the second shelf, the same one that had been there for weeks, the one he had mentioned in passing once as a mango of ambiguous ripeness that he kept meaning to do something about. Past ambiguous now. Past gone, really. Just waiting to be acknowledged.

She had thrown it away before she left. Quietly, without saying anything, the way you clear out what has gone past saving without requiring the other person to witness their own neglect and feel ashamed of it. She had just taken it from the shelf and put it in the bag she was throwing away anyway and tied the bag and that was that.

The mango was gone.

He would notice. He would open the refrigerator in the morning and the mango would not be there and he would know. He would know that she had been in the kitchen and seen it and dealt with it and not said a word.

She did not know if she could explain to anyone, not even Jess, why that felt like the most important thing she had done in months. The discarding of a piece of fruit. The small wordless act of tending to someone's world without requiring them to ask for it or thank you for it or even acknowledge it directly. Just: it needed doing and she did it.

The Uber pulled up to her building. She got out. She went upstairs.

She texted: Home safe.

A long pause. Then: I see you threw away the mango.

She laughed, the real one, alone on the couch with her jacket still on.

She sat down properly and typed back: It was time.

He responded: Yeah. She wrote: Go back to sleep. He wrote: Going.

She put the phone down. She sat in the quiet for a while. She thought about the mango, which was a small and slightly absurd thing to think about, and about what it had meant to see it on the shelf and know without deciding that she was going to take it with her. The not-deciding was interesting. The naturalness of it. She had walked into his kitchen and read the situation and acted, not from strategy, not from the calculation of the right gesture, just from the ordinary impulse to tend to a space you were in and a person you were for.

She was not sure when she had become a person who did that for someone else. She thought it might have happened gradually, without announcement, the way the things that matter most tend to happen.

She took off her jacket. She made tea. She sat at the kitchen island in the warm apartment that was exactly as she had arranged it, and she thought about him asleep in Silver Lake with the blanket around him and the steelbooks back in order and the refrigerator cleaned out, and she thought: I did that. Not for the camera. Not for the story. Not for anyone watching.

Just because he needed it and she was there.

She sat with that for a long time. The tea went cold. She let it.

Outside, the city continued. In Silver Lake, a man on a couch with a gray blanket around him was asleep in a warm room full of chosen things.

Somewhere on a shelf near the window, in the correct order now. She had fixed the steelbooks while he was in the bathroom, quietly, without ceremony, putting each one back in its place, the Criterion of A Love Supreme next to the Ennio Morricone collection and the hand-numbered Ghost in the Shell variant and all the other things he had kept with enormous care when the people he kept them for had gone.

He was still there. Somewhere in the room, still there.

She was getting very good at finding him.


## Chapter Twenty-Seven: There You Are

He came back on a Wednesday.

Not all the way, not instantly. The return was never a switch but a gradient, the glass thinning over days the way ice thins over a warming week, imperceptibly and then all at once. But Wednesday was the morning when he woke up and the ceiling was the ceiling in the full sense, not just a fact but a thing he was actually looking at, actually registering its specific quality of Wednesday-morning light, the way it came through the blinds in lines that moved as clouds moved outside, and he lay there watching the lines move and he was there for the watching. Present. The temperature of the room reaching him without translation.

He lay still for a while, not because he was unwilling to move but because the stillness was good, the active stillness of a person who has returned from somewhere and is taking a moment to confirm the return. He did a quiet inventory. The glass: thin. The world: its own temperature. His body: hungry, actually hungry, the signal arriving clean and direct.

He made eggs. He made them properly, the way he made them when he was fully himself, unhurried, with butter and a low flame and the particular patience of someone who believes the time spent on a thing is part of the thing. He ate them at the kitchen table with the morning light and the sound of the street coming up from outside and the Coltrane on the speaker, not loud, just present, the way music is present when you actually want it rather than needing it to fill something.

He texted Maya: I'm back. Really back.

She responded in two minutes: I know. You sound like yourself.

He looked at this for a moment. She had sent it before he'd said anything beyond I'm back. She had heard it in four words. He put the phone down and finished his eggs and sat with that, the specific and still-strange experience of being known accurately, and he did not try to analyze it, he just let it be what it was.

* * *

He recorded the episode that afternoon.

He had been thinking about it for weeks, the way you think about something that keeps arriving in the margins of your thoughts, showing up when you're doing other things, persistent in the way of ideas that are trying to get your attention. Ferris Bueller's Day Off. His favorite film. The one he had never done an episode on because it was too close, too much the thing itself rather than something he could hold at analytical distance and examine.

He set up the microphone. He put on the film while he worked, not to watch but to have it in the room, to have the sound of it while he thought.

The famous opening. Cameron's voice, the sick voice, the voice of someone who has organized his entire interior life around the conviction that the world is not safe for the full version of him. He let it run while he made notes he didn't look at, just wrote things down as they came, which was how his best episodes started, not with an argument but with accumulation, the gathering of the materials before you know what you're building.

He recorded for an hour and fourteen minutes. He talked about Cameron the way Maya had talked about Cameron months ago on the balcony, the person for whom the day actually costs something, the person who stands in front of his father's car, the person who has been standing at the window of his own life for so long that the window has started to feel like the thing itself. He talked about what it meant to break the thing your fear has organized itself around. He talked about Ferris, about the way Ferris loved Cameron without quite seeing him, loved the potential of him rather than the specific weight of him, and whether that was a failing or just the limit of what most love can do.

And then, without planning it, he talked about something else.

He talked about the glass. Not by that name, the glass was his private language, the interior shorthand he had never put into the episodes before, but he described it, the way you describe a thing when you are finally ready to describe it: from the inside, with precision, without drama. He talked about sitting on the couch in the late afternoon with the television off and knowing the kitchen was twelve feet away and being unable to make himself go. He talked about the lag, the passenger, the mild and distant interest of a man watching himself not move. He talked about being a person who had spent a long time building rooms he could be fully himself inside of and who was learning, slowly, that the rooms were not the same as the life.

He talked for twenty minutes about this. He did not cry. He was not confessional, and the distinction mattered to him. He was just accurate. He said the true things in the register of true things, which is quieter than people expect.

At the end he said: "Someone asked me once what it was like from the inside. And I said it was like knowing the kitchen is twelve feet away and not being able to make yourself go. And she said thank you for telling me that. Not: I'm sorry. Not: that sounds terrible. Just: thank you. And I've been thinking about that response ever since. Because it was the right response.

It was the response of someone who understood that the truth of a thing is worth having even when the thing is hard. Who wanted the actual information rather than a more manageable version of it." A pause. "I think that might be the whole thing. I think that might be what I've been trying to say for two and a half years of doing this. The whole thing is just: be the person who says thank you. Be the person who wants the actual information. Even when it costs something. Especially when it costs something."

He stopped recording.

He listened back to the first ten minutes. He sat with the headphones on in the lamp light of his apartment and he listened to his own voice saying the true things and he thought: this is good. Not in the self-congratulatory sense but in the quiet sense of recognizing something that has been made correctly, that has the right shape, that says the thing it meant to say.

He saved the file. He labeled it: ferris_bueller_v2.

He made tea. He sat with it at the kitchen table and he was aware of having made the thing without needing to broadcast it yet, and the awareness was not loss. He had expected it to feel like loss, the thing made in private, for no one, the Shuggie Otis principle, but it didn't feel like loss.

It felt like the thing was his. For right now, just his, and that was enough.

He thought about the other file sitting in the same folder, the one labeled DELETE, still undeleted. Two versions. He thought it was probably accurate that there had needed to be two.

He sat with it for a long time.

* * *

She listened. He watched the street. Outside a dog walker went past with three dogs of different sizes doing three different things simultaneously, which was a negotiation he admired. A woman in a yellow coat was on her phone. A delivery truck was double-parked with the specific confidence of a vehicle that knew it was going to be there exactly as long as it needed to be and had stopped apologizing for it.

The episode played. His voice in the room. The true things, said precisely, without drama. Cameron. The window. The car going through the glass. The kitchen twelve feet away. And then, at the end, the thing he had been working toward: Be the person who wants the actual information.

Even when it's hard. Even when the person is also hard.

You don't have to understand everything. You just have to keep wanting to know.

The episode ended. The room was quiet. The Sunday morning was outside.

Maya was quiet for a long moment. Then she said: "Derek."

"Yeah."

"This is the best thing you've made."

He looked at her. She was sitting in his bed in the UCLA sweatshirt holding the mug he had made her, her hair undone, the morning on her face, the real face, the unlit face, the face that no camera had ever quite caught because no camera had been there for it.

"Yeah," he said. "I think so too."

She got up. She padded past him toward the bathroom and she put her hand on his shoulder briefly as she went, just a touch, just the fact of her hand, and then she was gone and the chair was warm from the morning light and the mug was in his hands and outside the dog walker had turned the corner and the woman in the yellow coat had finished her call and the delivery truck was doing what it needed to do.

He picked up his phone. The episode had one hundred and four listeners.

He put the phone down. He sat in the chair with the morning and the mug and waited for her to come back, which she would, which she did, returning with her hair differently arranged and stealing his mug because hers had gone cold and he let her take it because that was one of the arrangements they had settled into, the small unremarked economies of two people sharing a space and a life and the ongoing project of knowing each other all the way down.

He looked at the shelf while he waited. The steelbooks in their rows.

The Criterion editions. The art prints in their frames: the Mondo Chinatown, the Mulholland Drive, the Ghost in the Shell variant. The Morricone beside the Coltrane. The Shuggie Otis beside the Morricone, where they had put it.

Everything in its place. The shelf had the quality it got when it was simply itself, not a record of anything, not a ledger of what had been kept and what had been lost. Just his things, in his apartment, on a Sunday morning.

Outside: the city.

Inside: them.

The glass was thin today. Almost nothing. Just the permanent fact of it, the scar that would always be the scar, the window he would always know was there. But on the other side of it, within reach, not requiring translation — 

Her.
