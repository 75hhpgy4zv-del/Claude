# 3

His mother's house had a smell that arrived before the door opened, and it worked on him
every single time, at thirty-five, from the driveway.

That was the trouble with the whole arrangement. Nobody in the family had ever been
cruel to him. If they had been cruel he could have built something out of it, a wall or
a policy. Instead there was the smell of curry leaves hitting hot oil, and his mother
saying *orey* in the exact register she had used when he was nine, and his father
getting up out of the chair to turn the television down rather than off, and the door
opening straight into the part of him that had no door.

"Annam tinnava?"

"Amma, it's eleven in the morning."

"That's not an answer."

"It's a very good answer. It's a dodge, which is a category of answer."

She hit him on the arm with a dish towel and he went to wash his hands, and for about
forty minutes it was fine. It was better than fine. His mother had questions about the
school year, real ones, and listened to the answers. His father asked him about the
brakes on the car and then talked for a while about a bridge retrofit in Bakersfield he
had read about, which was his father's way of talking about his other son, and Derek let
him, because it cost nothing.

Then at 11:50 Anand came in with a bag of mangoes and a story about the traffic.

---

His brother was ten years older, taller, and had the particular ease of a man who has
never once wondered whether he was welcome in a room. He hugged their mother. He clapped
Derek on the shoulder with the flat of his hand.

"There he is. The professor."

"Teacher."

"Same thing."

"It really isn't, but sure."

They ate. The food was excellent and his mother said three times that it hadn't come out
right. Anand talked about the firm, a merger, a man named Rick who was an idiot, a
project in Riverside that was six weeks behind because of somebody else. The table
arranged itself around him without anybody deciding to, the way water finds a slope.

Derek was fine. He was, genuinely, fine. He asked the follow-up questions, which he was
good at, and he made his father laugh twice, which was a metric he had tracked privately
since roughly 1998.

Then Anand put his fork down and said, "So what's the plan, though."

"With?"

"The internet thing." He said it kindly. That was the thing about Anand that nobody who
had not sat at this table could be made to understand: he said everything kindly. "The
movie stuff. Because Meera's cousin's kid, the one in Fremont, he does the same thing
with the games — twenty-two years old, buys his mother a car. And I'm sitting there
thinking, my brother's been doing this since, what, 2015?"

"Fourteen."

"Since 2014. Eleven years." He picked the fork back up. "So I'm asking, and I'm asking
as somebody who's on your side. What's the plan? Because if it's a hobby, great, God
bless, everybody needs a hobby. But eleven years is not a hobby, eleven years is an
investment, and I want to know what the return looks like."

"There isn't one."

"There's always one."

"There's genuinely not, in this case, and that's kind of the—"

"See, that's what I mean, that's the thing." Anand turned to their mother, warmly,
including her. "He does this. You ask him a business question and he gives you a
philosophy answer."

And their mother laughed. Not meanly. She laughed the way you laugh when your two sons
are being your two sons at your table, and she said, "Nijame," and put more rice on
Derek's plate.

His father said nothing at all, which was also an answer, and had been for twenty-five
years.

---

Here is what Derek did, which he would replay in the car.

He was funny about it. He was *good*. He said that his return on investment was four
hundred and eleven dollars a month and a comprehensive knowledge of Paul Verhoeven, and
Anand laughed. He said that the kid in Fremont was playing games, which is a growth
category, and he was talking about a Dutch satirist for four hours at midnight, which is
a shrinking category, and that he had chosen the shrinking category with his eyes open.
He said all of it lightly. Nobody's feelings were hurt. His mother relaxed. His father
laughed once, which counted.

He did not say the other thing.

The other thing had been sitting behind his teeth since the word *plan*, and it was this:
that he had a spreadsheet on his laptop called `THEATRE_v9.xlsx` which had six tabs and
a five-year model in it, that he had looked at nineteen commercial listings in three
counties, that he knew what a 4K laser projector cost installed and what the rights fee
looked like for a 35mm print of a Malayalam film from 1988 and how many seats you needed
to fill on a Tuesday to survive a Wednesday. That he had a *plan*. That the plan was the
most serious thing in his life and he had never said a word about it to a single member
of his family, because he had run the simulation of saying it at this table many times
and it always ended the same way, with Anand being kind about it.

So he ate the mangoes and helped with the dishes and stood in the doorway for the long
goodbye, and his mother held his face in both hands and said *jagratha, nanna*, and he
loved her so much it was difficult to stand there.

---

He got in the car. He put his seatbelt on. He did not start the engine.

The driveway was hot and the steering wheel was hot and across the street a man was
running a leaf blower for no reason anyone could determine.

*Eleven years is not a hobby, eleven years is an investment.*

The problem — and he could see the problem clearly, which was the worst part, because
seeing it had never once helped — was that Anand was not wrong. That was the design flaw
in the entire family. They were never wrong on the facts. Eleven years *was* a long time.
Four hundred dollars a month *was* not a living. A man of thirty-five with a spreadsheet
he had never shown anyone probably *should* be asked what the plan was.

They were only ever wrong about one thing, and it was so small and so total that he had
never found language for it that didn't sound like a complaint: they were asking on their
own behalf. Every question was a question about how his life was going to feel to *them*.
What's the plan meant *tell me a story about you I can repeat comfortably*. And the
maddening thing, the thing that made it impossible to be angry in any way that would hold
up, was that they meant it warmly. They wanted good things for him. They had simply never
once, in thirty-five years, checked what the good things were.

He had spent his twenties trying to explain this and had learned that explaining it made
him the difficult one.

He thought, briefly, about Sana, who had understood it inside of a month, and who had
been the first person he'd ever shown the spreadsheet to, at version 3, in 2016, in a
kitchen in Arcadia. And then he stopped thinking about Sana, with the practiced economy
of long habit, the way you step over a hole in a floor you have decided not to fix.

Then he sat there for eleven more minutes.

He was not upset. He wanted to be clear with himself about that, and he was, and it was
true. He had handled it well. He had been generous and quick and nobody had been hurt,
and he would go home and grade forty-one essays about *The Great Gatsby*'s first three
chapters and he would eat something standing up.

It was just that the door was open. It was always open. Their warmth had come through it
along with everything else, all at once, in a lump — the dish towel and the mangoes and
*jagratha, nanna* and *God bless, everybody needs a hobby* — and there was no sorting
mechanism, there had never been a sorting mechanism, and it would take until roughly
Tuesday for the lump to dissolve.

The leaf blower stopped. In the sudden quiet Derek started the car.
