# Chapter Eight
## Chemistry

He asked Ferreira on a Tuesday in August, in her office, with the door shut, having rehearsed it in a car for eleven minutes and then abandoned every version of it.

"I want to take the spring."

"Okay."

"That's — " Derek stopped. "That's it? I had a whole thing."

"You've had a whole thing since June, Derek, you've been carrying it around the building." Ferreira took her glasses off. "You've got a film being made of your book. Everybody here knows. Marcus Delgado told my *husband* at a gas station."

"It's ten weeks of shooting and then—"

"It's a semester. Take the semester." She put the glasses back on. "Unpaid, obviously, because the district is the district, and you'll want to talk to Yvonne about the insurance because that's the part that actually bites."

---

He should have left there and he did not.

"Can I do something else."

"Go on."

"I'm not doing this to get out of the building," he said. "I know that's what a leave of absence usually is. I've watched four people do it." He had his hands on his knees. "I'd like to still be on the curriculum committee. Remotely. I've got the sophomore sequence half rebuilt and if I hand it to somebody in January it dies, and I'd rather do it at midnight from a hotel than watch it die."

Ferreira looked at him for a moment.

"You want to do unpaid administrative work," she said, "on a leave of absence. From a film set."

"Yes."

"Derek, that is the single most Derek Kolluri sentence I have heard in six years." She was already writing. "Yes. Obviously yes. I'll put you on as a consultant so the committee has to actually listen to you, and I want the sequence by March, and I want you to understand that I'm agreeing to this because it costs me nothing and gets me a rebuilt sophomore year for free."

"That's fair."

"It's *extremely* good business and I'd like it acknowledged," said Ferreira. "Go and be a film person. Come back in August. And take the sabbatical properly, because if I hear you've been grading essays from a soundstage I'll have Wendy come over there."

---

They read five men against her in one afternoon and by the third one it had stopped being a casting session and become something Derek was never able to describe to Lucky at all.

The setup was ordinary. A room on the fourth floor in Century City, bad carpet, a camera on a tripod, four people behind a table. Ha-eun in a chair in the middle of the floor with the sides on her knee, doing the balcony scene five times against five different men, because that is what a chemistry read is: you sit your Maya down and you find out who she catches fire with.

Derek had been invited as a courtesy. He was in the corner with a folding chair and a bottle of water and instructions from Nadia to keep his face still.

---

**Rahul Varma**, first.

He was thirty-six and about four inches taller than Derek and he had the sort of face they put in suits, and he did the scene twice, cleanly, with two different readings.

"Lovely," Nadia said. "Give us a minute."

He was still getting his bag off the back of the chair when Ha-eun stood up and crossed the room to the corner.

"Well?"

"He's very good."

"He's *very* good," she agreed, and took the water bottle out of Derek's hand without asking and drank about a third of it. "So what's the face."

"There's no face."

"Derek, you've got the — " She did an impression of it, which involved lowering her chin about an inch and going flat around the mouth, and it was so exact that he laughed out loud in a room where four people were writing things down.

"He put the stress on *required.*"

"So did *you*—"

"I know I did, that's how I know it's wrong—"

"You did it three weeks ago and I've been waiting to bring it up," Ha-eun said, and handed him back an empty bottle, and went back to her chair.

Rahul Varma left with his bag on one shoulder and did not say anything to anybody.

---

**Josh Feng**, second.

He made a real choice — he played the balcony as though the man already knew it was going badly — and it was intelligent and it was clean and it did not do anything at all, which happens, and which nobody in the room held against him.

"Thank you, Josh. That was lovely."

He said thank you twice and went to the door.

Ha-eun was already at the corner.

"He's better than the first one."

"He's not."

"He *is*, you're being weird because the first one's handsome."

"That is not — okay, partly," said Derek, and she hit him on the arm with the back of her hand and went back to her chair.

Josh Feng stood in an open doorway with his bag on his shoulder for slightly too long, and Yolanda had to say his name twice.

---

**Aaron Sedeño**, third.

His read was the most technically accomplished of the afternoon and Nadia wrote three lines about it. He found something in the silence before *the view is the lie the city tells about itself* that nobody else got near. It sat there on the carpet doing nothing, the way an excellent thing does when it is by itself.

"Thank you, Aaron."

He picked up his water and his phone and got as far as the door, and behind him Ha-eun said, "Derek, what's the thing about the thing," and Derek said, "You'll have to be more specific," and she said, "The *thing*, the — with the guy, the — " and made a shape with both hands, and Derek said, "Oh, the guy at the bar," and she said, "*The guy at the bar*, yes," and both of them laughed at something that had not been said out loud.

Aaron Sedeño turned around in the doorway.

"Sorry — is that the writer?"

"That's the writer," said Yolanda.

"Right," he said, and looked at the corner for another second, and went.

---

**Dev Chaudhary**, fourth, by which point nobody was pretending.

He did the scene and it was fine.

Then he sat there with the pages on his knee and looked at Ha-eun, and looked at the corner, and said, "Can I ask something that's going to sound insane?"

"Go on," said Nadia, with the weariness of a woman who has been dreading a specific afternoon since nine.

"I've been in that corridor for two hours and the door isn't thick," said Dev, "and I've been listening to what I'm fairly sure is the best chemistry read of the afternoon, and every time the door opens it turns out that was the *break*, so I want to ask whether anybody's—"

"Do the scene again," said Nadia.

"I've done the scene."

"Do it again," said Nadia.

He did it again. It was fine.

---

Between the fourth and the fifth, Ha-eun came over and sat on the floor next to the folding chair, cross-legged, because there was no other chair, and put her back against the wall and her head at about the level of his knee.

"I'm going to say something and you're not allowed to be nice about it."

"Okay."

"I'm frightened."

He looked down at the top of her head.

"Of the read?"

"Of the *film.*" She had the sides rolled into a tube and was turning them. "I've been in a room for six hours and I've been extremely good and everybody's told me I've been extremely good, and I've done five men and I've been the best thing in the room five times, and none of it counts, because none of it is *acting.* I'm reading a scene I've had by heart since February to a man on a folding chair."

"That's not what you were doing."

"That is exactly what I was—"

"You were doing it *at* them," Derek said. "All five times. I watched. You gave the first one the version where she's amused and the second one the version where she's tired and you gave the fourth one a completely different woman, which I'd like to talk about later, because I don't think that woman's in the book."

Ha-eun stopped turning the tube.

"You could see that from the corner."

"It's a good corner."

"Nobody at the table saw that."

"The table's got a job. I'm a man on a chair with a bottle of water." He shrugged. "You're going to be fine. You're going to be better than fine and it's going to annoy a great many people."

She put her head back against the wall and looked at the ceiling for a second.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay?"

"Okay, I'll stop." She got up off the floor in one motion and took the water bottle again on the way past, which was empty, which she discovered four steps later and turned around about, and he said "there's nothing in it," and she said "I know that *now,*" and put it on the table in front of Nadia with some ceremony.

Nadia wrote something down that was not about an actor.

---

**Rahul Varma** came back at the end, fifth, because Nadia asked him to.

He did the scene a third time and it was the best of the day by a distance.

Then he put the pages on his knee and said, "Can I say the thing?"

"No," said Nadia.

"You know what I'm going to say—"

"I know exactly what you're going to say and I would like it not said in a room with a camera in it."

"It's not a *gossip* thing," Rahul said, wounded, entirely delighted. "It's a *craft* thing. I've been out there with four other guys for six hours and we have all been trying to reverse-engineer something we can hear through a *door*, and I would like it noted that this is the hardest job I have ever been offered and I am going to take it."

"You're taking it."

"I'm taking it, I'd be insane not to." He stood up and put his bag over his shoulder and shook Derek's hand for slightly too long, warmly, with the expression of a man who has decided to enjoy the next four months. "Whatever it is you two are doing. Don't get better at it before January. I need to be able to *see* it."

He left.

---

The room emptied in the usual stages. Yolanda went to make a call. The camera operator turned the tripod around and coiled cable. Somebody took the tape off the floor.

Nadia squared four pages of notes and did not move for a second.

"That was an extremely long day," she said, to nobody.

"He's the right one," Derek said.

"He was the right one at nine-forty. I've spent six hours making sure, which is my job, and I would like to say — " she put the notes in her bag " — that I have cast four hundred films and I have never once had to write the phrase *do not seat the author in the corner* in a production note, and I am going to write it tonight, and I am not going to explain it to anybody."

She went out.

Which left two people and a man coiling cable.

Ha-eun picked the empty water bottle back up off the table, looked at it, and put it in Derek's hand.

"Buy me a new one."

"There's a machine on three."

"I'm not going to a *machine*, Derek, I've been working since six," she said, and walked out ahead of him, and he followed her, and the man coiling cable did not look up.
