# Chapter Twenty
## It's Your Lucky Day

Ms. Ferreira asked him to come at lunch, not after school, which was how he knew it was not disciplinary.

She had been principal for six years and Derek had never once had to think about her, which was, he had decided long ago, the single highest compliment available to a school administrator. She had a bowl of clementines on the desk that were for anybody. She pushed it toward him without saying anything about it.

"I've had four emails," she said.

"Okay."

"I want to say at the top: none of them accuse you of anything. I've read all four twice and there is not one allegation in any of them. Do you want to read them?"

"No."

"Good answer." She turned her monitor a few degrees away, which he appreciated. "They're worried. That's the word all four of them use, and I believe it, because two of them are from parents I've dealt with for years and neither of them is a lunatic. The concern is disruption. And Derek, I have to tell you honestly, the concern is *correct.*"

He looked at the clementines.

"Say the specific thing," he said.

"There's video of you teaching."

"There's—"

"It's forty seconds. It's from period three on Monday, it's shot from a desk, and it's you explaining something about a poem." She said it evenly. "It's not unkind. Whoever posted it captioned it *he's actually a really good teacher,* and it has a hundred and sixty thousand views, and I have spent the last four days getting it taken down twice and it goes straight back up because thirty other people have saved it."

Derek sat in a chair in an office he had been in maybe nine times in nine years.

"Which class," he said.

"Third period."

"Who?"

"I'm not going to tell you that," Ms. Ferreira said, "and I'd like you to hear that as me protecting them and not as me withholding. They're sixteen. They did something thoughtless with a phone in a room where a thing was happening. That's — Derek, that is the entire developmental stage. If I disciplined every sixteen-year-old for that I would have no students."

"I'm not asking so I can—"

"I know why you're asking. You want to know so you can be normal with them and not accidentally be *different* with them, because you'd rather carry it than have them carry it." She looked at him. "Which is exactly why I'm not telling you. You'd be kinder to them than you are to everyone else and they'd notice, and then they'd know that you knew."

---

The offer, when it came, was gentle and it took him about four seconds to understand the shape of it.

There was a curriculum coordinator position. It was a real job, it had existed for years, it was currently half-filled by a man who was retiring in the spring, and it involved standards alignment and department scheduling and no classroom.

"For a semester," Ms. Ferreira said. "Possibly two. It's not a demotion, it's a lateral, the pay is identical, and I want to be extremely clear that this is an offer and not a direction, and that if you say no, nothing happens, and I will not raise it again."

"But you're raising it."

"I'm raising it once, because I would be a bad principal if I didn't, and because there's a district conversation happening about social media policy that I'd rather have *about* a hypothetical than about you." She folded her hands. "And because there is a version of the next four months where this gets worse rather than better, and if it does, the person it lands on is not you. It's a room of thirty-one sixteen-year-olds who are trying to get through *1984* while people film the back of your head."

Derek looked at the clementines for a long time.

"Can I say the honest thing," he said.

"I'd prefer it."

"I'm not going to take it, and the reason I'm not going to take it is not principle." He put his hands on his knees. "It's that the classroom is the last room I've got. I built a podcast and it's not mine anymore. I built a stream and it took nine minutes on Tuesday to find out that's gone. I had a Discord with a hundred and forty people in it and it's got four thousand now and the moderators are apologizing to *me*." He heard his own voice do something and let it. "And then there's period three. Which nobody in this has taken yet. And you're asking me to hand it over voluntarily so that it can't be taken, and I understand that that's the smart move, and I'm not going to make it."

Ms. Ferreira was quiet for a moment.

"That's not a good enough reason," she said.

"No."

"I'm going to write down that I offered it and you declined."

"You should."

"And Derek." She stood up, which meant it was over. "If it gets worse, I will not be able to make the same offer. It'll be a different conversation and it will have the district in it and I'll be on your side and it won't matter." She pushed the bowl an inch closer. "Take a clementine. You look terrible."

---

They found Lucky on the Thursday and it took them nine hours from a standing start.

The mechanism was not sophisticated. It did not need to be. Derek had almost no public footprint — the author photo, four hundred episodes of audio, a Twitch account — but Suresh Pillai had a decade of billboards and a verified account and a decade of tagged photographs, and somebody ran an image search on the author photo, and somewhere in 2019 there was a picture from Deepa's birthday where Lucky had tagged fourteen people and one of them was a man with his sleeves pushed up.

By dinner it had a name. By midnight there was a thread with a hundred and forty thousand likes, titled — and it was a good title, that was the thing that made him put the phone down, it was genuinely a good title —

> *ok so derek's best friend is the "IT'S YOUR LUCKY DAY" guy and this is now the best story on the internet*

---

Lucky loved it for about a day and a half.

That was the part Derek had not predicted and should have. He got a call on Friday morning at seven and it was Lucky at full volume, delighted, in a car.

"I'm *trending*."

"You're not trending."

"Derek. Deepa's sister sent me a screenshot. I'm on a *list*. There's a list of the funniest things this week and I'm number four."

"Lucky—"

"I'm number four and above me is a *dog*, which I'm going to be honest with you, I'm taking that personally—"

"Are you okay?"

"Am I — " A pause of genuine confusion. "Why wouldn't I be okay? Do you know what happened to me at the gas station this morning? Four people. *Four.* Normally it's one guy. One guy a day, that's the number, that's been the number for eleven years." He was laughing. "This is the best week I've had since the second commercial."

---

The Ralphs was on the Monday.

It was the one on Main in Alhambra, which was the one Derek used, and Lucky was there because Deepa had sent him for two things and he had bought nine, which was a documented and unchangeable feature of the man.

There were three of them. Late teens, maybe twenty. Perfectly nice — Derek would watch it about forty times and never once find a moment where they were anything other than nice — and one of them was filming, held low and to the side in the way people do now, and one of them said, "Are you the lucky guy?"

And Lucky, who had answered that question at a gas station, an urgent care, three DMVs, a wedding, and a funeral, said, "I'm the lucky guy."

They did the thing. He said the thing. He said it at full volume in a Ralphs at ten past seven in the evening — *IT'S YOUR LUCKY DAY!* — and they lost their minds, and he laughed, and it was, if you watched only that part, a lovely forty seconds.

Then one of them said, "Wait — you're Derek's friend."

And Lucky said, "That's my best friend."

And the kid said, "Is he okay? Everyone's really worried about him," and he said it *kindly*, that was the thing, there was no edge on it at all, and Derek could see on the video the exact moment Lucky stopped being a man doing a bit at a supermarket and became a man being asked about someone he loved.

"He's the best person I know," Lucky said.

"But is he okay—"

"He's had a hard few years." Lucky's hands had come up. He talks with his hands; he has always talked with his hands. "He's had a *really* hard few years, and he doesn't tell anybody anything, and I'm going to be honest with you guys because you seem nice — I don't actually know what's going on right now. He hasn't told me. I've known him since we were nineteen and I found out about all of this off the internet like everybody else." A laugh, and there was nothing in it. "So, yeah. Just — everybody leave him alone for a bit. He's a good guy. He's the best guy."

Nine seconds.

He was, Derek thought later, absolutely at his best in it. Warm. Unguarded. Entirely himself. He had never in his life said a calculated sentence and he did not start in a Ralphs.

---

The clip did four million by Wednesday.

*he doesn't tell anybody anything* went one way. *I found out off the internet like everybody else* went another, much worse way, into the seam that had already decided he was secretive, and it went there with the unimpeachable authority of a sixteen-year friendship. *He's had a really hard few years* sent forty thousand people back to page 141 with a new sense of purpose.

And a fourth thing happened, which nobody could have designed, which was that the internet fell in love with Suresh Pillai.

Genuinely. Completely. Within a week there were edits. There was a soundboard. There was a Reddit thread of decade-old commercials with a comment section like a wake. Somebody found a 2016 spot where he did the line to a woman who had won four hundred dollars and had started crying, and the caption was *this man has been out here doing this for a decade and we never noticed*, and it had a million views and every single reply was affectionate.

Lucky went from a man that one guy a day recognized at a gas station to a man who could not stand still anywhere in Los Angeles County.

---

He did not complain about it once.

That was what Derek could not get past. He waited for it — he waited for it for about ten days, braced, doing the arithmetic every night, because he knew exactly what he had cost and he had a whole apology drafted.

Lucky called on a Sunday.

"So the Lottery called," he said.

Derek closed his eyes. "Okay."

"No, it's — it's fine, it's fine, it's actually good. They're pleased. Engagement's up whatever percent, the comms lady used the word *organic* four times." A pause, and something underneath it. "They asked me to stop talking about you."

"Right."

"Not — they were nice about it. She was really nice. She said any commentary on a third party in a matter with reputational sensitivity, blah blah, and would I mind referring media inquiries to them." Another pause. "I've had a decade of that job and nobody has ever asked me to not say something before."

"Lucky, I'm—"

"Deepa's phone got the thing," Lucky said.

Derek stopped.

"What?"

"It's nothing. It's — it's not a big deal, I don't even know why I'm — " He heard him move. He heard a door. "Somebody got her number. It's not hard, she's got a business, it's on the *website.* And she's had about forty texts and they're all nice, they're all — it's people asking if you're okay, mostly. And one guy who wanted to know if I could get him a signed thing."

"Deepa's number."

"They're *nice*, Derek."

"Lucky."

"And then on Thursday somebody knocked on the door," Lucky said, "at eight-thirty at night, and it was a guy with a camera, and he was polite, and he asked if I'd do the line for his channel, and Meena answered it because she's nine and she thought it was a delivery."

Derek sat down on his own kitchen floor.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. So here's what's going to happen."

"No."

"You haven't—"

"You're about to do the thing," Lucky said, "and I've been waiting for you to do the thing for two weeks, and I called you today specifically to get out in front of the thing."

"Suresh."

"Oh, we're doing *Suresh* now—"

"I'm going to stop coming to your house," Derek said. "For a while. Not forever. And you're going to stop tagging me in anything, and if anyone asks you, you're going to say we're not close anymore, and you're going to say it in a way that reads, because you're a terrible liar but you're a *fantastic* performer and those are different skills—"

"Derek."

"—and this is not a discussion, because it's not your call. It's my mess and it's landing on a nine-year-old who thought a man with a camera was a *delivery*—"

"**Derek.**"

He stopped.

There was a long silence on the line. He could hear a television somewhere in Lucky's house, and somebody moving through a room, and the specific ambient sound of a family evening in a house that had absorbed twenty years of him.

"Say it," Derek said.

"You wrote a whole guy," Lucky said.

"...What?"

"In your book. You made up a friend, because when you went looking there was nothing on the shelf, because you weren't letting any of it land. You told me that. On the phone. Three weeks ago, on a Sunday, and I have thought about it every single day since and I have not been able to get it out of my *teeth.*"

Derek did not say anything.

"So you'll understand," Lucky said, "why I'm not going to sit here and let you handle me. Because that's what this is. This is you deciding what I can carry without asking me, and doing it in a kind voice, and calling it protecting my kid." A breath. "It's the exact thing you hate. It's the thing you have a *tattoo* about. It's what your ex did to you for five years and you can't see it because you're doing it in the other direction."

"That's not the same—"

"It's not the same, it's the same *shape*," Lucky said. "And I'm not going to do it. Deepa's not going to do it. We had the conversation on Thursday night after Meena went up, we had it for two hours, and we decided, and I'm calling you to tell you what we decided, not to ask."

Derek sat on the floor with his hand over his eyes.

"What did you decide."

"That it's a *hard year*," Lucky said. "That's it. That's the whole thing. It's a hard year and it'll stop, and Meena's fine, and Deepa turned her business phone off after seven which she should have done in 2019 anyway, and we're changing the door camera. And in eleven months this will be a story I tell at dinner and I'll be *unbearable* about it." His voice had come all the way up now. "And what I am not going to do — what neither of us is going to do — is lose sixteen years of you because some people were rude to my wife on a Thursday."

"Lucky—"

"You are not a *cost.*" He said it too loud and it broke slightly in the middle and he pushed straight on through it, badly, the way he did everything. "You're not — Derek, you've been running a spreadsheet on yourself since I met you. I watched you do it with Ananya, I watched you do it with your dad, I watched you do it for two years after the divorce where you'd cancel on me and say you were busy and you were sitting in the dark. And you're doing it again right now, on the phone, at me, out loud, and it's the same *spreadsheet*, and I'm telling you the number's wrong."

Derek sat on his kitchen floor in the dark.

"I don't know how to be somebody who doesn't do that," he said.

"Yeah, well." A wet sound that Lucky covered with a cough. "Neither do I, man. I'm winging it. I've been winging it since 2009."

---

They were quiet for a while, in the way they had been quiet at each other since they were nineteen and both working a room full of televisions in West Covina.

"Can I say the other thing," Lucky said eventually.

"Is it worse."

"It's much worse."

"Say it."

"You've never met her," Lucky said. "That's not — I know you can't. I know there's a whole thing and I don't need to know it, and I'm not asking, and honestly Deepa asks me weekly and I *enjoy* not knowing." A pause. "But you've been doing this since March, whatever it is. And Deepa's known you sixteen years and she's never met her, and you've never sat at our table with her, and Meena doesn't know she exists."

"I know."

"And that's not you protecting anybody," Lucky said. "That's just the way it is, right? That's just — that's the actual situation."

"That's the actual situation."

"Okay." A long breath. "That's the part I hate. Just so you know. It's not the guy at the door. It's that."

Derek looked at the dark ceiling of his kitchen.

"Yeah," he said. "Me too."
