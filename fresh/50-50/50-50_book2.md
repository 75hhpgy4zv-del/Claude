# Chapter One: Tuesday

The argument had started, as it always started, with the scallion pancakes.

He set the containers on the coffee table and she looked up at them and then at him with an expression of complete and preemptive innocence.

"Don't," he said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You have a face."

"I have my regular face."

"You have the scallion pancake face." He sat beside her and she shifted automatically to accommodate him, the small unconscious adjustment of two people who had learned each other's physical grammar. "Which is the face you make right before you say something indefensible about scallion pancakes."

"The ratio," she said, with the serenity of someone who has long since stopped needing to win, "is correct."

"There it is."

"It has always been correct."

"Maya."

"I want that on record. Formally. For the eighteen months of record."

He looked at the scallion pancakes. He looked at her. She was watching him with the expression that was almost a smile and knew it and was not going to do anything about it.

"I'm putting it in the vows," he said.

"You've been threatening that for three months."

"I have language."

"You do not have language."

"I have a draft. Provisional. Legally non-binding."

She laughed then, the real one, sideways and sudden. She had laughed at this exact threat in various forms approximately eleven times now and it still arrived without warning. He had catalogued this.

"How was your day," she said, when she had finished laughing.

"Good. Adesola was Adesola. I'm still waiting for the day the principal replaces me with her." He opened the nearest container. "How was the stream?"

She reached in beside him. "Really good, actually. We did that thing where I just didn't plan anything. Just showed up and talked."

"And?"

"They were there." She said it simply, with the particular quality she had when something had gone the way she wanted it to go and she was still basking in the warmth of it. "It felt like, I don't know. Like the early days. Before I knew what I was doing and it didn't matter because neither did they."

He looked at her. "That's a good stream."

"It was." She took a bite. Considered it. "These are correct," she said.

He said nothing. He had nothing to say. The case had been made and unmade eleven hundred times and the scallion pancakes were what they were and she was who she was and this was the world they lived in now.

---

The containers were empty by nine. Maya had migrated so that her back was against his shoulder, her legs across the cushions, the laptop abandoned on the table. The television was on at low volume, something neither of them was watching with any particular attention, the ambient presence of it comfortable rather than necessary. Outside the Silver Lake night was doing what it did, the neighborhood settling into its later hours, the restaurant two blocks over still audible as a low undertone if you were listening for it.

Derek was not listening for it. He felt the weight of her against him. The apartment at this hour had the quality of a space that had been chosen rather than arrived at, built over years by someone who needed to prove he could make something worth keeping.

She was quiet beside him for a while. Then: "The podcast is good right now."

"I think so," he said.

"You should." A beat. "I listened to the Chinatown one twice."

He looked down at her. "You told me."

"I'm telling you again." She didn't elaborate. She knew he received things better when they weren't explained at him. She had learned this the way you learn the grammar of a person you are paying attention to. She let it sit.

He sat with it. The episode had aired two weeks ago and he had made it the way he made all of them, following the thought until it went somewhere true, and the thing it had gone to was this: that Gittes loses not because he is wrong but because he is operating inside a system that was designed before he arrived to produce an outcome he is trying to prevent, and his competence and his honesty and his clear-eyed understanding of events are not defenses against that. They are, in a way, beside the point.

"The system produces the outcome," he said. Not to her exactly. Just into the room. "You can be completely right about what's happening and still lose because the machinery is larger than the individual understanding of it."

She was quiet for a moment. "Is that what you believe? About your own life?"

He thought about it seriously, the way she had learned to wait for, the pause that meant he was actually considering rather than reaching for the nearest acceptable answer. "I think I used to. I think I spent a long time believing that if I understood something clearly enough it would resolve itself. That comprehension was a kind of protection."

"And now?"

"Now I think understanding something clearly is its own thing. Separate from what you can do about it." He paused. "That's not the same as nothing."

She turned her head slightly to look at him. "Gittes understands everything at the end. He just can't stop it."

"Right."

"That's not a comfort."

"No," he said. "But he goes anyway."

She looked at him for a moment with the look that meant she was seeing rather than looking, the one he had learned to receive without deflecting. Then she settled back against his shoulder.

"That's why I listened twice," she said.

He didn't say anything. She hadn't needed him to.

The conversation moved the way their conversations moved when they were at their best, which was without destination, one thing opening into another, the film giving way to something about his students and then to something she had been thinking about the community and what it meant to be known by a large number of people who only knew one version of you, and then somehow to her grandmother, who had known only one version of her too but had never mistaken it for the whole, and then back to nothing in particular, the words thinning out naturally the way words do when two people have said enough and the silence that follows is not empty but continuous with everything that came before it.

After a while her breathing changed in the particular way it changed when she had stopped requiring anything from the moment except its continuation. He knew it without thinking it, the knowledge somewhere below language.

He sat with that for a while.

He looked at the shelf. The Morricone. The Coltrane. The Shuggie Otis where they had put it together. The shelf looked back at him, specific and exact and entirely itself.

Outside, a car passed. The upstairs neighbor moved through their apartment in the way they moved at this hour, the familiar overhead geography of another life. Maya's breathing was even and quiet against his shoulder.

He reached over and turned off the television.

The room settled into a late evening silence that was not empty but full, full of the city's low register, full of her presence, full of the accumulated weight of a Tuesday that had asked nothing of either of them and had been, for that reason, exactly enough.

# Chapter Two: Saturday

The grandmother's pot was already on when he came out of the bedroom.

This was one of the things he had learned about her apartment. Learning someone else's space was different from learning your own, not the acquisition of facts but the gradual absorption of a grammar. Her kitchen in the morning had its own logic. The pot first, always, before anything else, before the phone was checked or the blinds were opened or whatever the day required. He had watched this enough times now that its absence would have registered as wrongness before he could have named why.

She was at the island with her hands around a mug, not drinking, just holding, looking at nothing in particular through the window above the sink. The morning light in her apartment came in at a different angle than the morning light in his, something about the orientation of the building, and it landed on her now in the particular way it landed on Saturday mornings when there was nowhere to be yet, unhurried and warm and slightly golden, the light that Los Angeles occasionally produced when it remembered it was supposed to be beautiful.

He came and stood beside her. She handed him a mug without looking.

"Farmers market?" she said.

"Yeah."

She looked at him then, the considering look, the one that meant she was deciding something. "Los Feliz or the one on Larchmont?"

"Los Feliz."

She nodded, satisfied, the way she was satisfied when something confirmed what she had already decided.

They took her car because his was two blocks further and the morning was too good to walk two extra blocks in the wrong direction. She drove the way she always drove, one hand easy on the wheel, window down two inches, the city doing its Saturday thing around them, Los Angeles on a weekend morning when the traffic hadn't arrived yet and the light was still clean and the whole city felt briefly like the version of itself it was always trying to be.

He had his elbow on the window ledge. She had the radio on low, something neither of them was listening to with any real attention.

"Rajan texted," he said. "He wants to know if we have dietary restrictions."

She looked at him. "Rajan knows you don't have dietary restrictions."

"Rajan is being a good host."

"Rajan has fed you a hundred times."

"A hundred and twelve," he said. "I counted."

He glanced at her. "I told him no dal."

She said nothing. She was smiling at the road.

---

The market was doing what markets did on Saturday mornings in Los Feliz, unhurried and abundant, the stalls arranged along the closed-off street with their particular geometries of color, the stone fruit and the cut flowers and the bread that people carried under their arms like something precious. He had been coming to this market for six years, long enough that some of the vendors knew him by sight if not by name, the nod of recognition that meant nothing except that you had shown up enough times to be considered a regular.

Walking beside Maya, he felt a different quality of recognition.

It was subtle. It was always subtle, he had come to understand, because the people who recognized her were generally too aware of their own recognizing to be obvious about it. A second glance. A slight recalibration of attention. Once, a phone coming out, then going back into a pocket, the person apparently deciding against it. He noticed these things the way he noticed most things, with the part of him that was always quietly cataloguing, and he did not mention them to her because she knew and mentioning it would only make the knowing heavier.

She was looking at the stone fruit with the full attention she brought to things she was actually interested in. She picked up a peach, held it, set it down, picked up another. He stood slightly behind her and watched her do this and thought about nothing in particular.

"These," she said, holding out two peaches.

"Okay."

"You didn't look at them."

"I trust your peach judgment."

She looked at him with an expression that was trying to determine whether this was sincere or another front in the scallion pancake war. He kept his face neutral. She paid for the peaches.

They were at the bread stall when the woman approached. She was perhaps thirty, with the look of someone who had been working up to something for longer than the moment suggested. She stopped a few feet away, which was itself a kind of consideration, and she said Maya's name with the careful pronunciation of someone who wanted to get it right.

Maya turned. Her face did the thing it did, the warmth arriving with a completeness that was not performance but was also, Derek understood, a particular version of herself she kept ready for exactly this. He had seen it enough to know the warmth was real. It was always real. That was the thing he had understood about her early and had never stopped understanding.

"I'm sorry," the woman said. She stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "I'm sorry to bother you. I just." A breath, heavy, like someone who had been running and had only now stopped. "I'm Gabriela. Gabriela Mora. I just wanted to say thank you. I went through something last year. Something really hard. And your streams." She paused, and in the pause Derek could see the sentence she had practiced dissolving into the reality of actually standing here. "During that year. They were just right. I don't know if that — I just wanted you to know that."

"I'm glad," Maya said. The warmth had shifted into something quieter and more direct. "Genuinely, Gabriela."

The woman nodded, twice, the nod of someone trying to hold themselves together. Small and grateful, the smile of someone who has said the thing and been received. Then she looked at Derek, the polite acknowledgment of someone who knows they are talking to half of a pair, and something shifted in her expression, the warmth of someone who has been hoping for exactly this and is watching it arrive.

"You must be Derek," she said.

"I am," he said.

She looked at him for a moment with the considering look of someone who has formed opinions from a distance and is now calibrating them against the real thing. Then she laughed, small and genuine. "You are in the presence of greatness, you know. We're all expecting you not to ruin it."

She said it lightly. With a smile. The way you say a true thing when you've decided to let it travel as a joke.

"I know," Derek said. Warmly. Not deflecting, not performing humility, not reassuring the woman. Just the simple settled fact of it, said by a man who had looked at all of it and chosen it anyway and was glad. The woman looked at him for a moment and then laughed again, warmer now, something in his answer having confirmed what she'd hoped.

She said it had been lovely and she was sorry to interrupt and she walked back into the crowd with the clean graceful exit of someone who had taken only what she came for.

Maya watched her go.

Derek stood beside her and said nothing. He felt her the way he always felt her in moments like this, the peripheral attunement of two years, and could read the quality of what was happening without needing to look at it directly.

"She meant it kindly," Maya said. Quietly. Not quite to him.

"I know," he said, in the same tone as before.

She was still watching the place in the crowd where Gabriela Mora had disappeared. He looked at her profile. The slight compression at the corners of her mouth that was not the almost-smile, that was something else entirely, something he had catalogued separately. He knew the thought she was having. He knew where it lived in her and what it was made of and he knew, with the particular clarity of a man who pays close attention to the person he loves, that it was not yet ready to be spoken and that speaking it would not help.

He put it in the place he kept things that were real but not yet ready.

"Come on," he said. "Peaches are getting warm."

She looked at him. She knew he had heard her. She knew he had heard the thing underneath the thing she said. She also knew he was not going to push at it. That was what she needed right now. The not-pushing, the room to hold it without having to explain it.

She took his hand and they walked back through the market.

---

Rajan and Preethi's house was in Silver Lake, twelve minutes from the market, the kind of house that had been lived in long enough to look like the people inside it, books in mild disorder on shelves that had run out of space, a kitchen that smelled permanently of something having recently been cooked, a couch that had the deep-cushioned quality of furniture that had absorbed years of conversation. Derek had been coming here for eleven years. He knew which drawer the good bottle opener was in.

Preethi opened the door the way she always opened it, glad to see them rather than performing gladness, and she took the peaches from Maya with a sound of real pleasure, and then she took Maya by the arm and led her toward the kitchen with the purposeful energy of a woman who has been waiting to talk to someone and has now identified her opportunity.

Derek watched them go. Then Rajan appeared from the hallway with two beers and handed him one and they went to the back porch the way they always went to the back porch, because some things established themselves without anyone deciding on them.

The city below them was doing its Silver Lake thing. Rajan leaned on the railing and looked out at it and did not say anything for a moment, which was Rajan, eleven years of him, the willingness to let a silence be a silence before deciding what to do with it.

"So," Rajan said.

"So," Derek said.

Rajan looked at him. The look that meant he had been patient for a reasonable amount of time and was now going to ask the thing he had been not-asking. "Where are you."

Derek looked at the city.

"Derek."

"I know what you're asking."

"I know you know." Rajan took a drink of his beer. "I'm asking anyway."

From inside the apartment came the sound of Maya laughing at something Preethi had said, the real laugh, sudden and sideways, carrying easily through the open door. Derek looked toward it without meaning to.

Rajan watched him do this.

"Yeah," Rajan said, quietly. "Okay."

Derek looked back at the city. Something that was not quite a smile settled in him. He left it alone. He did not try to manage it. He had gotten better at not managing things.

"I'm waiting for the right moment," he said.

"You've been waiting for the right moment for three months."

"It's a significant moment."

"Derek. The right moment is any moment you're in the same room as her."

He thought about that. It was not wrong. "I have something in mind," he said.

Rajan looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, satisfied, the way he was satisfied when something confirmed what he had already suspected. "Good," he said.

Inside, Maya laughed again.

---

Dinner at Rajan and Preethi's was what it always was. Loud, warm, long. The food arriving in abundance, the way it arrived when the people making it believed feeding someone was a form of love, which Derek had always understood was true. Preethi had made three things and Rajan had made the rice badly and been corrected about it without ceremony and accepted the correction with the equanimity of a man who knew his limitations and had organized his life around them sensibly.

They talked about everything. Rajan's firm, a case that was going well and one that wasn't. Preethi's mother's visit, which had been complicated in ways that apparently took the whole drive home to begin describing. A film Derek had seen that Rajan hadn't and would not, he maintained, because he had a policy about films that made him feel things, which Maya found fascinating and Preethi found tiresome in the specific way of something that had been found tiresome for nine years and had not changed.

"What kind of policy," Maya said.

"A firm one," Rajan said.

"Based on what criteria."

"Based on whether Derek recommends them. Derek recommends things that are going to cost me something and then acts like that's a normal thing to do to a person."

"Chinatown cost him three days," Derek said to Maya.

"It cost me a week," Rajan said. "I maintain that Chinatown is not a film, it is a targeted emotional attack."

Maya was smiling. "I listened to his episode about it twice."

Rajan looked at Derek. Derek looked at his plate.

"Right," Rajan said. He reached for the serving dish with the expression of a man who has confirmed something and is choosing not to make an event of it. "More rice?"

The evening moved the way good evenings moved, which was without awareness of its own passage, and by the time they were on the couch with the remains of dinner cleared and something on the television that no one was watching with any real attention, the city outside had gone fully dark and late Saturday had arrived, the city quieter but not quiet, a night that still had time in it.

Maya was talking to Preethi about something, the two of them turned toward each other on the far end of the couch with the self-contained quality of a conversation that did not require or invite addition. Derek was aware of her the way he was always aware of her in rooms, not watching, just attuned, the peripheral knowledge of where she was and what she was doing that had installed itself without his deciding to let it.

Rajan came to stand beside him. He said nothing for a moment, looking across the room at nothing in particular. He took a drink of his beer.

Derek let the silence be the silence.

Across the room, Maya said something that made Preethi laugh loudly, and she looked up at the sound of it, and her eyes found Derek's the way they found him in rooms, without searching, just arriving, and she held it for a moment and then went back to Preethi.

He kept watching.

He was warm tonight, properly warm, and he thought: this is a good day. He thought it without anticipating the loss of it.

# Chapter Three: The Taiwanese Place

They had not been back since the early months.

He had suggested it on Tuesday, casually, the way he suggested things when he had already decided on them and was giving the decision the appearance of spontaneity. She had said yes the same way, which meant she had understood what he was doing and was choosing not to say so, which was one of the thousand small mercies of knowing someone well enough that they could read your silences.

The restaurant was the same. Of course it was the same. It had the quality of places that exist outside of time, restaurants that have been doing one thing well for long enough that change would be a kind of betrayal, the same tables, the same menu with its laminated pages, the same woman at the front who nodded at Derek with the recognition of a regular, the same quality of light, warm and slightly amber, the kind that made everything inside it look like it was already a memory.

They were shown to a table near the window.

Maya picked up the menu she did not need and looked at it.

Derek picked up the menu he did not need and looked at it.

"The scallion pancakes," she said.

"Yes," he said.

"We should get them."

"We should," he said.

She looked at him over the top of her menu. He was looking at his menu the way a man looks at something he is not reading. She had catalogued approximately forty different versions of Derek's attention and this was not any of them. This was something new, or not new exactly, she had seen it in pieces before, but assembled in this configuration, in this restaurant, on this particular evening, it was something she had not seen before and was finding, privately, extraordinary.

She went back to her menu.

"The pork chop rice is good," she said.

"I know."

"You've had it."

"Many times."

"Do you want it tonight."

He looked up. "What?"

"The pork chop rice. Do you want it."

"Oh." He looked at his menu. "Yes. Probably."

She set her menu down. She folded her hands on the table and looked at him with the look that was her trying not to smile, which she had never once sustained past the three-second mark in all the time she had known him. She made it to two.

"Derek."

"Maya."

"Are you okay."

"I'm fine," he said. "I'm completely fine."

"You've read that menu four times."

"I'm deciding."

"You just said you want the pork chop rice."

"I'm deciding about other things."

She looked at him. He looked at his menu. The restaurant moved around them with the comfortable ambient noise of a Saturday dinner service, other conversations at other tables, the specific warm clatter of a kitchen producing food for people who were hungry, and Derek Krishnamurthy was reading a menu he had memorized approximately forty visits ago and finding it very interesting.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay," he said.

The server came. They ordered. The scallion pancakes, the pork chop rice, something with tofu that Maya liked. The server left. Derek picked up his tea and set it down without drinking it.

Maya watched him do this and said nothing. She was enjoying herself enormously and felt no obligation to hide it and also felt no obligation to make it easier for him, because this was clearly something he needed to do in his own time and in his own way and her job right now was to be here. She was.

"This was the first place," he said, eventually.

"I know," she said.

"You invited me here. After the balcony."

"I did."

"I thought you were just being polite."

She looked at him. "Derek. I told you to come argue with me about scallion pancakes."

"I thought."

"That's not polite. That's an invitation."

He was quiet for a moment. "I know that now."

He looked at her. Something in his face shifted, the quality she had learned to recognize as Derek arriving at the edge of the thing he had been circling and choosing to step over it. His hand went to his jacket pocket. It had been going there all evening, she realized, the small unconscious movement she had noticed and filed and not mentioned, and now it went there with intention.

He put the ring on the table between them.

It was simple. A thin band, a stone that caught the amber light of the restaurant and held it. The ring of a man who had thought carefully about what she would want and had been right.

She looked at it for a moment.

Then she looked at him.

He was watching her with the expression she did not have a name for yet, the one she had no name for yet. Not nerves exactly. Not certainty either. The face of a man who already knows and is terrified anyway.

"I've had that for three months," he said.

"I know," she said.

He blinked. "You know."

"Derek." She looked at him with the full warmth of eighteen months and the eye roll of a woman who has watched this man receive every difficult thing in his life with extraordinary grace and is now watching him come apart over something that has been inevitable since approximately the second Tuesday they ever spent together. "I know you."

"Rajan told you."

"No."

"Preethi."

"Nobody told me anything." She looked at him steadily. "You've been threatening to put the scallion pancakes in the vows for exactly three months. You suggested this restaurant on Tuesday. You've had your hand in your jacket pocket four times tonight." A beat. "I know you."

He was quiet for a moment. Something moved across his face. She let it. "Four times," he said.

"At least four."

He looked at the ring on the table between them. He looked at her. He was still nervous, she could see it, the nervousness was real and was not going anywhere, and she loved him for it with a specific completeness that she did not have the architecture to express, the love of a woman watching a man come entirely undone by the fact that she matters to him.

"Maya," he said.

"Yes," she said.

He stopped. "I haven't asked yet."

"Derek."

"I've been thinking about what I wanted to say for a while now. About what this has been. About what you are to me and what I think we are together and why that matters in the way that it does." He paused. He was looking at her and she was looking at him and he had three more sentences after this one, carefully prepared, and he knew what they were and he knew they were good and he knew, with the specific clarity of a man who had just run out of patience for the distance between where he was and where he was trying to get, that none of it was necessary. He stopped. Not because he had lost the words. Because he had found something more direct than the words.

She waited. She would wait as long as he needed. She would sit in this restaurant with the scallion pancakes arriving and the amber light and his ring on the table between them and wait for however long it took him to find the words she knew he had been preparing for three months and she would not rush him and she would not smile and she absolutely smiled.

"You're smiling," he said.

"I'm not."

"You're absolutely smiling."

She said nothing. She was smiling.

He looked at her. The prepared speech was still there, complete, waiting. He left it there.

"Will you marry me."

Not a question exactly. The words had the shape of a question but the weight of something that had already been decided a long time ago and was only now being said out loud. No preamble. No speech. Just the thing itself, clean, the way he always got there eventually when he stopped building around it.

"Yes," she said. Simply. With the full register of it. Not the almost-smile. The real thing.

He picked up the ring. His hands were steady. Of course they were. The nervousness had been about the asking, not the doing, and the asking was done now and he was himself again, the version of himself that she had spent eighteen months learning to receive without deflecting, the version that was present and entirely there.

He put the ring on her finger.

And then neither of them said anything.

The restaurant continued around them, the warm amber clatter of other people's Saturday evenings, plates arriving at other tables, other conversations at their own register, the kitchen doing what kitchens do. None of it reached them. They were in the stillness of a moment that had been anticipated for months and had now arrived and was what it was. A man and a woman at a table. A ring on her finger. The rest of their lives beginning, quietly, from here.

She looked at her hand. At the ring catching the amber light.

He looked at her.

There was nothing to say that the moment didn't already contain. She understood this and so did he and neither of them tried to fill it. This was one of the things she loved about him, had loved since the balcony, the specific quality of a man who understood that some moments were complete in themselves and required no addition.

They stayed in the stillness for a while.

Then she said, quietly: "Three months."

"I was waiting for the right moment."

"And this is the right moment."

"Any moment I'm in the same room as you is the right moment." A beat. "Rajan told me that. I already knew it."

She looked at him with the look that was not the almost-smile and not the real laugh and was something else entirely, something she did not have a performance for, the look that was simply her, fully, in the way she was fully herself only when the camera was off and there was only him. "I know you knew," she said.

The scallion pancakes arrived. The server set them down with the brisk efficiency of someone who has not noticed that the world just changed at this particular table, which was right. How it should be. The world did not need to know. It had happened anyway.

She looked at the pancakes. She looked at him.

"They're going to be correct," she said.

"They are going to be wrong," he said. "They have always been wrong."

She laughed. The real one. Sideways and sudden and entirely itself. He had first heard it through a laptop screen and then in three dimensions on a balcony and every time since and it still arrived before he was ready for it and he thought, sitting in this restaurant on this particular Saturday, that he would spend the rest of his life not being ready for it and being glad of that.

They stayed for another two hours. They did not resolve the argument. They were not going to resolve the argument. That was not what the argument was for.

# Chapter Four: The Leak

She had asked him about his day.

This was later — four days after the Taiwanese place, a Sunday, the ring on her finger while she moved around the kitchen with the unhurried quality she had when there was nowhere to be and no performance required. He had been at the table watching her do this without trying to look like he was watching her. He had catalogued approximately fifteen things he loved about the way she moved in this kitchen and was adding to the list.

"Preethi texted again," she said. "She wants to know when we're going to do something. A dinner. A party. Something."

"What did you say?"

"That I'd ask you."

He looked at her. "I don't have a preference."

"You have opinions about everything."

"I have opinions about things that matter." He reached for his tea. "We can do whatever you want to do."

She leaned against the counter and looked at him with the look that was doing the quiet calculation she always did before she said the thing she had been circling. "Should we announce it? Officially."

He considered. "When do you want to."

"I don't know." She looked at the ring, the small thing she had been looking at four or five times an hour since Saturday in the way of someone who is not yet used to finding something there. "I want to have it for a few days first. Before it's anyone else's."

"Okay."

"Is that strange."

"No," he said. "That's correct."

She looked at him. The warmth arrived with its usual completeness, no preamble, just there. "A few days," she said. "Then we'll tell people."

---

On Monday she wore the ring to the coffee place near her apartment, the one where the morning staff knew her well enough to start her order when they saw her come through the door. She was not wearing it to conceal it. She was wearing it because it was on her finger and she was going to get coffee and the world was still the world. She sat in the corner with her laptop and worked for an hour, the ring catching light in the way that thin rings catch light, the way it had been catching light all morning in her apartment when she was not expecting it, a small warm surprise each time.

She went home. She made lunch. She sat with the ring for the rest of the afternoon, catching up on emails, planning the week's content with the ring present in the peripheral of her vision, hers and quiet.

She did not know about the photo until Tuesday afternoon.

---

She was on the phone with Jess when it happened, or rather when she found out it had already happened. Jess's voice changed first, mid-sentence, the shift in register that meant she was looking at something else at the same time.

"Maya."

"Yeah."

"Have you been on Twitter today?"

"Okay," she said.

By the next morning it had four hundred and eleven.

# Chapter Five: Saturday Night

# Chapter Six: The Isolation of Maya Chen

The title appeared in her notifications on a Thursday, two weeks after the announcement.

She had been back to streaming regularly, three times that week, the community warm and enormous in the way it was enormous, the congratulations still arriving in chat with the persistence of something that had not yet exhausted itself. Derek had appeared in the background of one stream, briefly, walking through the frame with a glass of water, and the chat had done what the chat did, a thousand messages in twelve seconds, *HI DEREK*, *Derek spotted*, *the man himself*, warm and quick and dispersing, and she had made the small gesture toward the camera that meant *yes, that's him, carry on* and carried on.

She had been telling herself it was working.

The title appeared in her YouTube notifications because someone had tagged her account in a comment on the video, which meant the algorithm had surfaced it, which meant the video had been there for some time, building, before she saw it.

*The Isolation of Maya Chen.*

Posted by: The Archivist.

Sixteen thousand views.

---

She watched it in her stream room, the door closed, the ring light off, the room existing in its natural state which was just a room, smallish, with equipment in it and a window she kept covered when she was recording. In the dark of the not-recording room she sat in the chair she sat in for streams and watched the video.

The voice was her first note. She had expected either amateur hour or something calculated and cold, the two modes she had encountered before in the small amount of parasocial criticism she had directly engaged with over seven years. This was neither. The voice was warm, the genuine warmth of someone who was invested, and underneath the warmth was something else, a particular kind of pressure, like a room where the air pressure has changed slightly and no one has opened a window.

*I want to start with Maya. I always want to start with Maya. That's the truth.*

A swallow. Audible in the edit, not cut.

*I've been watching her streams since 2021. I know her work the way—the way you know someone's work when it matters to you. I'm not going to pretend otherwise. So when I say what I'm about to say, I need you to understand that it's coming from that place. From caring.*

Maya kept her face neutral. She had learned this. The face of the viewer.

The clips began.

She had been prepared, she thought, for the structure of it. An argument assembled from footage. She had not been prepared for how accurately she recognized herself in the footage, or rather how accurately she recognized the question being asked. Clips from six months ago, eight months ago, a year ago. The stream numbers, which the Archivist had looked up and annotated on screen: four hours, five hours, four and a half. The clips from three months ago, two months ago: two hours. An hour and forty. One hour.

*She's scaling back,* the voice said. *There's nothing wrong with that on its face. People scale back. But look at when it starts.*

A graph. Annotated.

She looked at the graph. The Archivist had done their research. The curve was, in fact, real. She had been streaming less frequently. She had, in fact, been spending more time at Derek's apartment in Silver Lake, or he at hers, evenings that had not been streamed because they were evenings she was spending with him, privately, in the ordinary way of two people who had chosen each other.

She had not framed it to herself as streaming less. She had framed it as having a different kind of life.

*Ask yourself: what changed? What was different, around that time?*

A photograph. Her and Derek, from a fan shot she had not posed for, taken at an event she had attended six months ago, before the engagement. The two of them in conversation, neither of them aware of the camera.

*Him,* the voice said. Simply. The single word, landing.

---

She watched it to the end.

It was twelve minutes and forty-three seconds. It did not claim Derek had done anything specific. It did not use the word abuse. It did not use the word isolation. It used the words *quieter* and *less present* and *we used to know where she was* and *I hope she's okay* and *I'm worried* and *have you noticed*, and none of these were accusations and all of them together were an architecture.

At the end, the voice said: *I'll be watching. I'm always watching, Maya. We're all watching. We love you. Talk soon.*

She sat in the chair after the video ended.

The view count had been sixteen thousand when she opened it. She refreshed the page.

Eighteen thousand, three hundred.

The comments section was running. The pattern was visible even from the top scroll: people who recognized themselves in the watching, who also had noticed, who had been worried, who had been thinking the same thing, who were glad someone had said it, who remembered her from before, who could tell she was different now, who thought the man in the background walking with a glass of water had been *off* somehow, who hoped she was okay, who were here for her, who were always here for her, who would be watching.

*Talk soon,* someone had written in the comments, eleven hundred times, or rather the phrase had appeared eleven hundred times, copied and dispersed the way phrases spread through communities that want to show they are part of something.

*Talk soon.*

---

She called Derek.

"I need to tell you something," she said, "and I need you to let me tell it to you before you react."

"Okay." He had been in the middle of something, she could hear him setting it down.

She told him about the video. She described it with the care she took when describing something that had unsettled her and she wanted to convey what it actually was rather than what it had felt like, the difference mattering to her and to him in the same way. She gave him the view count. She told him the title. She told him about the graph.

When she finished he was quiet.

"How are you feeling about it," he said.

"I don't know yet." She stood at her window. The Burbank afternoon was doing its afternoon thing, the light at this hour always slightly bronze, always slightly ready to be over. "Angry, a little. And also." She paused. "The numbers on the graph are real."

"You've been streaming less."

"Yes."

"Because you've been living your life."

"Yes. But from the outside—" She stopped. "The data they used is accurate. The interpretation is what's wrong."

He was quiet for a moment. The quality of it was not absence but attention, the silence of someone turning something over carefully before they speak. She had learned to receive this. "What does it feel like that the data is accurate."

She had not expected that question. Or she had expected it and not expected to feel it the way she felt it, the small precise pressure of being known by someone who asks the actual question. "Like being seen from the wrong angle," she said. "Like someone found a photograph of you from the wrong angle and said: look, this is what she looks like."

"And."

"And I look different from the right angle."

"You look like someone who is happy," he said, simply.

She looked at the bronze afternoon. "I know." A pause. "Sixteen thousand people don't know that yet."

"They don't need to."

"Derek."

"They don't need to know that," he said again. "The people in your life know it. That's what knowing it actually means."

She heard him saying it and she knew what it was, the thing he believed about intimate knowledge being a different category from public data, she had heard him work through versions of this on the podcast and in their kitchen and late at night and she believed it too, she did. But she also knew sixteen thousand had become eighteen thousand in the twelve minutes she had been on the phone, and some of those eighteen thousand were the people she had spent seven years calling family, and they were worried about her, genuinely, in the way you were genuinely worried about someone you loved, and the worry was real even if the conclusion was wrong.

"It's going to get bigger," she said.

"Probably," he said. "Yes."

"I'm not going to respond to it. I don't think that's the right move."

"No," he said. "I don't either."

"But I want you to know it exists." She turned from the window. "I want you to know what's out there. So you're not—" She stopped. "So it's not a surprise."

She heard him receive this. The specific silence of a man being given information he would have preferred not to need and is accepting it with the grace he brought to things that required grace.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay," she said.

The view count, when she checked after she hung up, was twenty-one thousand.

She closed the tab. She put her phone down on the counter. She stood in her kitchen for a moment, the quiet kitchen that was hers, the light through the window, the ring on her finger, all the ordinary evidence of the actual life she was living.

She opened the tab again. She looked at the number.

Twenty-one thousand and four hundred.

She closed it for the last time that day and went to make dinner.

The Archivist's video would be at sixty-eight thousand by morning.

Neither of them knew this yet.

# Chapter Seven: The Board

It was a Sunday, his apartment, the particular quality of a Sunday that had no agenda and wasn't pretending to. She had her legs across his lap and her phone in her hand and he was reading something, and the afternoon was doing what Silver Lake afternoons did in this season, the light coming through the window at the angle that made the room look considered rather than just furnished.

He looked up.

"I have to show you something."

He put the book down. When she used that tone — not the careful one, not the managed one, the one that was slightly sideways and knew it — he gave her his full attention. She had learned this about him early and had never stopped being glad of it.

He took it. She watched him look at the screen and register what he was looking at. His face did the thing it did when it was actually processing rather than performing processing — the small stillness of it, the eyes moving.

He looked up at her.

"October," he said.

"Yes."

"This is from October."

"Yes."

"The first time," he said. Not a question.

"The first time," she said.

He was quiet for a moment.

"Maya."

"I know."

"I'm aware of when I made it."

He looked at the phone for another long moment. She could see him in it, the full attention, the cataloguing. The board had forty-seven pins. She had not counted them recently but she knew the number the way you know things you have been adding to quietly for months.

# Chapter Nine: Derek's Dark Patterns: A Deep Dive

# Chapter Twelve: The Recording
