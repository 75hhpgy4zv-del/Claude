# 3

His mother's house had a smell that arrived before the door did, and it got him from the
driveway every time. Curry leaves going into hot oil, hitting and spitting and curling
black at the edges. Under it the sweetness of onions that had been going an hour. Under
that, the house: thirty years of the same detergent, the same incense on Fridays, the
carpet in the front room that should have been replaced in 2009 and had instead been
shampooed annually by a man who did not believe in replacing carpet.

"Annam tinnava?"

"Amma, it's eleven in the morning."

"That's not an answer."

"It's a dodge. Dodges are a category of answer."

She hit him on the arm with a dish towel. She was seventy-one and had got smaller in a
way he noticed in eight-month increments, and she did the thing she always did in the
doorway, which was to hold his wrist while she talked, as though he might be planning to
leave through the wall.

Inside, his father got up out of the chair to turn the television down rather than off.
This was the greeting, and had been since roughly 2004: the volume going from eleven to
three, his father half out of the chair and then back into it, the news going on
happening silently in the corner for the rest of the visit. He asked about the brakes on
the Civic. Then he talked for eleven minutes about a bridge
retrofit in Bakersfield he'd read about, the deck replacement, the closure schedule, the
overrun. The retrofit was his father's way of talking about his other son, and Derek let
him, because it cost nothing and the old man was enjoying himself, one hand turning
slowly in the air to describe a span.

At 11:50 Anand came in with a bag of mangoes and a story about the traffic.

---

His brother was ten years older and taller and had never once in his life wondered
whether he was welcome in a room. He hugged their mother. He clapped Derek on the
shoulder with the flat of his hand.

"There he is. The professor."

"Teacher."

"Same thing."

They ate. The food came out in the order it had come out for twenty-five years and the
table filled up past the point of sense: pulihora, gutti vankaya, a fish curry that had
no business being that good, two vegetables nobody would finish, the pappu, the pickle,
a bowl of curd sitting in condensation at the edge like an afterthought that would turn
out to be the best thing on the table. His mother said three times that it hadn't come
out right. Nobody contradicted her, because contradicting her was part of the recipe and
she'd have been suspicious if it stopped.

Anand talked about the firm, a merger, a man named Rick who was an idiot, a project in
Riverside six weeks behind because of somebody else. Within four minutes everyone at the
table was turned toward him and nobody had decided to. Derek asked the follow-up
questions, which he was good at, and made his father laugh twice.

Then Anand put his fork down. "So what's the plan, though."

"With?"

"The internet thing." He said it kindly. That was the part nobody who hadn't sat at this
table could be made to understand. He said everything kindly. "Because Meera's cousin's
kid in Fremont does the same thing with the games. Twenty-two, bought his mother a car.
And I'm sitting here going, my brother's been at this since when, 2015?"

"Fourteen."

"Eleven years. So I'm asking as somebody on your side. What's the return?"

"Four hundred and eleven dollars a month and a comprehensive knowledge of Paul
Verhoeven."

Anand laughed. Their mother laughed. It was going fine.

"No, but seriously."

"That was serious."

"Okay, but you're a smart guy—"

"There's a five-year model." It came out of Derek's mouth before he had decided anything.
"Six tabs. I've walked nineteen properties in three counties. I know what a laser
projector costs installed and what the licensing runs on a thirty-five-millimetre print
out of Kerala and how many seats you have to sell on a Tuesday to survive a Wednesday."

The table went quiet in an interested way. His mother had stopped serving.

"A theatre," Derek said. "Not a multiplex. A house that runs world cinema, any era, and
actually programs it, and—"

"How much?"

"What?"

"To open the doors." Anand had his phone out, not to look at it, just holding it. "First
one. All in."

"One point four."

"Million."

"Yes."

Anand set the phone face-down on the tablecloth and left his hand on it. "Okay," he said,
nodding slowly, generously, already assembling something out of it. "Okay. And where does
one point four come from, on a teacher's salary, at thirty-five, with no—"

He stopped himself there and picked his water up.

"I'm not being a jerk. I'm the only person at this table who's going to ask you the
actual question."

"You're really not."

"Derek."

"Ask the actual question, then. Go ahead. Finish the sentence."

"I said I wasn't being a jerk."

"With no *what*, Anand." His voice had gone up and got precise at the same time, which
was what it did. "With no wife. Say it. It's been eleven months since you've come within
a mile of it and you've been circling since the mangoes, so let's just — no. Here's the
actual question, since you're the brave one. Ask me why a man whose wife left with his
best friend hasn't got one point four million dollars. I'd love to lay it out. I'd love
to do the tabs."

Nobody moved. His mother had the serving spoon still in the rice and did not take it
out. Behind Derek the television went on happening in the corner, a man in a blazer
mouthing silently about the freeway, and the ceiling fan turned, and the condensation
went on sliding down the outside of the bowl of curd, and the whole table waited the way
a room waits out a passing siren. Somewhere under the window a sprinkler head clicked
on and started its slow arc across the grass.

His mother said, quietly, "Orey."

One word. Something in Derek's chest went straight through the floor, because his own
voice was still hanging over the food and it was ugly and it had been aimed across a
table at a woman who had been up since six.

"Okay," he said. "No. I'm sorry. Anand — I'm sorry. That was completely out of line and
you didn't earn it. You asked a normal question and I've been carrying a speech about it
for six years and you got the whole thing in the face. That's not your fault. I'm sorry."

Anand put his hand up, easy, already past it. "Hey. It's fine. It's forgotten."

"It's not fine, I'm—"

"Derek." Warm. Enormous. "It's forgotten."

Then he turned to their mother and said, "This is why he's the sensitive one," and she
laughed with relief, a real one, her shoulders coming down two inches, and their father
reached across for the rice, and the fan turned, and the table closed over the whole
thing like water over a dropped stone.

Nobody asked another question about the theatre. Not one, for the remaining ninety
minutes.

---

At the door his mother took his face in both hands. Her palms smelled of coriander and
dish soap and she held on a beat past comfortable, the way she had at airports since he
was ten years old.

"Jagratha, nanna."

He got out onto the step and his father came out after him, which he did not do. The old
man stood squinting into the white eleven-o'clock light with his arms folded, working up
to something, and the sprinkler went on clicking around the side of the house.

"The projector," he said.

"What?"

"You said laser projector. There are two kinds?"

"There's — yeah. There's lamp and there's laser. Lamp's cheaper going in and you're
buying bulbs for the rest of your life."

His father nodded. He looked at the driveway for a while, at the oil stain that had been
there since the Corolla, and Derek stood there and let him take as long as he wanted.

"Bakersfield," his father said at last. "They used the cheap bearings. Forty years, now
they're paying for it." He reached over and patted Derek twice on the shoulder blade,
which was the most he had ever done. "Get the good one."

Then he went inside before Derek could put a single word together.

---

The car had been sitting in the sun for three hours and the air that came out when he
opened the door had a weight to it. Derek got in anyway. The seatbelt buckle was too hot
to touch and he took it by the strap, and the steering wheel was too hot to hold so he
rested his wrists at the bottom of it, and the whole dashboard gave off that smell that
hot cars give off, warm dust and old plastic and the ghost of every coffee he had ever
spilled in there. Across the street a man was running a leaf blower over a driveway that
was already clean.

He sat for eleven minutes.

What kept coming back was not the fight. Not *with no wife*, and not *it's forgotten*,
which would be with him at two in the morning for a week.

It was that his father had come out onto the step.

The leaf blower stopped. Derek started the car and drove home the long way, down through
the part of the valley where the strip malls give out, past the shuttered second-run
house on Peck with the marquee that still said CLOSED THANK YOU FOR 41 YEARS in letters
somebody had climbed a ladder to arrange.

He was inside for four minutes before he opened the laptop.

`THEATRE_v9.xlsx`. Last modified the eighth of June.

He sat down in front of it with his shoes still on. The tabs came up in their row along
the bottom, the way he had left them, and he went to the third one, and found the line
where he had put the lamp projector in because the lamp projector was what he could
defend to a stranger, and he deleted the number and typed in the other one.

It broke the model by two hundred and ten thousand dollars.

He looked at the red for a while. Then he saved it as v10, and got up, and went to find
something to eat.
