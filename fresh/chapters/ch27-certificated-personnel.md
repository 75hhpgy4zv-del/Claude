# Chapter Twenty-Seven
## Certificated Personnel

The letter came on the sixteenth of June in a window envelope with the district's return address on it, in among a water bill and something from a dentist he had not been to since 2019.

He opened it standing at the boxes by the gate because there was no reason not to.

It is one page. There is a reference number at the top and his employee ID under that, and the body of it is four sentences long, and the fourth sentence is *We thank you for your service to the District.*

He read it twice at the boxes and then again in the kitchen.

The call came the next morning at eleven twenty from a number he did not know.

"Mr. Kolluri? Sorry — is it Kuh-LOO-ree?"

"Kolluri. That's right."

"Great, thank you. My name's Denise Ruiz, I'm with Certificated Personnel, this is a courtesy follow-up on the notice we mailed on the twelfth. Have you received that?"

"I got it yesterday."

"Perfect." He could hear her typing. "So — just to confirm what's in the letter. Your unpaid leave concludes on July thirty-first, and the District won't be renewing the assignment for the coming year. Benefits run with the leave, so coverage terminates on the thirty-first as well, and you'll get the continuation packet in the mail separately, probably about ten days."

"Okay."

"Do you have any questions for me today?"

Derek stood in his kitchen with the letter in his hand.

"Can I ask what the reason is."

"So — " and there was a pause, and it was not an unkind pause; it was the pause of a person going and looking at a screen. "It's a non-renewal, so there isn't a reason field. It's not a disciplinary action. There's nothing on your record and nothing that would show on a reference."

"There's a review."

"Sorry?"

"There's a district review. My principal told me there was a review."

Typing.

"I don't have anything about a review," said Denise Ruiz. "I've got the leave, and I've got the non-renewal, and those are two separate — " She stopped. "You know what, that might be at the site level. Your principal would know more than me about that."

"Right."

"Was there anything else?"

He looked out of the window at a dead tree wired to two lengths of rebar.

"Who's taking Room 14?"

"Sorry?"

"My classroom. Room 14. It's the sophomore sequence and there's about two hundred pages of curriculum that I wrote that only makes sense if somebody walks them through it, and I'd like to know who to send it to."

There was a longer pause this time.

"I don't have room assignments," she said. "That's — that would be your site administrator. I'm sorry, I really only have the personnel action."

"Of course. Sorry."

"No, please. Um — " and for about a second and a half she was not reading from anything. "I'm sorry about this. It's a rotten one."

"Thank you."

"Okay. So we'll get you that packet, and if you don't see it by the twenty-seventh you'll want to call the main line and ask for Benefits, not for me, because I can't see any of that." She was typing again. "And you'll need to return District property to your site by the end of the month. Keys, badge, anything with an asset tag."

"I've got a badge and two keys."

"That's usually it," said Denise Ruiz. "Thanks so much, Mr. Kolluri. You have a good one."

The call was six minutes.

---

He drove out on the Friday to hand in two keys and a badge, because that was the actual instruction and because it gave the week a thing in it.

The building in late June is a different building. No kids, no bells, three of the four hallways dark because the district turns the lights off, and the whole place smelling of the floor product they only use in summer. Somebody had stacked forty chairs outside the gym and gone home.

Wendy was in the front office with a fan pointed at herself.

"Oh," she said.

"I've got keys."

"I know you've got keys, Ricardo emailed me about the keys, I've been *ignoring* the email about the keys." She did not take them. "Sit down a minute."

"I'm all right."

"Derek."

He sat down for a minute.

She got him a form and filled most of it in herself, and put the two keys and the badge in a manila envelope, and wrote his name on it in marker, and then stood there holding it, which is not a thing anybody does with an envelope.

"Are they letting you get your room?"

"Nobody's told me not to."

"Then go and get your room," said Wendy.

Room 14 is on the second floor at the end. It was about ninety degrees in there. The chairs were stacked six high on the desks the way the custodians do it in June, which makes a classroom look like a warehouse of itself.

He took down the Kurosawa one-sheet, and a laminated photocopy of the first page of *Beloved* that a kid had annotated so aggressively in 2019 that Derek had asked to keep it. He took the mug with the chip in the handle off the windowsill. There was still something on the whiteboard in his own handwriting from the twelfth of December — *THURSDAY: bring the thing you underlined* — and he left it, on the grounds that somebody else would deal with it.

Then he opened the desk drawer.

The lending copies are in there. Eight or nine paperbacks he gives to kids who do not give them back and who are not supposed to: *Beloved*, two *Slaughterhouse-Fives*, a *Persepolis* with the cover off.

Underneath the lending copies there is a letter, a page and a half, in blue ink, from a girl called Adaeze Nwosu who left in 2022.

He sat down in the reading chair to read it. In eleven years he had never once sat in that chair.

Then he put it in the inside pocket of his jacket rather than in the box.

Ferreira caught him in the parking lot.

She had come out of the side door at speed with no bag and no keys in her hand, which meant she had seen him from a window.

"Wendy said."

"I'm just doing the keys."

"I know what you're doing." She stopped about four feet away and put her hands in her pockets and did not come any closer, and he understood the four feet perfectly. "I'm going to be careful, because I've been told to be careful. That's what this is. I haven't gone strange."

"Okay."

"I fought it on the Tuesday and I lost it on the Tuesday, and I'm not allowed to tell you who with or what was said." Her jaw was doing something. "That's the whole of what I've got and it's nothing, and I've been sitting in that office for three days trying to find a version of it that's more than nothing."

"There isn't one."

"No."

The parking lot was empty except for eleven cars and the heat coming up off it.

"Marcia."

"Don't be nice to me about this, Derek, I will genuinely lose it in front of the gym."

"I was going to ask who's taking the sophomore sequence."

Ferreira looked at the ground for a second.

"Ask me in September," she said. "When you're a person again and I'm allowed to talk to you."

Then she went back in through the side door, and the door did the thing that heavy doors do, and he put a box in the back of a fifteen-year-old Honda and drove home.

The box was still by the door on the Saturday, and on the Sunday, with the mug wrapped in a sweater.

Hector knocked on the Sunday afternoon.

Derek was about nine feet away from the door when it happened. He heard the two knocks and then the pause and then the two knocks again, and then Hector saying "Derek? It's me about the wood, man" through the door in the raised, apologetic voice of a man who does not want to be a nuisance about wood.

The crate was still in four stacked pieces along the side wall where they had left it in May.

Derek did not open the door.

He stood in the hallway with a hand on the frame and listened to a neighbor say his name twice more and then go away, and then, about two minutes later, listened to the specific noise of one man dragging four sheets of plywood around the side of a building on his own.

By Monday there was nothing along the side wall and nobody had said anything about it.

---

The phone went at ten past eight on the Tuesday evening, which was ten past nine on the Wednesday morning in Cologne.

It was on the arm of the chair by his hand. He let it go four rings.

He had answered six emails that day inside the hour and one of them was a good note on a temp mix, and he had not opened the front door since Friday, and he had been in that chair since about two in the afternoon.

He cleared his throat, twice, because he had not said anything out loud since Friday and did not know what was going to come out of him.

"Hi."

"Hi," said Ha-eun, and there was nothing in her voice, so it had worked.

"It's the twenty-third," she said.

"It is."

"So it's four weeks."

The chair by the window is the good one. He bought it in 2019 and it is the only expensive object he owns, and she takes it every single time she is in that apartment.

He had been sitting in it in the dark, with the overhead off and both lamps off, since the middle of the afternoon.

"It's a Three," he said.

"Say more than that."

"It's a Three and it's a spiteful one." He heard himself doing it and did not stop. "It's got teeth this time, and it's not moving, and I think I'm a bit distracted with the school thing, which I'll tell you about properly when you're not walking somewhere."

"I'm not walking anywhere. What school thing?"

"They're not renewing me in August. It came in a letter on the sixteenth and a woman called Denise called me on the seventeenth to confirm it, and it isn't a firing — there's no reason field, there's nothing on my record, there's nothing to appeal." He said all of it accurately and in order, which is the easiest thing in the world to do. "It's eleven families and one very good letter. Ferreira was in the argument and lost it on a Tuesday and isn't allowed to tell me who with."

"*Derek*—"

"There's an insurance thing that runs out on the thirty-first of July and there's a continuation packet coming in the mail, which Denise was extremely clear about, and if it doesn't come by the twenty-seventh I call the main line and ask for Benefits and not for her."

There was a silence from Cologne.

"Is it a Three," said Ha-eun.

"Yeah."

"Say it again."

"It's a Three."

"...Where are you sitting?"

And he had the answer ready, because the competent version had had it ready since about the second ring.

"Kitchen."

"Which light's on."

"The one over the—"

Somebody said her name, close, and then said it again with something after it, and she said "*one minute*" away from the phone with an edge on it he had not heard her use before, and then a second voice, older, said something short in Korean that ended the discussion.

"That's the sound man," she said. "They've got him for forty minutes and then he's on a plane and there's a delay off the back wall I've been complaining about since Sunday."

"Then go."

"I'm not—" She stopped. He could hear her decide. "Which light."

"The one over the sink."

"Okay," said Ha-eun.

"How's Cologne?"

"Cologne's fine. Cologne's a shed with nine thousand people in it." A pause. "You'd have hated the sound. There's a delay off the back wall you could set a clock by."

"Did you ask them to fix it?"

"I asked them to fix it, and a man explained the building to me for eleven minutes."

"What did you say?"

"I said thank you," said Ha-eun, "and then I asked him to fix it again."

He laughed, and it came out at about the right volume and at about the right time, and the two of them talked about a delay off a back wall in a shed in Germany for nine or ten minutes, and it was good, and both of them said so.

"Tuesday," she said, at the end.

"Tuesday."

"And if it moves."

"If it moves I'll tell you the day it moves."

"*Good*," said Ha-eun, and went to work.

Derek put the phone face down on the arm of the chair.

He had not moved for eleven minutes and he did not move now. The window had gone from gray to nothing while they were talking and he had not registered it happening, and the tree was out there somewhere, wired to two lengths of rebar, and he sat in the good chair in the dark with his hands where they had been.
