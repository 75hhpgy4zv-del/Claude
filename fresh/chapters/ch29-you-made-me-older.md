# Chapter Twenty-Nine
## You Made Me Older

Vijay gave him nine days' notice, which was generous, and which meant he wanted something.

He came for a conference in Long Beach that ran Tuesday to Thursday and he stayed until Sunday, and he stayed at the apartment rather than the hotel the firm was paying for, because — and he said this twice, warmly, as a kindness — he wanted to actually *see* him for once.

He arrived on the Monday with a duty-free bag and a bottle of something Derek would not drink and an opinion about the parking situation on the street.

"You're in a permit zone."

"I'm aware."

"You've got a permit?"

"I've had a permit for nine years, Vijay."

"I'm just checking. People don't check." He put the bag on the counter. "Amma says you're not sleeping."

---

He was, in his own way, being extremely good about it.

That was the thing Derek had to keep reminding himself across four days, and did, and it did not help. Vijay had not come to be cruel. Vijay had never in his life come to be cruel. He cooked one night, badly, from a recipe he had watched on his phone, and was proud of it, and it was fine. He fixed the thing with the door that Derek had been meaning to fix since March, correctly, with tools he had bought that afternoon at his own expense, and would not take the money.

And he had, it emerged over the first evening, a plan for Derek's life.

"So the book's doing what, four hundred thousand?"

"Four twenty."

"Four twenty." Vijay said it the way he said load-bearing figures, which was with a small nod, as if the number had passed an inspection. "And there's a movie."

"There's a film, yes."

"So this is the moment." He put his beer down. "I've been thinking about this on the flight, actually, and I want you to hear me out before you do the thing where you—"

"Do the thing where I what."

"Where you're allergic to a good idea because I'm the one saying it." He grinned, and it was affectionate, and it was accurate, and Derek let it go. "This is the moment. You've got heat, right? Heat is a *window*. Nobody has a window forever. So you leave the school."

Derek looked at his brother across a kitchen counter.

"You leave the school," Vijay said, "you take a year, you do the second book properly with no distractions, you get an agent — a real one, not the one you got given, a *West Coast* one — and you turn this into what it's supposed to be. Because right now you're a guy who wrote a bestseller and grades essays, and that's not a *story*, Derek, that's a — nobody knows what to do with that."

"I like the school."

"I know you like the school."

"I'm good at it. That's not a small—"

"You're extremely good at it, that's why I'm saying this," Vijay said. "You could be extremely good at it in five years. It'll still be there. What won't still be there is *this*."

---

He had a whole architecture and he had built it on the plane.

There was a version with a production company in it. There was a version where Derek did what he called "the pod thing, but properly" — with a network, with an ad sales team, with somebody else doing the editing. There was a version, mentioned once, lightly, in the tone of a man raising a thing he has decided in advance to be reasonable about, in which the school stuff *was the brand* and Derek stayed for a year and made content about it.

Derek said no about nine times across four days and none of them landed, because none of them were the point. Vijay was not trying to win. Vijay was demonstrating care in the only currency he had ever had, which was *strategic attention*, and every no was received as a data point in a plan that was still being optimized.

By Wednesday night Derek had stopped arguing and started managing, and had noticed himself doing it, and had felt the specific cold of a man watching himself do a thing he had sworn off.

---

Vijay finished the book on Thursday.

He announced it at the door, coming in from the conference at seven, before he had taken his shoes off.

"Okay. I finished it."

"Finished—"

"The book. *The* book." He held up his phone, which had it on it, which meant he had been reading it in a conference in Long Beach. "I read the whole thing. I read it in four days, which for me is — you know what I'm like."

"You read the whole thing."

"Every page." He came in and put his bag down and got a beer, and he was warm, and pleased with himself, and entirely undefended, and Derek stood in his own kitchen and understood that this was going to go somewhere he had not prepared for. "It's good. Genuinely. I'm not — I know I'm not the audience, but it's *good*, and it's better than I expected, and I want to say that first."

"Thank you."

"The woman's very good. Maya. She's the best thing in it."

"She's the invented one."

"Is she?" Vijay was genuinely surprised. "Huh. She reads real."

---

"So I've got one note," he said.

Derek waited.

"You made me older."

The refrigerator did what refrigerators do.

"...Sorry?"

"Vijay. The brother. You made him fifty-two." He said it good-naturedly, with his hands out, the way you raise something you have decided not to make a thing of. "I'm forty-five. I was forty-two when you wrote it. That's a *ten-year* difference, Derek, and I'll be honest with you, it threw me for the first hundred pages, because I kept thinking — is this me? Is it not me? And then he's a civil engineer in New Jersey with two kids and a wife and I thought, okay, it's me, he's just ten years older."

Derek did not say anything.

"Which is fine," Vijay said. "It's fiction, I get it, I'm not one of those people. But I'd have — if you'd asked me, I'd have said forty-five. Or don't do the age at all. That's my whole note."

"That's your note."

"That's my note." He drank some of his beer. "It's a good book, man. Amma's very proud. She's told everyone."

---

Derek stood in his kitchen for a long moment.

There are, he had decided at some point in the previous three years, two kinds of silence available in a family, and this was the second kind: the one where you can feel the whole shape of a thing arriving and you have about four seconds to decide whether you're going to let it.

"Vijay."

"Yeah."

"Did you read chapter fifteen?"

"I read all of it."

"I know you read all of it. I'm asking about fifteen."

Vijay thought about it, with the mild uncertainty of a man being quizzed on something he genuinely has done and has not filed. "The visit? Where he comes to stay."

"Where he comes to stay."

"Yeah. It's good. The dal thing." He laughed. "*You should think about monetizing it better.* That's — that's actually a bit close, I'll give you that. That's the kind of thing I say."

"It's the exact thing you say."

"Okay. Yeah. Fair."

"He asks his brother how many people listen to the podcast," Derek said. "And his brother says *ballpark*. And then he hears the number and he makes a sound. And the book says the sound isn't cruel, because there's no cruelty in him, and that what's actually happening is that he's asking about listeners the way he'd ask about the tensile strength of a cable, because he only has one language and it's measurables, and he's using it to say *I love you* and it arrives as a valuation."

Vijay's face had not changed yet.

"And then it says," Derek went on, "that the brother says *the teaching is fine, but fine is not a plan.* And that the man it's said to goes and stands at the stove for a while, and doesn't say anything, and serves dinner."

Now it changed.

---

"Okay," Vijay said. "Okay, hang on—"

"You've been in that book for three years."

"Derek—"

"Four hundred and twenty thousand people have read you. Under your own name. Your job, your state, your two kids, your beer, your *sentences*." Derek had not raised his voice and he was aware that he was not going to. "There are people on the internet who have written essays about you. There is a woman in Ohio who wrote to me in March about her own brother and used your name to do it, because you're the one she had a word for."

"That's not—"

"And you read it in four days on your phone at a conference and your note is that I aged you up."

Vijay stood in a kitchen in Alhambra with a beer in his hand.

"That's not fair," he said.

"Which part."

"That's — you're being — " He put the beer down. "You want me to have *recognized* myself? Is that the — Derek, come on. It's a *novel.* It's a guy in New Jersey. Am I supposed to read a novel and go, ah, this is a portrait of my failings?"

"No," Derek said. "That's the thing. You're not. You're supposed to read three hundred pages of a man being described with total precision by his brother, and get to the end, and have *one question.* Any question. *Is that me. Was it like that. Did I do that.*" He put his hands flat on the counter. "And you don't. And that's not because you're a bad man, and it's not because you don't love me, because you do, you're the only one of us who calls Amma every Sunday and you drove four hours to my graduation and you fixed my *door* on Tuesday."

"So what—"

"It's that you have never once in forty-five years been curious about me," Derek said.

---

The apartment was extremely quiet.

"You've been *invested*," he said. "You've been invested my whole life. You have opinions about my career, my apartment, my parking, my podcast, my *money*, and every single one of them is generous and none of them has ever once required you to find out anything about me first. You've been managing me since I was ten. And it feels — from the inside, from where I've been standing since I was ten — it feels *exactly* like being loved, and it took me until about thirty-three to work out that they're two different things."

Vijay had gone very still.

"I've been swallowing that since I was ten," Derek said. "That's twenty-five years. And I'm not saying it now because you finally did something. You didn't do anything. You came here and you fixed a door and you were pleased about the book. I'm saying it because I've had a year in which four hundred thousand strangers read every honest thing I've ever written and had opinions about me, and *not one of them was more wrong than you are*, and they've never met me."

---

Vijay left on Sunday.

It did not resolve. That was the part Derek had not braced for and should have: there was no scene. Vijay did not have a realization. Vijay sat down on the couch on Thursday night and said, after about a minute, "I don't think that's true, but I hear that you think it is," which was, Derek understood, the most enormous thing his brother had ever said to him and was also nothing at all.

They were fine on Friday. They were fine on Saturday. They watched a film neither of them cared about and Vijay talked through it. On Sunday he stood in the doorway with his bag and said, "I still think you should leave the school," and Derek said "I know you do," and Vijay hugged him hard, twice, in the way he had hugged him since Derek was six, and said, "Call Amma."

At the top of the stairs he stopped.

"The tattoo on your back," he said. "The English one, on the shoulder. I saw it when you were getting the tools out."

Derek turned around.

"What does it say? I've never asked."

"You've never asked about any of them."

"I know," Vijay said, and had the grace, briefly, to look at the floor. "So."

Derek pulled his collar down at the back, which he could not see and had never seen, and said it from memory, because that is the only way he had ever been able to say it:

> *"It was always the becoming he dreamed of, never the being."*

"Who's that?"

"Fitzgerald. First novel. It's about a young man who is going to be extraordinary and never gets around to it, because being *about to be* something is much better than being it."

Vijay was quiet for a second.

"That's about you," he said.

"It's a warning."

"No, but — " He stopped, and started again, and it was the closest he came all week. "That's not what you are, though. You did the thing. You wrote the book. You're not a guy who was going to."

"I know."

"So why have you still got it on you?"

And Derek stood at the top of an outside staircase in Alhambra on a Sunday morning and found that his brother had asked him, at last, at forty-five, on the way out the door, an actual question about himself that he did not have an answer to.

"I don't know," he said.

"Huh," said Vijay, and went down the stairs, and got in the car.

---

Derek went inside and washed two glasses and sat down at the kitchen table and opened the file.

He wrote the brother scene that afternoon. Four thousand words. It took two and a half hours and it was very good and none of it was invented, and at the end of it he sat back and looked at it and thought, with total clarity and no defence available:

*I just did it again.*

He did not delete it.
