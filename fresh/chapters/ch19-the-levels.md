# Chapter Nineteen
## The Levels

They shot it over two days in week nine because it is a phone call, and a phone call is two people alone in two rooms, and you cannot get both of them in the same afternoon.

Wednesday was his side. Thursday was hers.

It is on the schedule as **Sc. 88 — INT. APARTMENT / READING CHAIR — DAY** and **Sc. 88A — INT. BURBANK APARTMENT / KITCHEN — DAY**, and between them they are the longest unbroken stretch of dialogue in the film.

Derek had known the date since October. It had been on the wall in the production office on a strip of colored card since October, and he had walked past it perhaps four hundred times.

Wednesday went fine.

Rahul was extraordinary and everybody knew it by eleven in the morning, and the rest of the day had the specific quiet of a crew watching somebody do the thing they were hired to do.

He did the levels eleven times.

He did not do them the way he had done them in a casting room in September — badly, on purpose, restarting sentences. He had spent four months on it since. What he did instead was worse to watch, which is that he did them *fluently*. He had the whole architecture and he laid it out in order, calmly, like a man explaining a system he has lived inside for eleven years and has had a long time to make legible, because that is what it is.

*There's always a glass. Even when everything is fine.*

*Two is where things feel slightly delayed.*

*Three is the muffle.*

And then, on the fourth take of the afternoon, at a line about a kitchen, he stopped being fluent for about a second and a half, and it went through the room like a change in cabin pressure, and Mags printed it and did four more and used that one.

Derek did not move for six hours.

He had thought about this day since October and had prepared for it in the way he prepares for things, which is to construct several versions of it in advance so that whichever one arrives has already been survived once.

None of them were this.

What he had not accounted for was how *good* it was going to be. Not accurate — accurate he had braced for, he had written it. Good. Rahul Varma sat in a reading chair on a soundstage in Sun Valley and said Derek's own architecture out loud in his own order, and made it better, and the crew stopped talking, and a woman in the sound department put a hand flat on the cart at one point and left it there.

At lunch Aleks came past and said, "That's heavy, that," and did not wait for an answer, which Derek was grateful for.

---

Thursday was her side and it took eleven hours and forty minutes.

Her side is a woman in a kitchen holding a phone, and she is not doing anything, and she has one line.

Eighty-eight A is four pages of listening. Ha-eun stood at a counter in Burbank-as-built-in-Sun-Valley with a phone against her ear and an earpiece feeding her Rahul from a room forty feet away, and for four pages her job was to receive.

"Do not do anything with your face," Mags said, at nine in the morning. "That's the note. That's the only note. Everybody wants to act at this and you can't, because he's telling her something enormous and the second she performs receiving it she's made it about her."

"So what do I do?"

"You listen. That's the job today." Mags was already walking. "And at the end you say the line, and you say it like you mean it, and you do not say it kindly."

Derek was at the counter with a coffee she had not asked him for and had taken anyway.

"Is it true," said Ha-eun.

"Which part."

"All of it. The glass. The kitchen being twelve feet away." She was not looking at him; she was looking at the phone in her hand, turning it. "Is it a thing you read about."

There were nine people within earshot, three of them holding equipment, and Bo was four feet away with a brush.

"It's true," said Derek.

"Okay."

"It's the most accurate four pages in the book and it took me two years to be able to write it down without making it *interesting*, and it isn't interesting, and that's why it works." He put the coffee on the counter within reach of her hand. "Level Three is boring. That's the whole point of Level Three."

"Bo," said Ha-eun, "do my jaw again."

"Your jaw's fine," said Bo, and did it again anyway.

They went twelve times before lunch and it was not right.

Nobody could say why, which is the worst kind of not-right. She was doing exactly what she had been asked. She held still. She let it come. She said the line at the end and it was warm and it was clean and it landed like a wet towel.

At about one Mags came and stood next to her at the counter and said, "You're being *grateful*."

"You told me to mean it—"

"You're being grateful *at him*. Which is very nice and it's the wrong thing." Mags leaned on the counter. "She's not thanking him for a gift. She's thanking him for *information*. He's just handed her the operating manual to a person she's decided to be near, and she wanted it, and she asked for it, and he gave it to her, and it's *useful.*"

Ha-eun stood at a counter with a dead phone in her hand.

"She's relieved," she said.

Mags looked at her.

"Yeah," she said. "She's relieved."

At two Mags cleared the floor.

She did it without ceremony and without explaining it, the way she did everything: she said "let's go minimum," and Denny said it into the radio, and thirty-odd people put things down and walked out of the building into a parking lot in the cold, and what was left was Mags, Denny, Tomas on sound, Priya on the camera, Bo, and a woman at a counter with a phone against her ear.

Derek stood up to go with them.

"Not you," said Mags, without looking around.

He sat back down.

Nobody in that building reacted to it in any way whatsoever. Denny finished his radio call. Priya changed a battery. A man Derek had never spoken to held the door for eleven people and let it shut.

They went four more times with six people in the room, and it got closer, and on the third of them Ha-eun looked at him.

It was nothing. It was a fraction of a second in the middle of four pages of listening, at the line about the kitchen, and nobody else in that building saw it and it did not make the take unusable.

Derek waited until the reset and then got up and walked out, past Denny, who did not ask.

They got it at four in the afternoon.

Ha-eun listened to eleven minutes of a man's voice in an earpiece describing a room he cannot get out of, and did nothing with her face, and at the end of it there was a silence of about four seconds that nobody had blocked and that Mags did not cut.

And then she said, "*Thank you for telling me that,*" and it was not kind at all. It was flat and it was fast and it had something underneath it that was almost impatient, the sound of somebody who has been handed a real thing at last and is not going to make a performance out of receiving it.

"Cut," said Mags. "That's the film."

Nobody moved for a second. Then Denny said "*checking the gate*" and forty people started doing their jobs again.

Derek was outside by the trucks when she came off.

It was cold. There was a man from transport eating a sandwich on a step. He stood by a container with a dead lemon tree in it and looked at his phone without turning it on.

She came out in the coat and stopped about four feet away.

"You've been out here an hour."

"Forty minutes."

"An hour," said Ha-eun. "Marisol told me."

Neither of them said anything for a moment. A truck reversed somewhere behind the stage and somebody shouted a number.

"It's a good scene," she said.

"It is."

"Derek."

"It's a *good scene*. I'm not being — " He stopped, and started again, and got it right the second time. "I wrote that in 2021 on a Tuesday and I didn't tell anybody I'd written it, and today a man said it out loud to forty people and one of them cried in a truck at lunchtime. That's — I don't have anything for that. It's the best day I've had in nine years and I've been standing next to a dumpster for an hour."

Ha-eun looked at him.

"Good," she said. "That's allowed, that one. You're allowed that one."

She came to Alhambra at nine-thirty and stood in the middle of the kitchen with her coat on until he took it off her.

That is not a figure of speech. She stood there with her arms slightly out from her sides and waited, and he came over and undid the two buttons and took it off her shoulders and hung it on the hook, and she let him, and did not say thank you, and did not appear to think anything about it had happened.

He turned the overhead off and left the two lamps on, because she had been under eleven hundred watts since five-thirty in the morning.

He did not put anything on. She had had a man's voice in an earpiece for eleven hours and forty minutes, and he had watched her take it out at ten past four and put a hand over that ear for about nine seconds.

He took the pin out of the back of her hair, which took a moment to find, and put it on the counter where she would see it in the morning, and did not say anything about it.

Then she sat down on the floor by the cabinets.

"There's food."

"Mm."

"Do you want it on the floor or at the—"

"Floor," said Gracie.

He put the plate on the floor. She looked at it for a moment and did not pick it up, and after about nine seconds she said, "It's hot."

"It's food."

"It's *hot*, Derek."

So he took it away and put ice in it — two cubes, on the rice, at the edge, because she will not eat anything above about lukewarm when she is like this and had told him so exactly once, in February, in a car, in four words — and brought it back, and she ate it with her legs out in front of her like a child on a carpet.

She talked for a while about a woman at the airline, and then stopped in the middle of that and asked him what the thing on the shelf was, meaning the Morricone, which he had told her about twice. Then she said she was cold, and he got the blanket — the one from the yard in April, which is not the good one and is the one she wants — and she got under it and put her feet against his leg and left them there and said nothing about it.

Then, at about ten-thirty, from under the blanket, in a completely flat voice: "I want the noodles."

"You've eaten."

"I want the *noodles*."

"You've had a whole plate, Gracie."

"I *want* them," said Gracie.

He made the noodles.

He also cut two green onions into them on the diagonal, because she picks the green parts out of everything and leaves the white; and put four sticks of radish on the side, cut the short way, because she had eaten most of a plate of it at a place on Sawtelle in June and again in September and had not commented either time; and put the sriracha in in a spiral rather than a blob, because a blob has to be stirred and she does not stir things.

Then he cracked the egg in off the shell with one hand, which is a thing he can do and does not get to do in front of anybody.

She ate all of it in about four minutes without lifting her head off the cushion more than she had to, and then held the bowl up without looking at him.

"Another one."

"There isn't another one, Gracie."

"Make another one."

He made another one. He took his time about the egg.

She ate two-thirds of it, put the bowl on the floor, and said, "*Good*."

Then, after about nine seconds: "where did you get radish."

"It's been in the fridge since November."

Gracie turned her head.

"You don't eat radish," she said.

"No."

"You have never eaten a radish in your life. I've watched you eat about two hundred meals."

"No."

She looked at him for a moment longer and then put her head back down, and about four minutes later said, "the green onion was right," which was the entire review and was the best one he got that year.

---

When he got up to take the bowls away she made a noise of protest, because him getting up meant the blanket moved, and she was not interested in the bowls.

"Sit down."

"I'm putting these in the—"

"*Sit down.*"

He sat down. The bowls stayed on the floor until one-thirty in the morning.

At about eleven she got into his lap.

There was no lead-up to it and no comment about it. She got up off the floor, turned around, sat down across him with her knees over one of his legs and her face in his collarbone, and put one hand into the front of his shirt at the shoulder and shut it, and went quiet.

He put a hand on her back and left it there and did not move again.

"Are you asleep?"

"No."

"You're heavy, Gracie."

"Mm," said Gracie, and did not move, and did not get lighter.

---

At about eleven-thirty she said, into his collarbone, without lifting her head:

"Have you got a Five."

Derek did not say anything.

"It's in the book," said Gracie.

"I know it's in the book."

"Have you got one."

She did not move and she did not help him and it took him a while.

"Yes," he said.

"Once?"

"Once."

"January."

"...You've got it memorized."

"Yes."

"*Okay*," said Gracie, and put a hand flat on the side of his neck, and left it there.

She did not say thank you for telling me.

A long time later she said, "I look at your shelf."

"...What?"

"When I come in."

Derek looked at the shelf. It is nine feet from the couch and it has about two hundred steelbooks on it and it has been in alphabetical order since 2018, and there is a chapter in his own novel in which a man explains what it means when it is not.

"How long," he said.

"April."

"Every time?"

"Mm."

"I didn't say," said Gracie.

"No."

"I don't want to do it any more."

"Okay."

"Give me a number."

"Okay."

"Not *tired.*"

"Not tired. You'll get a number, Gracie."

"Again."

"You'll get a number."

"*Good*," said Gracie, and put her head back where it had been, and did not move again for a long time.
