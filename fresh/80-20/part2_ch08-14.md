## Chapter Eight: Don't Apologize for That

The restaurant was on a side street that didn't advertise itself. It had the confidence of a place that had been good for long enough that it didn't need to perform goodness. Eight tables. A laminated menu with photographs.

Lighting about function rather than atmosphere, which meant the food was always the point.

He had been coming here since before the divorce. He had not brought anyone here since Priya, and he registered this on the walk over the way you register a thing you have decided not to examine too closely.

He made it anyway.

The owner, a woman in her sixties who had never told him her name and whom he had never asked, gave him the same nod she always gave him and pointed at the usual table. He pointed back.

"Do you know what you want?" he asked.

"I'll have whatever you think I should have," Maya said, which was the answer that required the most trust and which she gave without apparent deliberation.

He ordered in what he hoped was enough Mandarin to be polite and enough humility to be honest about his limits. The owner responded in English without making it a thing.

The food arrived, three plates, more than they could finish, and she looked at it with the expression of someone prepared to be pleased.

She ate adventurously and without ceremony. She had opinions about the scallion pancakes. He had opposing opinions. The argument was brief and comfortable and settled nothing.

He lost his thread twice. This was the ADHD in its social form, the good-day version, where a word would catch and drag him down a lateral path and he would surface four sentences later in a different place than he intended. The first time it happened he followed the thread and let her follow with him. The second time he caught himself and said, "Sorry — I went sideways," and started to redirect.

"Don't apologize for that," she said.

He stopped. The redirect evaporated.

"My cousin has ADHD," she said. She was eating, still, not making it a moment. "He's thirty-two. He's spent his whole life apologizing for the way his brain moves. His parents, his teachers, everyone treated it like a failure of effort rather than a different kind of wiring. And I watched that happen and I always thought..." She set down her chopsticks. "It's not a thing to apologize for. It's just how you go. And the lateral things you go to are usually interesting."

He was quiet for a moment. Not the passive quiet of the cold days.

The active quiet of someone receiving something they needed to receive carefully.

"Most people find it exhausting," he said. "Eventually."

"I'm not most people." She said it without arrogance, just as information. "And I've noticed you always come back to where we were. You don't actually lose the thread. You just take it somewhere else for a minute."

He looked at her. He did not say: that is the most accurate description of how I move through conversation that anyone has ever given me. He did not say: Priya used to wait for me to come back with the slight visible patience of someone performing patience, and the visibility of the performance was the thing that made me understand, slowly and then all at once, that I was someone to be patient with rather than someone to be with.

He said: "The dumpling situation is better than last time and last time was already good."

They talked about the food and then about her grandmother's cooking and then about whether the best food is always the food made by someone cooking for love rather than for impression, which led without strain to a conversation about whether all art is better when made for love, which led back to Shuggie Otis recording alone in his bedroom. The same place they had been that morning but approached from a different angle.

He was not watching himself. He was just there.

Her phone went off.

She glanced at it and said: "I'm sorry, one second, it's my manager."

She was already standing.

He said of course. She moved toward the back of the restaurant and he topped up his tea and waited.

She came back two minutes later. "Sorry, sorry. Where were we."

"Food made for love," he said.

"Right." She sat down and then paused with a small wince. "It always takes me a few minutes to get out of work mode. Like I can still feel myself in it after I hang up."

She said it the way you mention a known thing about yourself. Just a fact, lightly held.

"Don't apologize for that," he said.

She smiled and picked up her chopsticks and they went back to the food.

He did not say anything about it. They talked through the rest of lunch, her grandmother and the particular authority of food cooked for people you knew, and the conversation was good and he was in it, fully in it, and at some point the beat was just a beat and the afternoon was the afternoon again.

He filed it in the place he kept things that were real but not yet ready.

It took up almost no space.

On the sidewalk the afternoon was gold in the Silver Lake way and she had the bag with the two records under her arm and he had his hands in his jacket pockets.

"The scallion pancake argument is not over," she said.

"The scallion pancake argument will never be over. That's why it's good."

She smiled. The real one. Fully the real one. "Thank you for today. For all of this."

"Thank you for coming here. You didn't have to."

"I wanted to," she said. The same register she had used about the Shuggie Otis. Just: I wanted to. The truest possible reason.

He watched her walk to her car. He was warm today, properly warm, and he thought: this is a good day. He thought it without anticipating the loss of it.


## Chapter Nine: He Waits

She got on the 101 at four-fifteen and drove north with the traffic already thickening with the Lost Highway argument still in her head. She had been wrong about one thing and she knew she was wrong, and the knowing was not uncomfortable. This was something she had noticed: being wrong around him did not produce the instinctive self-protective recalibration it sometimes produced around other people.

She called Jess at four-thirty, the traffic having slowed to the pace of a philosophical negotiation.

"How was it," Jess said.

"Good. Really good, actually."

"Tell me."

"We went to a record store. He found this Morricone collection he'd been looking for and he held it against his chest. Like it was something he didn't want to put down. And then he told me about it and everything about him changed. The way he stood. He was completely present in a way that I don't see in people that often, where something is happening and they're not also performing the thing happening."

"He sounds like a lot," Jess said. Not unkindly.

"He is a lot. But it's not the kind of a lot that wants something from you. It's just the kind that's actually there. Most people I spend time with, I can feel them monitoring the interaction. Checking how it's landing.

Adjusting. But with him it's like the monitoring lag is just gone. He says the thing and then he waits to see what you do with it."

"He waits," Jess said.

"He waits."

A silence. The traffic moved two car lengths.

"Maya," Jess said.

"I know."

"I'm just noting that in the four years I've known you, you have described three men to me in detail. One of them you dated for seven months and spent the whole time managing the public angle. One of them you almost dated and then his team reached out about a collaboration opportunity and you realized that was the actual point. And this one you described by saying he waits."

She watched the brake lights cascade ahead in the golden traffic. "He told me not to apologize for the ADHD thing. He started to apologize for losing his thread and I said don't apologize for that and he went quiet in a way that meant something landed."

"What landed?"

"The quiet was the receiving kind, not the shutting-down kind. He has this history. He doesn't talk about it directly but it's in the shape of things.

His family. And I think there was a marriage, it didn't end well, and I think whatever happened told him that being fully himself was a problem to be managed. And I watch him remember that and then forget it and when he forgets it he's just entirely there."

"Are you?" Jess asked. "Just there. Around him."

"More than usual," she said.

She got home at six. She ordered from the Taiwanese place and ate at the kitchen island and texted him a photo of the bag.

He responded: I hope you got the scallion pancakes.

She responded: obviously.

She put the Shuggie Otis on, streaming service not a record player, not the same thing, but the music was the music. She sat on her couch and listened.

He had been right. She could hear the room. The bedroom. The three years of making something in private, the isolation in the sound, the beauty that had grown in the dark. She listened and thought about her own private things: the videos she recorded and never posted. The folder she called drafts that she hadn't opened in three months.

She texted him at nine: I listened to the Shuggie Otis.

He responded: And?

She typed: You were right about the room. You can hear him being alone in it. But it doesn't feel sad, exactly. It feels like the aloneness was necessary.

A pause. Then: Yes. That's exactly it. The aloneness was the condition, not the problem.

She looked at this for a moment. Something in it sat strangely, not wrong, just slightly more present than she'd expected a text to be.

> *Maya: I built my apartment like that I think*

> *Maya: like it was designed not to need anyone in it*

A pause. She sent it and then sat looking at it, not entirely sure what she'd meant.

> *Derek: that's a good apartment to have*

> *Derek: and also kind of a sad one*

> *Maya: yeah*

> *Maya: both*

> *Derek: goodnight*

> *Maya: goodnight*

She sat for a while after she put the phone down. The music ended and she let it end without starting it again. She thought about getting up to make tea. She didn't.

She sat in the quiet and felt the thing she was feeling, which was large enough to require a name but which she had not yet given one. The drafts folder. The videos recorded at two in the morning and never posted, the ones where she had said something true and then watched it back and understood that the saying-true was the whole of it — that posting would not improve it, that some things were worth less the moment they were given an audience. She had not understood this about herself until recently.

She was still understanding it.

She opened her phone. The draft recording app was two taps away.

She knew, somewhere in the operational part of her brain that had been running her career for seven years, that what she was feeling right now was content. She could feel the architecture already assembling.

She put the phone face-down.

Not yet. Not this one.

She sat with it, the unprocessed thing, the actual thing still warm in the room, and after a while she thought: I am not going to make this into a video. Then she went to bed, and in the morning she was not sure she had made the right choice and she was also not sure it had been a choice exactly, more like a staying, a not-moving. She was still thinking about what that meant on the drive to the brand meeting she had at nine.


## Chapter Ten: Are You Being Careful

Rajan got to the restaurant early, which almost never happened. He sat with his phone face-down and ordered a beer and thought about the drive over, which had been the usual 405 negotiation, forty minutes of his life he would not get back. Preethi's mother had been in the guest room for eleven days.

The guest room was eight feet from the kitchen and Rajan had developed, in eleven days, a detailed fantasy about a hotel in Pasadena that he was not going to act on but that he returned to the way you return to a book you are not quite ready to finish.

Derek came in the way Derek always came in, which was to say he took in the room before he took in the table. A habit from teaching, probably, or from the streaming — scanning for the live wire, the thing worth following. He found Rajan, registered the beer, and said: "Long week?"

"Preethi's mother," Rajan said, in the tone that contained everything.

Derek sat. He ordered. He asked two follow-up questions about the guest room situation, which were the right two questions, and Rajan answered them, and for ten minutes they were just two men eating and talking about the ordinary difficulty of other people's mothers, and this was one of the things Rajan had valued about Derek for fifteen years: that he could be genuinely present for someone else's problem without making the presence a performance. He was actually curious about the guest room. He actually wanted to know.

Then the food was mostly gone and Rajan refilled his water and said: "Okay. Tell me about the woman."

Derek told it. Without the podcast voice — Rajan had heard the podcast voice enough times to recognize when it was absent, which was the tell, the sign that something was actual. Just the facts, laid out in sequence.

The summit. The balcony. The Robocop conversation. The Silver Lake Saturday. The phone call that had gone until nearly three in the morning, which Derek mentioned with the slight flatness of someone reporting a flight time, and which landed in Rajan's chest like a stone into water.

He ate and listened and did not show what the landing did, because showing it would change what Derek told him, and he needed Derek to tell him the whole thing.

It was the voice. That was what got him. Not the content — the content was what it was, a woman Derek had met, they liked each other, it was early — but the way Derek's voice moved when he talked about her.

There was a quality to it that Rajan had not heard in two years. Longer, maybe. A quality that he had heard before Priya, in the early days before Derek started making adjustments, before the adjustments became the shape of things.

He had watched that happen. He had watched Derek's voice when he talked about Priya in the beginning, warm and certain, and then watched it change, slowly and then faster, the certainty becoming careful, the warmth becoming managed. He had not said anything because it had not been the right time and then there was never a right time and then Priya was gone and Derek came out the other side smaller than he had gone in.

He had not said anything. That was the fact he lived with.

Derek finished. He drank his water. He waited with the particular patience of someone who has said a true thing and is willing to sit with it.

"She sounds like a lot of person," Rajan said.

"She is. In the good way."

"There's a good way?"

"When it's not directed at you as a problem to solve, yeah."

Rajan looked at him. Derek was holding his chopsticks loosely in one hand, not eating, just holding them the way you hold something when you have forgotten you are holding it. He was looking at the table but seeing something else. Rajan had not seen this particular version of Derek's face in a very long time, and seeing it now produced in him a feeling that was equal parts relief and fear, and the fear was louder.

Because here was the thing he understood about Derek, had understood since they were twenty-three and figuring out how to be people: Derek did not do this partway. When Derek decided someone was worth going toward he went all the way, full weight, no hedging. It was one of the best things about him. It was also the thing that made the gaps so bad. The further in he went before he found the gap, the worse the landing. And the voice Rajan was hearing right now, the quality of it, the slight openness of it, told him Derek was already further in than he had probably admitted to himself.

"Are you being careful?" Rajan said.

"I'm always careful."

"That's not what I asked."

Derek set down his chopsticks. Outside, Koreatown was doing its evening thing: the signs in two languages, the density of the street, the smell of the barbecue places drifting down the block.

"I know it's not what you asked," he said.

Rajan turned his water glass in his hands. He thought about how to say the thing accurately. He had learned, with Derek specifically, that imprecision was worse than silence — Derek would take an imprecise concern and argue it on the imprecise terms and they would end up debating the wrong thing entirely.

"I'm not worried about who she is," he said. "I want to be clear about that. The subscribers, the industry thing, whatever. I don't care about any of that." He put the glass down. "What I'm worried about is that she seems real to you. And the last time someone seemed real to you, you spent five years making yourself smaller and called it accommodation."

"She's not Priya."

"I know she's not Priya." He said it with the edge that meant: stop defending against the argument I'm not making. "Priya was easy to read once you knew what to look for. This one — from what you're describing — she's harder. She has the language. She knows what the real thing looks like. And that's what worries me. The closer the version is to the thing, the longer it takes you to find the gap." He stopped. "And you don't come back the same from those gaps."

Derek looked at the table.

"I watched you after," Rajan said. Not unkindly. Just the thing itself, the one he had not said when he should have. "Not just the immediate after.

The two years after. I watched you get smaller and then I watched you stop expecting to be known and call it realistic. And I think you've been okay.

Genuinely okay. But okay is not—" He gestured at Derek across the table.

"It's not this. You don't look like this when you're okay."

"I don't know what that means."

"I know you don't." And that, specifically, was the thing that made Rajan's chest do what it was doing. Because the Derek who didn't know what that meant was the Derek from before, the one who had not yet learned to monitor himself out of a room. "That's actually the thing that worries me least."

A delivery truck outside was attempting the block, the driver on the horn with the resignation of someone who had accepted the futility and was doing it anyway.

"Does she know about the depression?" Rajan said.

A pause. "She knows some. The levels thing came up a little. She handled it right."

"What does right mean?"

Derek was quiet for a moment. He had the expression he got when he was locating a precise thing. "She didn't panic and she didn't perform not- panicking. She just stayed. In the way that was actually useful rather than the way that was useful for her to feel like she'd done something."

Rajan looked at him. He sat with that for a moment.

"That's different," he said.

"Yeah."

He picked his chopsticks back up. The fear was still there, would probably stay there until he had more information, which was how fear worked when it was the legitimate kind. But under it something else had shifted, something he did not have a clean word for. He had come here expecting to have the careful conversation. He was still going to have it. But the thing Derek had just described — the staying, the not performing the not-panicking — was not nothing. That was a specific and rare thing. He had been married to Preethi for nine years and he knew what it looked like when someone stayed in the right way.

"Then be careful in the way that's actually careful," he said. "Not the way where you preemptively decide it won't work so you don't have to risk finding out."

Derek looked at him.

"I know you," Rajan said. "I've known you since before Priya. I know what you looked like before you started editing yourself for her and what you looked like after. And I know what you look like right now, talking about this woman, and it's the first thing. So be careful in the real way."

"I'm trying," Derek said.

"I know." That was also true. Derek was trying in a way that meant something, the kind of trying that required courage rather than just effort.

"That's why I'm not more worried."

He refilled his beer. He said: "Tell Preethi's mother I said the guest room situation is unreasonable."

"I'm not getting involved in that."

"I know," Rajan said. "Nobody ever does."

He watched Derek leave. He sat for a moment with the last of his beer and the noise of the street and the specific weight of a thing said too late once and not too late now. He thought about Preethi, nine years, the ways she had stayed when staying was hard, the ways he had stayed. He thought about Derek's voice when he talked about the phone call that had gone until nearly three.

He paid the bill. He drove home through the familiar Koreatown streets and then the freeway and then his own neighborhood, the guest room light still on when he pulled into the driveway, and he sat in the car for a moment before going inside.

He was still worried. He was going to stay worried until he had a reason not to be. But under the worry something else was present, something quieter, and he recognized it after a moment as the particular quality of hope that arrives when you have been waiting for something to happen to someone you love and you hear, in their voice, that it might be happening.

He got out of the car. He went inside.

* * *

The bad week arrived without announcement on a Sunday, nine days after Silver Lake.

He woke and something was wrong with the day before he was fully awake. Not a feeling exactly, more the absence of the quality that mornings usually had, some low-grade warmth that was just the body being willing to be in the day. It was gone. The room was the same room. He registered this with the flat inventory of someone who had done this many times: the quality of the ceiling, the quality of the light, the quality of his own interior, which was like a room with all the furniture still present and all the warmth gone out of it.

He got up. He showered. These were the minimum requirements and he met them because he had made a private agreement with himself years ago, after the winter three years back that he did not discuss, that the minimum requirements were not optional. You shower. You eat something.

You go where you are supposed to go. The rest was negotiable, but the minimum requirements were the floor and the floor held.

He went to school. He taught. From the outside he was fine, was Derek, or a version of Derek that passed in the fluorescent light of a Monday morning. His students did not know. One of them, a junior named Adesola who had strong opinions about everything and whom he genuinely liked, said something sharp about the passage they were discussing and he responded with the right question and she ran with it, and from the inside he watched this happen from a small cold distance, as if through a window he could see clearly through but not open.

He did not text Maya that day. Not a decision. The decision-making machinery was running at reduced capacity, which was itself a symptom.

He was hungry at lunch and ate half of what he had brought. He drove home and sat on the couch.

The essays were on the desk. Thirty-two of them, due back Thursday.

He had opened the folder when he got home and read the first sentence of the top essay three times and put the folder down and gone to the couch and the couch was where he was now.

The television was off. He could see himself in the dark screen of it. A man on a couch, still, in a lamp-lit room full of carefully chosen things. He looked like someone waiting. He was not waiting for anything. The light outside moved from the late-afternoon quality to the early-evening quality and he watched this change with the mild interest of someone watching weather through a window they cannot open.

His stomach made a sound. He thought: I should eat something. The thought arrived complete and correct. He did not move. The kitchen was twelve feet away. He knew this. The knowing produced nothing.

At some point he picked up his phone. Maya had sent a photo of the Shuggie Otis record sleeve. She had apparently ordered a physical copy. It had arrived with no caption. Just the photo. He looked at it. He did not respond. He set the phone face-down on the cushion beside him. Not cruelty. Not a decision. Just the severed signal between the wanting-to and the doing, and the wanting-to was thin anyway, thin and far away somewhere he could not currently reach.

He went to bed at nine. He had not eaten dinner. He had not opened the essays. He lay in the dark and knew this would not last forever, that it had not lasted forever before, that he had a track record of returning that was by any actuarial standard reliable.

He held onto that the way you hold a railing in the dark.

In the morning it was still the same. He texted Maya before he lost the nerve: Sorry for the quiet. Rough few days. I'm okay, just under. She responded: I know. The photo was too much input when you're under. I won't do that again.

He read that twice. She had noticed the no-response and correctly diagnosed it as a symptom rather than a slight and adjusted her behavior and told him she was adjusting without making it about herself or requiring him to manage her feelings about it.

He typed: It wasn't a bad idea. It was a good idea at a bad time.

There's a difference.

> *Maya: okay*

> *Maya: is there anything useful I can do from here*

> *Derek: no. I just have to wait it out*

> *Maya: okay. I'm here*

> *Derek: I know*

> *Derek: that helps more than you know*

He put the phone in his pocket and went to school and came home and opened the essays and graded six of them, which was not thirty-two but was six more than zero, which was the math available to him. He ate the dal in the refrigerator. It tasted like the memory of itself but it was food and he ate it.

He was waiting. He had done this before. The railing held.


## Chapter Eleven: The Dark

The Dark The cold had mostly lifted by the following Saturday. He had come back through the week the way he always came back, incrementally, the grading finished by Wednesday, the essays returned Thursday with comments that were probably a little better than usual, the over- compensation of a man who had lost a few days to it and was making it up in the margins.

He had picked the film and she had agreed without asking what it was, which he had taken seriously as an act of trust, because the unasked question implied confidence in his judgment and confidence in his judgment implied something. He was trying not to over-read. He was also not going to pretend he hadn't noticed.

The cinema in Los Feliz was the kind that showed things in the order they deserved rather than the order studios preferred, which meant on any given weekend you might find a Tati retrospective sharing a marquee with something from Ozu. Derek had been coming here since he moved to Los Angeles and he had a particular feeling about the place that was adjacent to love in the way you can love something that has never once let you down.

Maya arrived in the same hat-and-sunglasses configuration as Silver Lake and they stood in the small lobby and he handed her a ticket and she looked at the title on it.

"Chungking Express," she said.

"1994. Wong Kar-wai."

"I've seen it."

"I know. You told me. That's why I picked it."

She looked at him. "Because you wanted to watch it with someone who already knew it."

"Because I wanted to watch it with you knowing it. It's different the second time. Third, in your case, you said."

"Third," she confirmed. Something in her face that was not quite a smile but was in its neighborhood. "Okay."

They found seats toward the back, where the angle was right and the sound was cleanest. The theater was two-thirds full, which for a Saturday showing of a thirty-year-old film was a good number, and the audience had the quality that audiences for this kind of film always had: self-selected, attentive, there on purpose.

The lights went down.

In the dark they were not performing anything. This was the thing about cinemas that Derek had valued his whole life and that he had sometimes struggled to explain to people who didn't already feel it: the dark removed the social calculus. You didn't have to manage your face. You could just be in the thing, and whatever the thing did to you happened in private, and the person next to you was having their own private experience, and the shared privacy of it was its own kind of intimacy, more honest in some ways than looking at each other, because you were both looking at something true.

He watched Chungking Express and felt it the way he always felt it.

Faye Wong singing California Dreamin' at full volume in the lunch counter, the camera's restless anxious closeness, Tony Leung's cop falling in love with a woman who is already redecorating his apartment, the whole film asking its question in the gentlest possible way: how do you reach someone who is moving? How do you love something in motion?

Beside him, in the dark, Maya was still.

He was aware of her the way you are aware of someone in a theater when the film is doing something, peripherally, the sense of another person's presence registering alongside the screen. At the scene where Tony Leung's character realizes she has already left, finds the ticket stub, understands the whole of it too late, he heard her exhale, a quiet sound, involuntary, the sound of a person receiving something they weren't braced for.

He did not look at her. He watched the film.

Afterward they walked west on Vermont into the evening, which had turned cool and purple in the way of Los Angeles evenings when the day has been warm and the temperature drops cleanly, and they walked without a destination, just the sidewalk and the ordinary noise of a neighborhood at night.

"The cop who loves her," Maya said. "He doesn't actually know her."

"He knows the version of her she let him see."

"Which he confuses for her."

"Right."

"And she knows that's what he's doing," Maya said. "The whole time.

She knows he's in love with something she constructed. And she doesn't correct it. Why? Because it's easier? Because she doesn't know how to offer the real version?"

"I think she doesn't know if the real version is worth offering," Derek said. "I think she's spent long enough being the constructed version that she's not sure there's enough of the other one left to give someone."

Maya was quiet for half a block.

"That's the saddest reading," she said.

"I know."

"Is it the right one?"

He thought about it. The city moved around them: a restaurant letting out, a couple with a dog, the distant freeway. "I think it's one of them. I think the film allows you to hold more than one. Maybe she also just isn't ready. Maybe the real version is there but the timing is wrong or she hasn't met the person who makes her believe it's safe to put it down."

"And then she does," Maya said. "In the end."

"In the end she does."

They stopped at a corner, waiting for the light. He was aware of the smallness of the distance between them, the ordinary proximal closeness of two people on a sidewalk that was, in this instance, not ordinary.

"I think I've been her," Maya said. "The constructed version for so long that I forgot to maintain the other one."

"You haven't forgotten," he said. "It's right there. I can see it."

She turned and looked at him. The light changed and neither of them moved for a moment.

"How?" she said. Not rhetorical. Actually asking.

"Because you told me about the aloneness at nine o'clock on a Saturday without filtering it first. Because you said don't apologize for that and meant it in the direction of yourself too. I could hear it. Because you bought the Shuggie Otis and didn't post about it." He paused. "The real version is very present. She's just not used to having someone look directly at her."

Something moved in Maya's face. The small recalibration, the receiving, and then the steadying.

"We should cross," she said. The light was still green.

"Yeah," he said.

They crossed. They walked another block and then stopped at the natural end of the walkable distance and turned back toward the cars.

On the sidewalk in front of his car there was a moment. He was aware of it arriving the way you are aware of weather, the change in pressure, the quality of the air before the thing itself. She was looking at him and he was looking at her and the moment was there between them with its particular weight and its particular invitation.

He did not reach for her.

She did not reach for him.

Not yet was the language of it. Not because either of them was afraid, exactly, but because the thing between them had been building with a kind of care that neither wanted to interrupt by moving too quickly, and they both understood this without saying it, and the understanding itself was a form of intimacy.

"Next time," he said. Not a question.

"Next time," she confirmed. Not an answer, or the only answer.

He drove home. He put the Coltrane on. He sat in his good chair and listened to the whole side and he did not try to do anything with the listening except listen, which was itself a kind of discipline, on evenings when there was a lot to turn over.

He thought about the cop who loved the woman who was already leaving. About the way the film held that without sentimentality, the perpetual slightly-missed quality of longing, the way desire always arrives a beat before or after the thing it is reaching for. He had loved that film for fifteen years and taught it twice and talked about it on the podcast and it had never, before tonight, landed quite so close to the place where he was actually living.

She had said: I think I've been her. The constructed version for so long I forgot to maintain the other one.

And he had said: You haven't forgotten. I can see it. And he had meant it completely. He had meant every specific piece of the evidence he offered: the aloneness texted at nine o'clock, the don't-apologize, the Shuggie Otis not posted. And he had also been aware, somewhere beneath the meaning it, that he was doing what he always did, which was follow the thread, find the true thing, hold it up in the light. He did this with films. He did this with students. He did it instinctively and it was one of the real things about him and it was also, sometimes, a way of staying at the level of the true-thing- about-someone rather than the harder and less articulable question of what to do with it.

She was standing on a corner in Los Feliz and the light had changed and she had asked him: how, and he had told her, and the telling was accurate and the accuracy mattered and it was also not the same as whatever came next. Whatever came next was going to be less articulable than the evidence. It was going to require something from him that the podcast version of himself, the thread-following version, the version that could find the live wire in any room, was not going to be able to provide from a distance.

He thought: I don't want to miss by a beat.

He thought: I don't think I will.

He thought, for a moment, about what it would mean to be wrong about that. The specific shape of being wrong about that, given everything.

Priya was in the room in the way she was always in the room when he was here: not painfully, just present, the way a room holds its history. He sat with her there for a moment, without flinching from it, which was the thing he was trying to get better at. The room could hold her and still be his. Both things were true.

He went to bed and slept without the usual negotiation, deeply and without interruption, and in the morning he was warm.


## Chapter Twelve: The Metabolizing

The Metabolizing She filmed it on a Tuesday, three days after the cinema.

She had not planned to film it. She had been sitting in the stream room — not to stream, just because the chair was the right height and the room was the right temperature. She did some of her best thinking there when it was offline. The camera dark. Nothing watching. She had been thinking about what he said on the sidewalk. The real version is very present. She's just not used to having someone look directly at her.

Three days, and it was still moving. The way thoughts move when they've found something true to attach to. Not anxiously. With weight.

And then the camera was on.

She didn't decide. Or she didn't feel the deciding. One moment the room was dark and quiet and the next the red light was on and she was talking and the talking had the shape it got when she was saying something real, the specific rhythm of it, and she let it go where it went.

She talked for twenty-two minutes. She didn't talk about him — not by name, not by implication, not in any way that would identify him or them.

She talked about the broader thing, which was real, which had been real long before she met him: the experience of being visible to millions of people and genuinely, privately lonely in the specific way that is only possible when the visibility is itself the thing keeping you from actual connection. How the performance of openness becomes, after long enough, the substitute for it. How you can say true things publicly and still be withholding everything that matters.

She talked about paying attention. About what it felt like to be with someone who was paying attention not to the version of you they had decided on but to what you were actually doing in real time. About how unfamiliar that was. About what the unfamiliarity said about the texture of her daily life.

She didn't say his name. She didn't need to.

* * *

She was in the kitchen when she became aware that the video was edited.

Not finishing editing. Finished. Fourteen minutes. She had watched it back at some point — she must have — and it was good. She knew it was good the way she always knew: the craft and the content, the real thing inside the made thing. She was standing at the kitchen counter with a glass of water and the timeline already had the shape of a done thing.

She sat down at the island. She thought: I was going to call him.

That had been the plan. His voice first, then the deciding. She held onto that — the intention of it, the order she had meant to follow.

Somewhere between the plan and the kitchen it hadn't changed exactly.

Just continued past her.

She opened the laptop. The file was there, labeled and ready. The thumbnail she had chosen was good. She looked at it for a while.

She thought about calling him. She picked up her phone, set it back down. She thought: I'll call when I know what I want to do with it. That was reasonable. That was just being careful.

She made more tea. She went to bed. The file sat in the laptop.

On Thursday she posted it.

The scheduled notification came through while she was in the kitchen.

Then the views. Then the comments arriving in the warm recognizing way they did when she said something people had been waiting to hear said.

Two million within six hours. Four million by the next day. I didn't know how to explain this and now I do. I feel so seen right now. Maya Chen is the only person on the internet who tells the truth.

She read them. Genuine and warm and directed at the video-Maya, the one who had processed the experience into something receivable. She sat with that in the small distant way of things she wasn't quite reaching.

She texted Derek that afternoon: I posted a new video today. Thought you might want to watch it.

She kept it light. Kept the door open. She didn't say: this is about you, this is about us, I filmed something and then it was posted and I'm not entirely sure of the geography between those two events. Just: here it is.

He responded three hours later: I saw it.

That was all.

She waited for more. The three dots appeared and disappeared twice.

Then nothing.

She put the phone down. She thought: he's fine, he's just tired, it doesn't mean anything. She thought it with the particular efficiency of someone who has had practice not looking at a thing directly. She went to make dinner and the thought receded and she let it.

In the morning the apartment was clean. The counters wiped, the island cleared, everything returned to its correct position on the shelf. The grandmother's teapot gleamed.

She made her tea and did not think about this.

* * *

His texts stayed short for the next four days. Not cold, not withdrawn, just reduced. The lateral reach was gone, the thread-following, the three- message things where one thought opened into another and he followed it wherever it went. In its place: answers. Complete and warm and finished.

She would say something and he would respond and the response would close the exchange rather than continue it.

She noticed. She was always noticing. The question was what she did with the noticing.

She told herself it was the glass. She had learned the shape of his bad weeks — she knew the signs, and this looked like that, close enough to that.

So she gave him space the way he'd asked. Small things: a photo, a podcast clip. Nothing that required anything back. She was considerate. Thoughtful.

Doing exactly what he had asked.

She did not look at the other explanation.

Eight days after the video she was on his couch on a Sunday and they were watching something and she was aware, in the way she was always aware, of the quality of his presence beside her. He was there. He was fine.

Not a bad week; the fullness of him was back, the temperature was right.

He was watching the film.

She said: "I should have told you before I posted it."

He didn't look at her. He was quiet for a moment, the film still running.

"The video," she said. "I knew it was something that touched us before it was the video's. And I didn't tell you."

The film ran. A minute passed.

"Yeah," he said. Just that.

She waited. He was looking at the screen. She could feel him deciding something.

"I kept the file for two days," she said. "I told myself I was deciding.

But I think — " She paused. The accurate version was harder than she expected. "I think the deciding happened without me. And then it was done, and I didn't know how to go back to before it was done and have the conversation I should have had first."

He was quiet for a long time. The film continued. She watched his face in the light of it.

"I noticed the not-calling," he said finally. "I noticed that you figured out how to tell me without having to actually tell me. And I filed it." He said it without accusation but with the precision of someone who has been precise about their own patterns long enough to recognize them in someone else. "I shouldn't have filed it. I should have said something when it happened."

"I should have called," she said.

"Yeah."

They sat with that. The film was still running. Neither of them were watching it.

"Are we okay?" she said.

He turned and looked at her. The brief internal calibration, and then: "We're okay. We're also in the middle of something that isn't finished. Both of those things."

Not a reassurance. An accurate report.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay," he said.

He reached over and turned the volume back up. She sat with the not- finished thing and didn't try to process it and didn't reach for her phone and let the film run. The cost was this: the sitting with the not-finished, the thing between them that she had put there in a moment she hadn't quite been present for, and that was going to require something from her she hadn't yet figured out how to give.

She didn't look away.


## Chapter Thirteen: Access

Access He met Jess on a Friday night at a restaurant in West Hollywood that Derek would not have chosen for himself but that he understood upon walking in, chosen by people who knew this neighborhood, who moved through it with the fluency of belonging that you either have or you are performing. He had dressed carefully and felt he had mostly gotten it right, which was its own small negotiation.

Jess was exactly what he had expected: quick, affectionate, with the kind of humor that surprised you and landed clean. She had dark eyes that missed very little and a way of making space in a conversation that suggested she had been doing it professionally for long enough that it had become simply how she moved. She liked him. He could tell within the first ten minutes and he was glad of it, not because her approval was necessary but because it made the dinner easier, and the dinner was already carrying some weight.

Maya was different at dinner with them. Not worse, not more performed, not more guarded, just differently calibrated, the way anyone is around people who have known them a long time. She was more shorthand.

More reference-dense. They had seven years of shared language and she moved through it with ease, and Derek followed where he could and noted where he could not without making anything of it.

He was good at the dinner. This was not false modesty in reverse. He was genuinely good at it, made Jess laugh with the real laugh more than once. He talked about the Coltrane and about a student who had written an essay that week about The Great Gatsby that had the quality of a person who had just understood something for the first time and put it on paper while it was still warm, and the table leaned in, and the dinner was good.

And underneath the dinner, so quiet he almost did not notice it, a question was forming.

He noticed it on the drive home, alone, the freeway doing its late- Friday thing. The question was not about Maya or about the dinner but about the geometry of it, the way her world had a size and a density that his did not, the way Jess was a node in a network that extended outward in ways he had not mapped and could not fully map, the way the restaurant and the neighborhood and the table were all part of a life that had been built at a scale he was observing from the outside.

He had been let into a layer of it. The layer was genuine. He did not doubt that, had no reason to. But there was a difference between being let into a layer and being part of the architecture, and he was starting to understand that the difference mattered, and that he was going to have to figure out which one he was before the not-knowing became a problem.

He was not Priya-worried. He was precise about this, running the diagnostic with the care of someone who knows their own patterns well enough to distinguish between a pattern and a real thing. Priya had managed him. Maya was doing the opposite of managing. Receiving him, pushing back on him, holding him to the yes. The shape was different.

But the question remained. Not about trust. About belonging. About the geometry of it, the size of her world and the smallness of the seam where he existed in it, and whether the seam was narrow because of her or because of him.

That last part was the thing he kept returning to on the drive home.

He had set the terms. He had said, early and clearly: I am not content, I am a person, keep me out of the frame. And she had respected that. She had been careful with it, more careful than he had any right to expect. So if the carefulness had produced a kind of distance he could feel but not name, if living outside the frame meant living at a slight remove from the life the frame documented, then wasn't that his own geometry? His own choice, producing its own consequence?

He had wanted the wall. He had asked for it specifically. So if the wall produced a kind of distance he could feel at the edges of things. That was his own architecture, wasn't it. His own choice.

He wasn't sure. He sat with the not-being-sure and eventually the Morricone ended and he went to bed.

He texted her: Good dinner. Your people are good.

She responded in a minute: She texted me from the bathroom during dessert. I am not telling you what she said.

> *Maya: I really do not*

> *Derek: Maya*

> *Maya: she said and I quote "okay he is the real thing"*

> *Maya: which from Jess is essentially a Nobel Prize*

> *Derek: I like Jess*

He put the phone down. The Morricone played on. The question was still there, quiet and patient, not urgent yet. Just a thing that was real and that he was going to have to bring into the light eventually, and that could wait for a little while longer, while the music played and the apartment was warm and the record turned.


## Chapter Fourteen: Not Content

Not Content The photo appeared on a Thursday morning, three weeks after the cinema. She saw it at seven-fifteen, before she was fully awake, which was the worst possible time. Her defenses were down, her mind still assembling itself from sleep, and the image arrived before she had the tools to receive it correctly.

It was blurry. Taken from across the street with a long lens, the quality of the image suggesting someone with patience and no particular concern for the people in it. It showed the sidewalk outside the Los Feliz cinema, and two figures she recognized immediately, and the caption said: maya chen spotted with mystery man after private film screening.

The comments below it had theories. Twelve hundred of them in the two hours since it had been posted, which meant it had been picked up somewhere larger and distributed outward the way these things distributed.

Some of the comments were warmly speculative. Some were the other kind.

She put the phone face-down and lay in bed for a moment, looking at the ceiling of her bedroom, which was the color of early morning and asked nothing of her.

She had known this was a possibility. She had been managing her own visibility for seven years and had developed the particular pragmatism of someone who understands that their life has a public dimension that exists whether they tend it or not. She had known, from the first time they were in public together, that the possibility existed. She had told herself she would deal with it when it arrived.

It had arrived.

She picked up the phone and called him before she had made a plan, which was unusual for her and felt correct anyway.

He picked up on the third ring. She heard the ambient silence of his apartment in the background and was briefly glad for the familiarity of it.

"Hey," she said. "Before you see something online, I want to tell you about it."

A beat. "Okay."

She told him. The photo, the site, the caption, the comments. She kept it factual, which was the right register, not performing calm, not performing distress, just telling him what had happened the way you tell someone what has happened when you have decided to be the person who tells them directly.

When she finished he was quiet for a moment.

"Are you okay?" he said.

She had expected: what do we do, or: does this change anything, or even just the silence of someone processing an unwelcome development.

She had not expected: are you okay. She sat with that.

"I am okay," she said. "A little — yeah, I am okay. Are you?"

"I am trying to figure out how I feel about it," he said. Honest rather than reassuring. She appreciated it.

"You can feel whatever you feel. I am not asking you to manage it."

"I know." A pause. "I think what I feel is — " He stopped. Started again. "I am not part of any story you have to tell. That is what I want you to know."

She was quiet.

"I mean it in both directions," he said. "I am not asking you to protect me. I am not asking you to explain me or contextualize me or make me make sense in the context of your public life. I am not content. I am a person. And I need you to know that I know the difference, even if no one else does."

The sentence arrived and she received it with the full attention it required.

I am not content. Not angry, not threatened, not issuing ultimatums.

Just: naming a thing clearly, in the same register he used for everything true and difficult, the flat honest voice that had no performance in it, that said the thing and then stopped and let the thing be what it was.

"I know," she said. "I know you are not."

"Do you?" he said. Not accusatory. Just asking.

She thought about the video. About the yes. About the practiced efficiency with which her life processed real things into shareable ones.

About the fourteen minutes and the four million comments and what it had cost to look at what she had done and not look away.

"I am learning to know it," she said. "I think that is the honest answer."

He was quiet for a moment. Then, softer: "That is the right answer. That is better than yes."

"I am not going to confirm or deny it," she said. "The photo. I am just going to let it be what it is. A blurry picture of two people leaving a cinema."

"Okay."

"Is that okay with you?"

"It is your call to make," he said. "That part of your life is not mine to manage. I just needed to say the other part."

"I heard the other part."

"Good."

They talked for a few more minutes, and then he had to get ready for school and she had to begin the process of deciding how to move through a day in which a version of her private life had entered the public one without her permission. This was not the first time this had happened. It was the first time it had happened to something she was actually trying to protect.

In the afternoon she sat in the stream room in the offline chair with the ring light still off and thought about what he had said.

I am not content.

She had been in this industry long enough to know how rare that kind of sentence was. Not the sentiment; lots of people said they wanted to be treated as a person. What was rare was the register. The absence of demand in it. The absence of grievance. He had not said: you need to protect me, or: don't do this to me, or: choose. He had said: I am not content. Present tense, declarative, a fact about himself offered without a corresponding request. She had received it and said the right things and meant them, and the call had ended and she had sat on her couch and thought: I meant them and they were also the right things to say, and I cannot tell anymore whether those are always two separate things or whether they have become, after enough practice, the same thing.

She texted him: I heard the other part. I am going to keep hearing it.

He responded twenty minutes later: Good. Now stop texting me, I am teaching Fitzgerald.

She put the phone down and something loosened slightly in her chest, the relief of receiving warmth from a direction where you were unsure it was coming. The smile was real. She knew it was real. She also knew that the relief meant she had been more afraid than she had admitted to herself, and that the fear had been about something more than the photo and the "not content" conversation, something underneath those things, the accumulating question of whether there was a version of her life that could hold a person who insisted on being a person.

She did not know the answer to that yet. She was beginning to understand that not knowing was the honest position.

She sat in the stream room for another twenty minutes after she put the phone down. The ring light was off. The camera was dark. She was just a person in a chair in a room, not streaming, not performing, not metabolizing anything, just sitting with the chair and the dark and the feeling she hadn't finished feeling.

Then she turned the ring light on.

She streamed that evening to forty-three thousand people and was good at it, was herself doing it, and it was real. And somewhere around the forty-minute mark she noticed, in the small way you notice something without looking at it directly, that she was a little elsewhere. Not unhappily.

Just: elsewhere, the way a part of you is always elsewhere when another part of you has opened a question it hasn't finished asking.

She didn't examine it. She kept going.
