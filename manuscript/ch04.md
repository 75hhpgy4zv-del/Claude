# 4

The stream was called *Second Viewing* because Derek believed, with the conviction of a
man who had lost friendships over it, that nobody had ever seen a film they had only
seen once.

He went live at 10:40 on Wednesdays, which was a stupid time for a working adult to do
anything. He had tried nine. At nine he was still coming down off the day, still
carrying fifth period around in his jaw, and the first forty minutes of any nine o'clock
stream had a brittle quality he could hear on playback. At 10:40 every sensible person
on the block had gone to bed and the house had gone quiet with them, and he could talk.

Eighty-one people were watching him talk about *Fire Walk With Me*.

He had the disc in the tray, which nobody could see and which mattered to him. He had
the good lamp on and the bad lamp off. He had a mug of the tea he only drank on
Wednesdays, and behind him on the shelf, out of focus, a *Twin Peaks* steelbook stood
face-out where a person watching at 480p on a phone would register it as a shape and a
person watching at full resolution would register it as a declaration.

"So here's my problem with the discourse," he said. "And I want to be careful, because
I've been wrong about this movie before, on record, and people send me the clip."

*they do*
*we do*
*link it*

"The received line is that the show is a mystery about a dead girl and the film is the
tragedy underneath it. Fine. True. Undergraduate. But that framing still puts Laura in
the object position — she's the *underneath*, she's the thing the film exhumes. And I
think that's exactly the reading the film was built to refuse."

He leaned back. The chair complained. Eighty-three.

"The show is about a town of people who love a dead girl. Loving her is comfortable.
She can't disagree with any of it. The film is about the girl, alive, telling everyone
what is happening to her, in daylight, in words, and every single person she tells
finds a reason not to hear it. That's not subtext. Lynch — who is the most famous
subtext merchant in American film, a man who built a career on the idea that the lawn
has bugs under it — makes this one movie where the surface is *screaming*. And it was
booed at Cannes. It got the worst reviews of his life. Which, I would argue, is the
film completing itself."

*chills*
*mothlight: oh that's the read*
*PERSIMMON_77 has gifted 5 subs*

"Thank you, Persimmon. You're a menace and I love you."

He talked for another twenty minutes, most of it good, some of it the same four
sentences rearranged, which was the tax on doing this live. Around eleven-thirty a new
name came into chat with the pale badge of a fresh sub, handle **tuesdaysoff**, and
said nothing for a while, which he noticed only later and only because of what happened
next.

Then it said:

*tuesdaysoff: does the movie work if you haven't seen the show*

"Does it—" He stopped. He actually stopped, mid-reach for the mug.

*tuesdaysoff: like structurally. it only lands as a corrective if you've already done
the failing. you have to have been one of the people who loved the dead girl. so is it
a movie or is it a bill*

Derek sat with that.

Eighty-nine people watched him sit with it, and he let them, because the alternative
was saying something worse than nothing. He had been doing this podcast for eleven
years — six of them to an audience that would have fit in his classroom, badly — and
in that time he had received perhaps four questions he did not already have an answer
to. He kept a document. It had four things in it.

"Okay," he said. "So that's the best question anyone's asked me this year, and I want
to be annoying about it before I answer, because I think the question has a hidden
premise and the premise is where the good part is."

He picked up the mug. Put it down without drinking.

"You're assuming the debt is transferable. That if I come to the film clean, I owe
nothing, because I wasn't there for the failure. But Laura isn't only being failed by
Twin Peaks. She's being failed by a whole genre — the beautiful ruined girl, the
plastic and the log and the homecoming photo. Everyone who walks into that movie has
seen a hundred dead girls used as furniture. You don't need the show. You've been doing
the failing your entire life at twenty-four frames a second."

*mothlight: oh no*
*kaiju_dad: he's cooking*
*PERSIMMON_77: BILL COLLECTOR LYNCH*

"So — it's a movie," he said. "But it's a movie in the shape of a bill. And I think
whoever just typed that already knew, and wanted to watch me get there."

*tuesdaysoff: i wanted to watch you get there*

He ended at 12:20, which was late, and did the outro badly, which he always did,
because he could not make himself perform an ending. Then the light on the camera went
out and he was a thirty-five-year-old man alone in a room with a cold mug, and the
house did the thing houses do.

Ninety-one at peak. He noted it, the way he noted it every week, with an accuracy that
he had long ago stopped being embarrassed by.

---

The direct message was waiting when he opened the laptop the next morning, before
school, standing at the counter eating a banana he did not want.

**marimint**, with the check next to it.

*hey — that was tuesdaysoff last night. the alt is so i can watch things without it
becoming a whole thing. i've been listening to Second Viewing since like february.
episode 174 got me through a bad week. anyway hi.*

Derek read it twice. He put the banana down. He looked at the ceiling for a moment,
patience performed for an audience of nobody.

He knew exactly who marimint was. Of course he did. He had made a living — not a
living; he had made about four hundred dollars a month — inside the same building she
owned a floor of. He knew her numbers the way a man who runs a diner knows the numbers
of the chain across the street. Six point four million. He knew what she looked like
when she laughed at something genuinely funny versus when she laughed at something a
guest had said. He had, three years ago, said something about her on the podcast that
he now regretted, and he remembered regretting it, which was different from having
apologized for it.

He also knew that people impersonated her constantly. He had seen the accounts. There
was an entire ecology of them, and they all did this — the too-specific detail, the
*episode 174*, the lowercase, the warmth. It was a good one. It had done its homework.
Episode 174 was a real episode and it had four hundred views.

He typed:

*Whoever you are, this is a good bit and I'd rather you didn't. If you like the show,
listen to the show. That's the whole transaction and it's a fine one.*

He sent it. He got in the car. He taught *Of Mice and Men* to four groups of sophomores
who had, collectively, read eleven pages of it, and at lunch he did not eat, and at
2:15 he checked his phone in the stairwell like a fifteen-year-old.

*ok so I genuinely respect that, and also — how would you like me to prove it. because
i've thought about it and every option is either insane or a flex and i don't want to
do either one to you.*

He wrote back from the stairwell, one-handed, faster than he meant to:

*Those are the only two options. That's the point. There's no version of proving it
that isn't a demonstration of access. Which is why I'd rather assume you're a person
with a lot of time.*

*that's bleak*

*It's Thursday.*

Then, from the bottom of the stairwell, because he could not help it, because he had
never once in his life let a good question sit unfinished:

*Also your read was wrong about one thing and it's been bothering me since midnight.
The film isn't a bill. A bill implies the debt can be settled. Nobody in that movie
gets absolved, including the audience, including Lynch. It's an invoice with no payment
address.*

The three dots came up. Went away. Came up.

*ok. yeah. it's an invoice with no payment address.*

*it took me seven months to think of the question and you improved it in a stairwell*

*I'm at work.*

*i know. you're a teacher.*

Derek stood very still in the stairwell between the second and third floors, holding
his phone, while above him a door banged open and the noise of fourteen hundred
teenagers came down the shaft like weather.

He had never said that on the podcast. He had been careful about it for eleven years —
not secretive, careful, the way you are careful with a thing you would like to keep
separate from the thing that pays you. He had said *my day job*. He had said *I have to
be up at six*. He had, once, in 2019, said *my students*, and had cut it in the edit,
and then put it back, and then cut it again.

He typed: *How do you know that.*

*because you grade papers on stream when you think the chat isn't looking. you have a
pen you click. and in episode 174 you talked about being tired in a way that was really
specific and honestly i wrote it down.*

He did not answer for six hours.

That night he did not stream, because it was a Thursday and he was not a lunatic, and
he sat on the end of his bed with the laptop open to nothing in particular and thought
about the shape of what had just happened to him with the cold, unhurried thoroughness
he brought to everything he could not control.

Here was the thing. If it was fake, he had been polite and firm and had lost nothing.

And if it was real, then one of the most-watched people alive had spent seven months in
a room with ninety other people, listening to him, and had come out of it wanting to
hand him a question rather than a compliment — and Derek could not make that shape hold
in his hands. Not because it was implausible. Because he had a fairly complete record
of what happened to him when someone chose him, and the record was consistent, and the
record said that the interesting part was always at the beginning.

He wrote: *If this is real, I'm going to be extremely normal about it, which I suspect
is not what you're used to and may not be what you want.*

He looked at it for a while. Then he deleted *which I suspect is not what you're used
to and may not be what you want*, because it was a bid, and he knew it was a bid, and
he had promised himself in a therapist's office he could no longer afford that he would
stop making them.

He sent: *If this is real, I'm going to be extremely normal about it.*

She wrote back in under a minute.

*that's the entire pitch. that's why i subbed.*
