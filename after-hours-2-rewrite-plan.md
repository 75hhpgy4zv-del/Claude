# After Hours 2: Rewrite Plan

## THE PROBLEMS (Honest Inventory)

### Prose
- **Triplicate constructions** everywhere: "the soft one, the real one, the one that meant..." — filler disguised as lyricism
- **Clinical sex narration**: describing arousal like a medical textbook instead of evoking it
- **"The real laugh/smile"**: nobody fake-laughs in intimate conversation, stop specifying
- **Dash addiction**: em-dashes breaking every sentence into parenthetical asides, destroying flow
- **Over-explaining**: telling the reader what they already understood from the action
- **Looping paragraphs**: circling back to restate what was already clear
- **Staccato fragment style**: punchy short sentences with no lyrical variation, reads like a list
- **Repetitive dialogue**: same word ending 4+ consecutive lines, mistaking repetition for emphasis

### Structure
- **Sirin is a hollow plot device**: 15 chapters of "making notes," one coffee, immediate surrender, love letter. No teeth. No consequences. No real action.
- **"Code red" never pays off**: the group chat escalation in Ch 10 is a huge structural moment that's never mentioned again
- **Continuity errors**: Lin's wedding attendance discussed in two separate chapters. Things established and then forgotten.
- **Final 10 chapters are the same chapter repeated**: "The ring caught the light. Noo-Noo faced the wall. The hiding was over." Said five different ways across five chapters with no emotional variation.
- **Chapters lack distinct purpose**: many chapters exist to hit a plot beat but don't change anything emotionally

### Sex
- **Quality degrades**: early scenes had heat and emotional specificity; later scenes became perfunctory checkboxes
- **Same template every time**: Jan pins, JingJing bratty quip, thrust description, orgasm, forehead kiss
- **No variation in dynamic**: the DD/lg and bratty sub dynamics flatten into one mode
- **Sex disconnected from story**: scenes inserted because "there should be sex here" not because the sex reveals something

## PROSE RULES FOR THE REWRITE

1. **No triplicate constructions of ANY kind.** Not "the X, the Y, the Z." Not "Not X. Not Y. The other kind." Not "No X. No Y. No Z." The absence-of construction (defining by listing what something ISN'T before revealing what it IS) is the same banned pattern. Two negatives followed by a positive ("Not X, not Y, just Z") in prose is the same violation. If it has three parts in any configuration — noun lists, negative lists, absence-of reveals — kill it.
2. **No "the real one."** Every laugh is real. Every smile is real. Describe what makes THIS one specific instead.
3. **Maximum 3 em-dashes per page.** Use commas, periods, subordinate clauses. Let sentences breathe.
4. **Never explain what the action already showed.** If Jan's jaw tightens, don't add "because she was holding something heavy." The jaw said it.
5. **Vary sentence length deliberately.** Long sentences for languor and building tension. Short ones for impact. The rhythm should match the emotional register of the scene.
6. **Dialogue should sound like speech, not prose.** People interrupt. People trail off. People say dumb things. People don't deliver thematic monologues during sex.
7. **Sex should read like sex feels** — messy, urgent, specific, surprising. Not like a choreography notation.
8. **Trust the reader.** Say it once. Say it well. Move on.
9. **Kill the word "composure."** Find twelve other ways to describe Jan's self-control.
10. **Noo-Noo facing the wall is a once-per-act image, not a chapter-ending refrain.**
11. **No stacked short declarative sentences** as scene-setting lists. "X. Y. Z." is a list masquerading as prose.
12. **Never use "the way [character] always [verb]" / "like she always did" / "[character]'s usual [noun]."** These are empty references that avoid actual description. SHOW what the look/action IS. Don't reference a habit the reader hasn't witnessed — describe what's happening now.
13. **"The way" is a hinge for empty constructions.** Most uses of "the way [someone] [verb]" are filler that avoids describing the actual thing. Before writing "the way," ask: can I just describe the thing directly? If yes, do that.
14. **Never step out of a sensory scene to explain it.** During sex, during a kiss, during any scene that lives in the body — the narrator does not get to pause and deliver a lecture. No categorizing what type of sex this is. No thesis statements about what a character's body "needs." No essay paragraphs analyzing why someone is loud. If the reader isn't in the bed, on the wall, in the body, the sentence is wrong. The dialogue and the physical description carry it. Trust them.
15. **No bridge sentences that summarize what the next sentence shows.** "Jan gave her all of it. Sank to the hilt" — the second sentence does the work. The first is a narrator confirming delivery. Cut the bridge. Start with the verb.
16. **No comma-separated triplicates.** Three clauses or phrases of roughly equal length joined by commas create a hollow metronome cadence: "X, Y, and Z." The rhythm sounds like prose without doing work. Two items break the pattern. If you need three details, vary the structure — make one a separate sentence, nest one inside another, change the lengths so they don't land identically.
17. **No echo-word bridges.** Pulling a word from dialogue and repeating it as a standalone sentence to hinge into narration ("harder—" / "Harder. Jan's rhythm shifted...") is a crutch. The reader heard the dialogue. Start with the action.

## PRE-COMMIT REVIEW (MANDATORY)

Before committing ANY new chapter or significant edit, read the new text and ask these questions of every sentence. This is not a grep. This is a read.

1. **Does this sentence keep me in the scene or pull me to a lectern?** If it's explaining, categorizing, or delivering a thesis about what's happening — cut it. Especially during sex.
2. **Am I defining something by listing what it ISN'T?** "Not X. Not Y. The real thing." Kill it. Say what it IS.
3. **Does "the way" appear?** Is it doing real work (a simile with a specific vehicle) or is it a hinge for an empty habitual reference? If the latter, describe the thing directly.
4. **Did I just write the same construction I'm supposed to be fixing?** Read the replacement text as if someone else wrote it. Check for X/Y parallels, triplicates, absence-of patterns IN THE NEW TEXT.
5. **Is the narrator delivering a paper during sex?** If a paragraph during a sex scene could be read aloud at a conference, it doesn't belong in the scene.
6. **Is there a bridge sentence before the real sentence?** "Jan did it. [Description of Jan doing it.]" Cut the bridge.

## SIRIN: THE FIX

### The Problem
Sirin does nothing. She notices things. She makes notes. She has one conversation with Jan. She writes a love letter. All tension, no consequence. The story would be identical if she weren't in it — the crew would still be anxious, Jan and JingJing would still be reckless, the wedding would still happen. Sirin is a scarecrow.

### The Fix: Sirin Must Act and Her Actions Must Cost Something

**Phase 1 (Ch 1-7): The Observer**
Sirin arrives. She's professional, warm, disarming. She does what journalists do — watches, listens, builds rapport with crew members. She's not a threat yet. She's furniture. This is accurate to how embedded journalists work and it's why the crew underestimates how much she's absorbing.

**Phase 2 (Ch 8-12): The Pattern**
Sirin doesn't catch Jan and JingJing. She catches the CREW. She notices:
- Lin's physiological stress responses when Jan touches JingJing
- Fah's supernatural ability to appear at doorways at exactly the wrong moment
- The way camera operators subtly reframe shots to exclude moments that last a beat too long
- Schedule anomalies: Jan and JingJing always in the same car, same hotel floor, adjacent call times

She starts cross-referencing. Pulls their social media posting times (they post within minutes of each other, every time, suggesting they're in the same room). Checks hotel bookings on location shoots. Talks to catering staff about how many meals get delivered to Jan's room (double portions, every time).

**THIS IS WHAT A JOURNALIST ACTUALLY DOES.** Not "make a note." Investigate.

**Phase 3 (Ch 12-13): The Documentary Rough Cut**
Sirin shows Prae a rough cut of documentary footage — not the article, the actual video. In the footage: a moment she captured through a doorway. Jan adjusting JingJing's collar. It's three seconds long. It's not proof of anything. But the INTIMACY of the gesture — the domesticity, the ownership in the touch — is unmistakable to anyone watching.

Prae sees it. Tells Lin. Lin tells Jan.

THIS is the "code red" trigger. Not a group chat that goes nowhere — an actual piece of evidence that exists in the world and could be released.

**Phase 4 (Ch 14-17): The Negotiation**
The crew goes into actual crisis mode. Concrete actions:
- New physical protocols: Jan and JingJing are never alone in the same room on set. Fah is assigned as a permanent shadow.
- Social media lockdown: all posts go through Lin for approval
- The dressing room gets a SCHEDULE — staggered times, no overlap
- Lin personally reviews Sirin's daily footage logs

Jan and JingJing chafe against this. They resist. They break the rules. Each time they break a rule, there's a REAL consequence — Lin has to have an uncomfortable conversation with Sirin, or Fah has to confiscate footage, or the schedule gets tighter. The crew's fear creates real friction with Jan and JingJing's obliviousness.

**Phase 5 (Ch 17): The Conversation**
Sirin sits Jan down. But it's not "I'm not trying to out you." It's: "I have enough for the story. I have the rough cut. I have the scheduling data. I have the crew's behavior pattern. I have a blurry fan photo from Chiang Mai. I have your social media timing analysis. None of it is a smoking gun. All of it together is a very clear picture."

She pauses.

"I'm choosing not to publish the picture. I'm choosing to let you tell it first. But I need you to understand that this choice is costing me professionally. My editor wants the story. The story is my career. I'm giving that up because I think you deserve to own this. That's my gift to you. What I need from you is a promise: when you're ready, you come to me first. Not the network. Not another journalist. Me. Because I earned this story by choosing not to take it."

THIS is a real negotiation. It costs Sirin something. It gives Jan something real to reckon with. And it creates a debt that structures the endgame.

**Phase 6 (Ch 25-28): The Payoff**
When Jan and JingJing go public, they go to Sirin first. They give her the exclusive sit-down. Not because she threatened them — because she earned it. Sirin's piece is still a love letter, but it's earned now. She earned the right to write it by NOT writing the exposé.

### What This Changes
- Sirin has agency, motivation, professional stakes
- The "code red" has teeth and consequences
- The crew's interference creates real friction (not just anxiety)
- Jan and JingJing breaking rules has actual costs
- The resolution involves a real exchange, not a surrender
- Sirin's final article is earned, not gifted
