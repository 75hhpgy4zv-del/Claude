# Chapter Eighteen
## 7.4(b)

Nobody said anything to her, and that was the whole of it.

That was the thing she would have found impossible to explain to him, and did not try to for another nine days: that there was no meeting. There was no summons, no closed door, no man behind a desk saying *we need to discuss something.* Nobody at Bluehour Media accused Kang Ha-eun of anything, at any point, ever.

They just started adjusting.

**Monday.** Her security detail went from two to four. The explanation, delivered by a person from operations she had met twice, was the airport clip and the elevated volume of location-based chatter, and it was true, and it was correct, and it was the responsible thing to do.

**Tuesday.** A soft hold went on the second week of December. No reason given, because holds don't have reasons, they have codes, and the code was one she recognized from the year they'd shot the Japanese thing. Two days later she found out from Ba-da, indirectly, that the second week of December was the week she had blocked out herself, three months ago, in a schedule meeting, saying *I want that week and I'm not going to explain why.*

**Wednesday.** Her personal card — not the company card, the personal one, the one that went through a family office in Seoul because everything of hers went through somebody — flagged a hotel booking in Los Angeles from six weeks earlier for review. It was a routine review. She got an email about it that used the word *routine* twice.

**Thursday.** A wellness check. That was the actual term used, in an actual email, from a department that had been created eighteen months ago after a thing that had happened to a boy at another company, and which existed because it should exist, and which was staffed by good people. A woman called Yoon-seo, thirty-one, kind, extremely competent, sat with her for fifty minutes and asked how she was sleeping and whether she was eating and whether there was anything going on she'd like support with, and Ha-eun said no, and Yoon-seo wrote something down.

**Friday.** Ji-won was moved.

---

She found out about that one in a corridor, which was correct, because everything real in her life happened in corridors.

"They're giving me Nari's block for the promotion cycle," Ji-won said.

Ha-eun stopped walking.

"You're my manager."

"I'm still your manager. I'm going to be running two blocks for three weeks, which is normal, everyone does it, Sun-woo did it last year." Ji-won was looking at her tablet. "It means I'm not in the car for about a third of your movements. There'll be a second person on those. Kyung-min. He's good, he's been here four years, you've met him."

"Unni."

"He's good."

"*Unni.*"

Ji-won looked up.

And Ha-eun watched a woman she had known for six years decide, in about a second and a half, how much to say in a corridor.

"You remember what I told you at the door in April," Ji-won said.

"You said you weren't asking where I go."

"I said I was *telling* you that I wasn't asking, which is a different sentence, and that one day somebody would ask me, and that on that day I would be able to say it and it would be true." Ji-won's face did not change at all. "Somebody asked me on Tuesday."

The corridor was fluorescent and a hundred and thirty feet long.

"What did you say?"

"I said I didn't know where you went." Ji-won put the tablet against her chest. "And it was true. And they believed me, because it was true, because I have spent seven months making sure it stayed true, which has been the hardest thing I have ever done in this job by an enormous distance." A pause. "And then on Friday I got Nari's block."

"They didn't believe you."

"They believed me completely," Ji-won said. "That's the problem. I'm not being punished, Ha-eun-ah. I'm being *routed around*. There's no accusation. There's nothing to appeal. Somebody looked at a situation and concluded that the most efficient adjustment was for me to be in the car slightly less, and they were right, and if I go and ask about it I'll be told it's a normal reallocation and I'll be told the truth."

Ha-eun stood in the corridor.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"Don't." Ji-won started walking again. "That's the one thing I'd genuinely rather you didn't do. If you apologize then I have to say it's fine, and then we've both said a thing we don't mean, and then it's *between* us. Don't put it between us."

---

Jang Se-ri gave her the file on the following Wednesday, in the legal department, at ten past six in the evening, without being asked.

"You should read your contract," she said.

Ha-eun looked at her.

"Sunbae."

"I'm not going to say anything else and I'm not going to sit here while you read it and I would like you to notice that I've given you the whole file and not a summary." Jang Se-ri had her coat on and her bag on her shoulder. She was forty-one. She had told Ha-eun in an elevator when she was twenty that she should never sign anything on a Friday, and had never once, in six years, said another unsolicited word to her. "It's Wednesday," she said, and left.

---

She read it in her apartment.

She got home at nine and did not turn most of the lights on, and she sat at the kitchen island with the file — a real file, paper, two hundred and six pages, because that was what Jang Se-ri had chosen to hand her rather than a link that would have shown somebody she'd opened it — and she read her own life.

She had read it before. That was the thing. She had read it in 2019 in a room with three people in it and again in 2021 when they'd amended the Japanese schedule and again in pieces about fourteen times since, and she had always read it the way she read everything, which was fast and well, catching the mechanism, checking the numbers, holding the shape of the whole thing in her head.

Miru had said: *read it again with him in the room in your head.*

She turned to seven.

---

> **7.4 CONDUCT AND REPUTATION**
>
> **(b)** The Artist shall promptly notify the Company in writing of any personal relationship, association, or circumstance which a reasonable person would consider capable of affecting the Artist's public image or the commercial value of the Artist's activities. Such notification shall be provided in advance where practicable. The Company shall have sole discretion as to any consequent adjustment of the Artist's activities, schedule, or promotional obligations.
>
> Where the Company determines that any matter under this clause has resulted in a quantifiable diminution of revenue, the Company may allocate such diminution against the Artist's unrecouped balance under Schedule 3.
>
> For the avoidance of doubt, obligations under this clause are joint and several across the Members as defined in Schedule 1.

She read it four times.

Then she got up and got a glass of water and did not drink any of it and came back and read it again.

---

It was not what people thought it was. That was the first thing and it made her, briefly, almost hysterical.

There was a version of this clause that everybody outside believed in — the crude one, the one that got written about in English-language articles by people who had never seen one, *idols are banned from dating.* That clause did not exist. It had not existed in a contract of this class for years. Nobody at Bluehour Media had ever told her she could not have a private life and if she had gone into a room and asked, eleven people would have looked at her with genuine confusion and said *of course you can.*

What existed was this.

**She had to tell them.** In writing. In advance where practicable. Any relationship, association or *circumstance* — and she sat with *circumstance* for a while, because a lawyer had chosen that word and had chosen it because it does not have edges.

**They decided what happened next.** Sole discretion. Not consultation, not appeal, not a standard, not a threshold. Discretion.

**And if it cost money, the money came out of her.**

That was the machinery. Not a punishment. Not termination, not a fine, nothing that could be described in a headline as a company doing something to a woman. Just an *allocation.* If the company determined — itself, by its own instruments, with no obligation to show her the arithmetic — that her private life had produced a quantifiable diminution, the number went onto the balance she was already carrying.

She had turned to Schedule 3 by then. She knew what was in Schedule 3. Everybody's Schedule 3 was the same shape: training, housing, vocal, dance, language, the first two videos, styling, the Japanese debut, seven years of a company spending money on a girl and keeping the receipts. She had been repaying it since she was nineteen and there was a real number at the bottom of it and the number had been going down and she was proud of that in a way she had never said out loud to anyone.

The punishment for loving someone was **debt.**

And she sat in her kitchen in the dark and laughed once, out loud, with no humour in it at all, because of course it was. Of course that was the shape. She had been named for it.

---

Then she got to the last line and stopped being able to breathe properly for a while.

> *For the avoidance of doubt, obligations under this clause are joint and several across the Members as defined in Schedule 1.*

Joint and several.

Miru. Nari. So-hyun.

If she triggered it — if the company determined a number, any number, by any method it liked — the allocation did not land on her. It landed on the account. And the account was four women.

Nari was twenty-three. Nari had cried on a practice room floor seven days ago about an eight-count. Nari's mother had a shop in Daegu that had been mortgaged a second time in 2019 and Ha-eun knew that because Nari had told her at two in the morning in a van and had asked her not to tell anyone.

So-hyun went home at ten every night.

She read the line about nine more times, and each time she understood more precisely what it was, and what it was was this: *the company does not have to stop you. The company has to make sure the cost of it is carried by the people you love, and then step back, and let you do the arithmetic yourself.*

Nobody would ever have to threaten her. Nobody would ever have to say a single word to her.

They had not said a single word to her.

---

At twenty past one she read it out loud.

She had not planned to. She had read it silently nine times and something in it was not landing, the way things did not land when you read them, and so she pushed the file into the light and read §7.4(b) aloud in her own kitchen at one in the morning, all of it, from *The Artist shall promptly notify* down to *Schedule 1*.

And the voice that came out was the boardroom voice.

She heard it happen and could not stop it. It was the voice she used on nine and the voice she had used on Director Ryu about the unilateral extension, level and warm and unhurried, the voice of a woman who is entirely comfortable with a document and is about to explain what is wrong with clause four. It was a good voice. It had won her things.

She read a clause about her own life in it, to an empty kitchen, and about two-thirds of the way through she heard herself, from a small distance, the way you hear yourself in a recording.

She stopped.

"Okay," she said, out loud, in a completely different voice — smaller, higher, unmistakably younger, the one that came out at hotel menus and at his kitchen counter about onions. "Okay. Okay, so — "

And then she couldn't.

She sat at a kitchen island at half past one in the morning with two hundred and eighteen pages in front of her, and the Executive had just finished reading the clause to the Girl, and the Girl had understood it perfectly on the first pass, because the Girl was not stupid, the Girl had never been stupid, the Girl had simply never once been the person in the room when anything was decided.

She put her head down on the file.

She stayed like that for a while.

---

Somewhere in there she found the signature page.

There were two signatures on the 2012 trainee agreement. One was her mother's, small and upright and very careful, the way her mother wrote everything, in the box marked 법정대리인.

The other was hers. She was twelve. It was not a signature; it was a *drawing* of one. She had clearly practiced it, because there was a confidence in it that a twelve-year-old only gets from practicing, and she remembered — she remembered *sitting on her bed doing it,* doing it over and over on the back of a homework sheet, because she had been told there was going to be a signing and she had wanted to have a good one ready.

She had been excited.

That was the part she had to put her hand over her mouth about. She had sat on a bed in Ilsan and practiced her signature for an hour because she was going to get to sign something, and nobody had lied to her, and everything they had said was true, and she had been *so happy.*

Then the 2019 exclusive, seven years later. One signature, no guardian box. Nineteen years old.

The handwriting had not changed at all.

---

She did not call him.

She thought about it for a long time — it was ten in the morning where he was, he'd be between fourth and fifth, and rule fourteen sat there on page four of her own document in her own handwriting — and she did not call him.

She wrote it out on the plastic phone instead, three times, and deleted it three times, and the reason was not that she was protecting him. She was extremely clear with herself about that, sitting in the dark, and she checked it twice the way you check a sum.

It was that the clause said *the Members*.

And there was no version of that call that did not end with him understanding that the price of him was being carried, in part, by a twenty-three-year-old in Daegu whose mother had a shop. And he would hear that once and he would never be able to unhear it, and he would start doing arithmetic that was not his to do, and he would arrive at an answer, because he was very good at arriving at answers and he was the most honest man she had ever met, and she knew exactly what the honest answer to that arithmetic was.

She turned the phone face-down on the counter.

She thought: *that's not lying to him.*

She thought: *that is exactly the thing he made me write down.*

She sat with both of those in the dark for a long time and did not resolve them, and at ten past three she got up and put the two hundred and ten pages back in order and squared the edges, twice, because her hands wanted something to do.
