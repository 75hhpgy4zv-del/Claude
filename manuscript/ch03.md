# 3

His mother's house had a smell that arrived before the door did, and it got him from the
driveway every time.

"Annam tinnava?"

"Amma, it's eleven in the morning."

"That's not an answer."

"It's a dodge. Dodges are a category of answer."

She hit him on the arm with a dish towel. Inside, his father got up out of the chair to
turn the television down rather than off, which was the greeting, and asked him about
the brakes on the Civic, and then talked for eleven minutes about a bridge retrofit in
Bakersfield he'd read about. The retrofit was his father's way of talking about his
other son. Derek let him. It cost nothing and his father was enjoying himself.

At 11:50 Anand came in with a bag of mangoes and a story about the traffic.

---

His brother was ten years older and taller and had the particular ease of a man who has
never once wondered whether he was welcome in a room. He hugged their mother. He clapped
Derek on the shoulder with the flat of his hand.

"There he is. The professor."

"Teacher."

"Same thing."

They ate. The food was extraordinary and his mother said three times that it hadn't come
out right. Anand talked about the firm, a merger, a man named Rick who was an idiot, a
project in Riverside six weeks behind because of somebody else. The table arranged itself
around him the way water finds a slope.

Derek asked the follow-up questions, which he was good at, and made his father laugh
twice.

Then Anand put his fork down. "So what's the plan, though."

"With?"

"The internet thing." He said it kindly. That was the part nobody who hadn't sat at this
table could be made to understand. He said everything kindly. "Because Meera's cousin's
kid in Fremont does the same thing with the games. Twenty-two, bought his mother a car.
And I'm sitting here going, my brother's been at this since when, 2015?"

"Fourteen."

"Eleven years. So I'm asking as somebody on your side. What's the return?"

"Four hundred and eleven dollars a month and a comprehensive knowledge of Paul
Verhoeven."

Anand laughed. Their mother laughed. It was going fine.

"No, but seriously."

"That was serious."

"Okay, but you're a smart guy—"

"There's a five-year model." It came out of Derek's mouth before he had decided anything.
"Six tabs. I've walked nineteen properties in three counties. I know what a laser
projector costs installed and what the licensing runs on a thirty-five-millimetre print
out of Kerala and how many seats you have to sell on a Tuesday to survive a Wednesday."

The table went quiet in an interested way. His mother had stopped serving.

"A theatre," Derek said. "Not a multiplex. A house that runs world cinema, any era, and
actually programs it, and—"

"How much?"

"What?"

"To open the doors." Anand had his phone out, not to look at it, just holding it. "First
one. All in."

"One point four."

"Million."

"Yes."

"Okay." Anand nodded slowly, generously, already assembling something. "Okay. And where's
one point four come from, on a teacher's salary, at thirty-five, with no—" He stopped
himself, which was worse than not stopping. "I'm not being a jerk. I'm the only person at
this table who's going to ask you the actual question."

"You're really not."

"Derek."

"Ask the actual question, then. Go ahead. Say the end of that sentence."

"I said I wasn't being a jerk."

"With no *what*, Anand." His voice had gone up and gotten precise at the same time,
which was what it did. "With no wife. Say it. It's been eleven months since you've come
within a mile of it and you've been circling all morning, so let's just — no, actually,
here's the actual question, since you're the only one brave enough. Ask me why a man
whose wife left with his best friend hasn't got one point four million dollars. Because
I'd love to lay that out for you. I'd love to do the tabs."

Nobody moved.

His mother said, quietly, "Orey."

And that was it. That was the whole thing, one word, and something in Derek's chest went
through the floor, because he could hear his own voice still hanging in the room and it
was ugly and it was aimed at a woman who had spent the morning cooking.

"Okay," he said. "No. I'm sorry. Anand, I'm sorry, that was — that was completely out of
line and you didn't earn it. You asked me a normal question and I've been holding a
speech about it for six years and you got the whole thing in the face. That's not your
fault. I'm sorry."

Anand put his hand up, easy, already past it. "Hey. It's fine. It's forgotten."

"It's not fine, I'm—"

"Derek." Warm. Enormous. "It's forgotten."

And then he turned to their mother and said, "This is why he's the sensitive one," and
she laughed with relief, and his father reached for the rice, and the table closed over
it like water.

Nobody asked another question about the theatre. Not one, for the remaining ninety
minutes.

---

At the door his mother held his face in both hands and said *jagratha, nanna*, and he
loved her so much it was hard to stand there.

His father came out onto the step, which he did not do.

"The projector," he said.

"What?"

"You said laser projector. There are two kinds?"

"There's — yeah. There's lamp and there's laser. Lamp's cheaper up front and you're
buying bulbs forever."

His father nodded. He looked at the driveway for a while.

"Bakersfield," he said. "They used the cheap bearings. Forty years, now they're paying
for it." He patted Derek twice on the shoulder blade, which was the most he had ever
done. "Get the good one."

Then he went back inside before Derek could say anything at all.

---

Derek got in the car. He put his seatbelt on. He did not start the engine.

Across the street a man was running a leaf blower for no reason anybody could determine.

He sat there for eleven minutes with the heat coming up off the dashboard, and what he
kept coming back to was not the fight, and not *with no wife*, and not even *it's
forgotten*, which would be with him at two in the morning for a week.

It was that his father had come out onto the step. That was going to cost him the whole
rest of the day.

The leaf blower stopped. In the quiet, Derek started the car.
