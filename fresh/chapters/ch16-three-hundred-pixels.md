# Chapter Sixteen
## Three Hundred Pixels

Ji-won knocked at 6:04, which was thirty-six minutes early, and did not wait.

Ha-eun was awake. She had been awake since about four in the way she was sometimes awake since about four, lying in the dark doing nothing, which was the closest thing to a hobby she had. She sat up.

"Unni."

Ji-won came and sat on the end of the bed, which she had not done in six years, and put her phone into Ha-eun's hands screen-up and already playing.

---

It was nine seconds long.

It had been shot at Tom Bradley, arrivals side, on a Thursday three weeks ago, by somebody who had not been looking for her — that was clear from the movement of it, the way the phone swung up late and had to correct. There were four people in frame at the start and then a security back and then, for about a second and a half, her, mid-stride, in the mask and the cap, being walked at speed toward a door.

She looked at herself. That was the first thing and it took no time at all, because she had been looking at herself professionally since she was nineteen and could read a frame of it the way other people read a sentence. Nothing. She was nothing in it. Mask, cap, grey, four feet of motion, a woman with a schedule.

"That's fine," she said. "That's completely—"

"Keep watching."

The clip ran out and looped.

The second time, she watched the edge of the frame.

At second six, at the extreme right, a black car with the rear door open and a hand on the top of the doorframe. That was all. A forearm to the elbow, the rest of the person outside the shot entirely, holding a door the way you hold a door for somebody.

The forearm had writing on it.

---

"Okay," Ha-eun said.

"Ha-eun-ah."

"Okay. Okay. It's — " She was already zooming it, which was pointless; there was nothing there to zoom to. Three hundred pixels of skin and something dark on it. "You can't read that. There's no *reading* that, unni, that's — that's a smudge, that's noise, that's not—"

"They read it," Ji-won said.

---

Here is what had happened, as far as anyone was ever able to reconstruct it, and Ha-eun would eventually be able to recite the sequence the way you recite a route you drive every day.

The clip went up at 11:40 p.m. Korean time, from an account with four hundred and sixty followers, with a caption that was not about her. The account belonged to a person who had been clearing their camera roll and had found nine seconds from a trip in the autumn and had posted it because they had been at the same airport as somebody, which is a thing people do.

It was reposted at 12:10 by a fan account with ninety thousand followers, cropped, for the second and a half of her.

At 12:52 somebody posted the uncropped version back, with a circle drawn on the right-hand edge of the frame, and the words: *who is this*

At 1:30 there were four hundred replies and the most-liked one said *staff*.

At 2:11 a person in Nagoya, who had a Japanese-language K-pop account with thirteen thousand followers and a stated professional interest in nothing at all, posted the frame at 400% with the contrast pushed and said: *it's Japanese. give me a minute.*

At 2:40 she posted the sentence.

> 耳当たりの良い嘘を聞かされるより、残酷な真実を告げられる方がいい。

At 3:15 somebody had it in English.

At 4:02 somebody had found the sentence in a *High and Low* subtitle file and knew where it was from and posted a still from the film, and that gave the arm a *type*, and the type was: a man in his thirties who cares a great deal about Kurosawa.

At 5:20 an account that had been quiet for two years posted three images side by side. The frame from the airport. A photograph of the same forearm, in better light, at a different angle. And a book jacket.

The middle image was an author photo. It was three years old, it had been taken in a bookshop in Los Feliz by somebody's friend for four hundred dollars, and in it a man was not smiling because he had been told to smile, and his sleeves were pushed up.

The caption on the post was five words.

*he wrote a book about it*

---

"It's six-twenty," Ji-won said. "It's been eleven hours."

Ha-eun sat on a bed in Hannam with a phone in her hands.

"Eleven hours," she said.

"They didn't have anything. They had *three hundred pixels*, Ha-eun-ah, and a person in Japan who was awake, and eleven hours." Ji-won's voice was doing something Ha-eun had heard maybe twice. "I have been in this industry for fourteen years and I have never seen it go that fast and I want you to understand that nobody made it go fast. Nobody organized it. It's not a campaign. It's four hundred thousand people each doing about nine seconds of work."

---

She did the arithmetic she had been doing since she was nineteen.

There was a protocol. There had always been a protocol, and the protocol had three parts, and she could run all three of them in her head while she brushed her teeth, which is what she did.

**One: is it deniable.** Yes. Completely. A car door and an arm. There was no photograph of the two of them, there had never been a photograph of the two of them, there was no reservation with both names, no transfer, no shared vehicle, no story with a start and an end. It was an arm. He had a professional relationship with her company that was minuted and could be produced. The whole thing could be walked into the ground inside a day.

**Two: what does the company do.** The company would find out by nine. Somebody in comms had a dashboard and the dashboard had already gone off. There would be a call. The call would be pleasant.

**Three: what do you do first.**

And the answer to three had been the same for seven years, and the answer was *nothing*. You do nothing. You do not respond, you do not adjust, you do not cancel, you do not delete, you do not put out anything, you do not text anybody anything that can be read later. You get in the car at seven and you do the day.

She stood in the bathroom with her toothbrush and looked at herself and thought: *and you don't tell him until it's shaped.*

That was in the protocol too. It had never been written down. You did not hand somebody the raw thing. You waited until you knew what it was, and then you told them what it was, and that was kinder, that was *obviously* kinder, that was the entire technique of nine years of managing four other women and a mother and a company —

She put the toothbrush down.

---

**14. You don't lie to me to protect me.**

He had written it in her document in the passenger seat of a Honda on level six with a pen out of the door pocket, and she had written it into her copy in full, in English, which had taken a while, and underlined it twice.

*You're going to regret fourteen.*

*Probably.*

*No, I mean it. You've just made me promise to hand you things that will hurt you and I'm going to do it, because I keep contracts. So when I hand you the first one, don't look at me like I did it.*

---

She called him at 6:41, which was 1:41 in the afternoon on a Thursday, which meant fifth period.

He picked up on the fourth ring and said, very quietly, "I'm in a room with thirty-one people. Are you okay?"

"Yes. Nobody's hurt. It's not an emergency and I need five minutes and I don't want to do it at eight at night."

A pause, and then the sound of a door, and then a corridor, and then a different acoustic — outside, she thought, from the traffic.

"Okay," Derek said. "Go."

She told him.

She told him all of it, in order, with the times, because the times were the thing. The nine seconds. The camera roll. The crop and the uncrop and the circle. The account in Nagoya at 2:11 with a stated professional interest in nothing at all. The subtitle file. The author photo from a bookshop in Los Feliz for four hundred dollars.

And the five-word caption.

She did not soften any of it and she did not do the thing where you put the worst part in the middle, and she was aware, the entire time, of doing something with her hands that she had not done since she was about fifteen, which was pulling the cuff of her sleeve down over them.

When she stopped there was traffic on the line for a while.

"Okay," Derek said.

"That's it?"

"No, that's — give me a second, I'm putting it in order." More traffic. "Which tattoo."

"The forearm. The Kurosawa."

He made a sound. Not a laugh. Adjacent to one.

"What."

"Nothing. It's not funny."

"Say it."

"It's the one that says I'd rather be told the cruel truth than fed gentle lies," Derek said. "That's the one they read. Out of twelve. Of all of them, that's the one that was pointing at a camera."

Ha-eun stood in a bedroom in Hannam and put her hand over her mouth.

"I know," he said.

"I'm not laughing—"

"You're allowed. It's — that is genuinely the funniest thing that has ever happened to me and I'm going to be able to enjoy it in about four years."

---

Then he said, "Can I ask you something and you answer it accurately."

"Yes."

"Was there a version of this call where you waited?"

She had known it was coming. She had known it from the second he said *give me a second, I'm putting it in order,* because he put things in order and this was the order.

"Yes," she said. "There was a version where I let the company tell me what it was by lunchtime, and then I got in the car, and then I called you tonight and told you a *finished* thing. With a shape on it. That version is better in every way I know how to measure and it was what I was going to do, and I got as far as the bathroom."

"And?"

"And I remembered you made me write it down."

"That's not why you called."

"It's *exactly* why I called—"

"Ha-eun." Traffic. "You didn't call because there's a document in my glovebox. You called because you didn't want to be the person who decides what I can hear. Those are different, and it's the second one."

She stood there.

"Don't be nice to me right now," she said. "That's — I'm holding something together and you're being nice at it."

"Okay."

"Say something normal."

"The clip has how many views?"

"Three million," Ha-eun said. "It was nine when Ji-won came in and it was six when I got out of the bathroom."

Another long pause on a phone line eight thousand miles long.

"That's a really big number," Derek said. "I'm going to be honest with you, I've been sitting here trying to have a proportionate response and the number's not helping."

"There isn't a proportionate response."

"No."

"That's the thing I've never been able to explain to anyone." She sat down on the end of the bed where Ji-won had sat. "There isn't one. It's not big enough to be an emergency and it's much too big to be nothing, and there's no *feeling* that's the right size for it, so you just don't have one, and then people say you're cold."

"I don't think you're cold."

"I know you don't."

"Ha-eun."

"Don't." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "I have a car at seven and I have to be a person in about fourteen minutes."

"Then go be a person," he said. "I've got sixth period and you've got a car, and this is going to be exactly as bad tomorrow, and we can do it tomorrow."

"Okay."

"Ha-eun."

"What."

"You did the hard version," Derek said. "I know that. You had a better option and you didn't take it, and it cost you the whole morning, and I'm not going to look at you like you did it."

---

She got in the car at seven.

She did the day. She did a fitting and a recording and forty minutes of a thing with a brand where she was warm and specific and funny in two languages, and at 2:15 in the afternoon Bluehour's comms lead called Ji-won and the call was pleasant and lasted nine minutes and contained the word *monitoring* four times.

At 4:40 she was in a van on the Gangbyeon expressway with her phone face-down on her knee, and she turned it over, and searched the account that had posted the three images.

The post had four hundred and seven thousand likes.

Underneath it, the top reply — pinned, because the account holder had pinned it — was a photograph somebody had taken of their own phone, of a used-book listing on a site she didn't recognize, for a paperback with an ugly cover.

**$4.99. Good condition. Ships from Ohio.**

The caption said: *just ordered it, will report back*

Under that, three hundred and nine replies, and the first one said *same*, and the one below it said *same*, and the one below that said *there's a whole chapter about his depression, it's in the free preview, page 141*

Ha-eun sat in a van on an expressway in Seoul.

She had read page 141 nine times. She had read it on a plane in February and in a bathroom in Jakarta and in bed in three hotels, and she had underlined it once and scribbled the underline out because underlining the whole thing had felt like leaving a note for somebody.

She looked out the window at the river going past and thought, with total clarity and no drama at all:

*They're going to read him.*

*They're going to read all of him, and it took eighteen hours, and nobody had to break into anything, because he printed it, three years ago, and it's four dollars and ninety-nine cents, and it ships from Ohio.*

She put the phone face-down on her knee.

Ji-won, in the front seat, did not turn around.

"Unni," Ha-eun said.

"Mm."

"How long do we have."

Ji-won was quiet for a moment.

"Before what," she said, which was not a question, and was the kindest thing anybody said to her that day.
