# 22

The room she streamed from was smaller than he expected and much uglier, and he loved
it for that.

From the front — from the six-hundred-thousand side — it was warm wood and plants and a
shelf of things at pleasant intervals. From where Derek sat, on a low grey couch behind
the monitors, it was a plywood flat braced with two-by-fours, a coil of cable taped down
with gaffer, and a plant that turned out to be three plants, one of them dead, arranged
to read as abundance from precisely one angle. There was a bin of spare cables. There
was a granola bar wrapper on the floor that he wanted to pick up and did not, because
picking it up would have been a small act of ownership and he had been in Los Angeles
for four days.

Halle sat cross-legged in an office chair by the door with a laptop on her knees and a
headset half on, one ear free. She had been introduced to him as Mari's editor, which
he had since worked out was roughly the way a country describes its army as a defence
force.

"You can talk," Halle said, without looking up. "Mic's cardioid. You'd have to shout."

"I wasn't going to talk."

"You've been not-talking for twenty minutes. It's loud."

Mari was in the chair, in the light, with her back to them. She had a hairband on her
wrist. She had, forty minutes ago, eaten half a bowl of rice standing at the counter
and told him a long story about her cousin's wedding in which the punchline was
delivered in Tagalog and then explained badly and then abandoned, laughing, and she had
put her cold hand on the back of his neck on her way past.

"Two minutes," Halle said.

Mari rolled her shoulders. She said something to herself that he could not hear. She
reached forward, and the countdown went up on the second monitor, and Derek watched the
back of her head, pleased with himself for being back here at all, and thought that he
knew what she looked like right now.

Then the number hit zero.

---

She comes on smiling.

"Hi. Hi. Hello. Okay, we're — is it—" She squints at something off-frame. "We're live.
Great. Cool. Hi."

The chat starts as a trickle and is not a trickle for long. It comes up the side of the
monitor in a column, white on grey, faster than reading.

*HIIII*
*first*
*mari!!!*
*hi mari*
*bro i have an exam in 6 hours*
*HI*
*she looks so pretty today*
*first*

"Okay so I have to tell you what happened at the airport," she says, "and I need you to
understand that none of it was my fault, and also I have proof, which is the part that
makes it worse."

She tells it. It takes four minutes. It is the story about her cousin's wedding — the
same story, the same cousin — and it is better. The punchline is in English. The pause
before the punchline is a beat and a half longer than it was in the kitchen, and it
lands, and forty thousand people say *LMAOO* in a column that moves like water going
down a drain.

"—and my *mother*," she says, "my mother says, and I quote, *well, you're always late
anyway.*"

*LMAOOO*
*mom ate*
*not mom*
*MOTHER*
*shes so real for that*

"So that's my week. That's where I'm at. Okay, what are we — okay. We're doing the
thing. I know. I *know*." She pulls the chair in. "Give me one second, let me get set
up, tell me about your week while I do this. Genuinely. Someone tell me something good
that happened."

The column changes direction. It fills with weeks. Someone got a job. Someone's dog is
out of surgery. Someone failed a class and types it three times before anyone sees it.

"Oh — the dog. The dog! Okay, whoever that was, the dog. That's — I needed that,
actually, thank you." A beat. "And whoever said the class thing, hey. Hey. It's a
class. I want you to say that out loud. *It's a class.* You're allowed to fail a class,
that's what they're *for*, they're little failure containers."

*hes right u know*
*mari therapy hours*
*I NEEDED THIS TODAY*
*shes so genuine*

The count in the corner reads 612,904.

She is very good at this. She is better at this than he has ever been at anything, and
he has spent his adult life in front of a room.

---

He clocked it at the eleven-minute mark, and once he saw it he could not stop seeing it.

She meant it. All of it. He had come to Los Angeles half prepared to catch her
pretending, which would have been so much easier, and she meant every word. She
meant the dog. She meant the class. She had said almost exactly the same thing to him
on the phone in October about a kid in his fourth period, unprompted, when nobody was
watching and there was nothing to be gained.

It was a shift of about thirty degrees, and it lived in her face.

Off-camera, at rest, her face did very little. Strangers read it as unfriendly and it
was not unfriendly; it was simply unemployed. Then the number hit zero and the whole
apparatus came up thirty degrees — the eyes wider by some fraction he could have
measured if he'd been a worse person, the mouth ready a half-second before the joke
arrived, the head tilted to make a camera feel spoken to. It was the difference between
a lamp in a room and a lamp aimed at something.

And the awful part, the part he sat with on the grey couch while forty thousand
strangers had the best four minutes of their day: the thirty degrees was *hers*. Her,
turned up, made reproducible, made deliverable at 4 p.m. Pacific, six days a week, for
nine years.

He had spent an hour in a car in October explaining to her — patiently, at length, with
examples — that the parasocial relationship was a true perception of a real person. He
had been so pleased with himself. He'd said: *they do know you. They're just wrong about
what knowing is worth.*

He had thought he was defending her. Sitting behind the monitors he understood that
what he had actually been doing was defending himself, retroactively, on a Sunday
afternoon in 2023, when he had watched Danielle Marsh talk about missing her mother on
a phone-shot live stream from a hotel room in Seoul, and had felt his chest go warm and
complicated, and had gone to bed obscurely comforted by a twenty-year-old who did not
know he was alive.

He had checked it for a crush, the way you check a pocket. There wasn't one. She was
beautiful and that had almost nothing to do with it. What she had was an unguarded
brightness that worked harder on some people than on others, and Derek had known for
most of his life which kind of person he was. The same equipment that found the one kid
in fifth period going under in total silence also meant a stranger's happiness could
get into him uninvited. It came in through the same door. He had never once worked out
how to leave the door open at school and shut everywhere else.

He had told Mari about that. In November, on the phone, half joking, and she hadn't
laughed, and she'd asked him what he thought he wanted from it, and he had told her the
truth, because he could not do the other thing.

What he had not told her, in the car or on the phone or anywhere else, was the rest of
it. That what Danielle Marsh had done to him by accident on a Sunday afternoon was the
same thing Mari did on purpose, at four o'clock, six days a week, to six hundred
thousand people who were built with the same door he was.

Now, on the couch, he thought: *I am in that column.*

And then, fast and hot, catching himself doing arithmetic on a woman he loved: *no.
Don't. It's not the same and you know it isn't, and reaching for that comparison is a
way of making yourself smaller so that being left will make sense later.*

He knew the move. He had been doing the move his whole life. Knowing the name of it had
never once stopped him.

---

She talked for another two hours.

He watched her be magnificent — there was no other word, and he had a lot of words, it
was the one thing he had — and somewhere in the second hour the shape of it finally
came together in his hands. He had braced for jealousy. He had been half hoping for it,
because jealousy is solvable.

It was this:

There were ninety-one people who came to a room on Wednesdays at 10:40 to hear Derek
talk about a movie. Ninety-one. And every one of them got *all of him* — the actual
man, the same one who taught fifth period and could not do small talk in a staff room
and had not watched *Ant-Man* in ten years over a promise made to nobody. He had no
version. He had never built one. It had never occurred to him to build one, and he had
always privately understood this as integrity.

Sitting behind her monitors he understood that it was not integrity. It was *scale*. He
was whole in front of an audience because his audience was ninety-one people, and a man
can be whole in front of ninety-one people the way a man can carry a table across a
room. Put six hundred thousand in the column and something has to be built, and what
gets built is a version, and the version is true, and it will still stand between you
and every single person on the other side of it forever.

She was not less honest than him. She was more loved than a person can be honest at.

And there was no fix. That was the part that sat down on his chest. There was no
conversation they could have, no boundary, no arrangement, no amount of him being
extremely normal about it, that made the number smaller. The number was the weather. It
had been the weather before he arrived and it would be the weather after, and the only
question available to him — the only one — was whether he wanted to stand in it.

Halle got up at some point and put a glass of water on the desk inside Mari's reach
without breaking her sightline, an act of such practiced invisibility that Derek felt
something in him go quiet with respect.

"She'll be another forty," Halle said, passing. "You okay?"

"Yeah."

Halle looked at him for a second longer than the answer needed. "Yeah," she said. "The
first one's rough. Everybody thinks it's going to be fake."

"It isn't."

"No," Halle said. "It's worse."

---

At 6:40 Mari said good night to six hundred and eight thousand people in a voice like a
hand on a shoulder, and the light went out, and the thirty degrees came off her face
like a coat, and she sat for a moment with her hands in her lap in the ugly little room
full of cable.

Then she turned around in the chair and found him on the couch and said, in the flat
unemployed voice that no one else on earth was allowed to hear:

"God, I'm starving. Did you eat? You didn't eat."

Derek got up and picked the granola bar wrapper up off the floor and put it in the bin
and said, "I'll cook."
