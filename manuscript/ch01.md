# 1

The copier on the second floor had been broken since June and the sign taped to it
still said BACK MONDAY, which had been true once.

Derek pressed the button anyway at 7:20 on the first morning. The machine woke, thought
about it, ran something internal that sounded like a drawer closing in another building,
and put a red triangle on the panel. The hallway was empty in a way it would
not be again until June: floors waxed to a shine nobody was going to look at, every
door shut, the radiators ticking, four hundred lockers standing
in a row with their vents like little shut mouths. Somebody's laminated poster about
growth mindset had come off the wall over the summer and lay face-up on the tile where
it had been since August.

He went down to the first floor, where the good copier lived behind the attendance
office, and where Rosalind looked up at him over her glasses.

"You're early."

"I'm at the end of a long chain of failure."

"Second floor's still out."

"Second floor is a monument, Roz. We should put a plaque on it."

She let him use the good one. She always let him use the good one, because eleven years
ago he had spent a Saturday getting her nephew's college essay into a shape that got him
into Cal Poly, and she had a longer memory than the district did.

Priya found him there with ninety-four warm syllabi in his arms.

"Do you have a second."

"I have four minutes and I'm using them to be smug about paper."

"Rakesh is out." She said it flatly, which was how she said everything that was going to
cost him. "Not out today. Out out. Kidney thing, they're saying December at the earliest,
and the district's given me a long-term sub with a business degree and a Chromebook."

Derek stopped straightening the stack.

"His sophomores," Priya said.

"You want me to take a section."

"I want you to take his fourth period and let the sub have the rest, because fourth
period is the one that'll eat him, and because you'll say yes."

"That's an unbelievable thing to say out loud."

"I've been doing this nineteen years. I don't have time to build up to it."

The copier went on feeding paper into the tray in that warm rhythmic slap, and Derek
stood there and did the arithmetic he already knew the answer to. Four hours. That was
what it came to. Between 4:40 and 8:40 on a Wednesday there were four hours that belonged
to nobody but him, and a sixth prep would take them and go on taking them until December,
and there was a file on his laptop he had not opened since June.

"Fourth period," he said.

"Thank you."

"I want the good projector."

"You'll get the good projector."

"And I want you to remember this in the spring when I ask you for something."

Priya was already walking. "You never ask me for anything," she said. "That's the problem
with you."

---

His room got the afternoon sun and there was nothing to be done about it. By fifth period
in late August it sat at eighty-four degrees with the blinds down, and the two fans he had
bought himself pushed the heat around in a circle, and the whole place smelled of floor
wax and pencil shavings and twenty-nine teenagers who had just come in off a blacktop.
The clock over the door was four minutes fast and had been for six years. On the back
wall, above the bookcase, a strip of butcher paper he'd put up in 2019 said WHAT IS IT
DOING TO YOU, in his own handwriting, which had faded from black to a kind of bruise
colour.

They came in warm and loud and slightly unwell. American Literature, twenty-nine juniors
in a room built for twenty-four. Three of them he already knew. The rest were a wall.

He did not do names first. He waited until they had mostly stopped moving. Then he picked
a book up off the desk and read to them.

*Márgarét, áre you gríeving*
*Over Goldengrove unleaving?*

Somebody laughed, because of the way the accents made him say it. He kept going. He read
it the way it was written, which is to say strangely, with the stresses landing where
Hopkins had hammered them in, and by the fourth line the laughing had stopped, not
because anyone had decided to be respectful but because the noise of it had gotten
interesting.

*Ah! ás the heart grows older*
*It will come to such sights colder*
*By and by, nor spare a sigh*
*Though worlds of wanwood leafmeal lie…*

He read to the end. He let the room sit for a second, which was longer than it sounds.

"Okay. Nobody tell me what it means. That's a trap and I'm not interested. Tell me what
it *sounded* like."

Nothing, for a beat.

"Sad," said a girl in the second row, in a voice that dared him to say it was wrong.

"Sad. Yes. Good. Why — no, sorry, that's the same trap wearing a hat." He put the book
down. "Try this. It sounded sad *before* you knew what any of it meant. Half those words
aren't words. *Wanwood. Leafmeal.* He made them up. And it still landed. So what does
that tell you about how this thing is working on you?"

"That it's cheating," said a boy by the window.

Derek pointed at him, delighted. "It's completely cheating. What's your name?"

"Marcus."

"Marcus says it's cheating and Marcus is right, and that's the class. Everything we read
this year is going to be doing something to you underneath the part you can explain, and
the only real skill I've got to hand you is catching it in the act." He picked the book
back up. "Also, for the record: it's a kid crying about leaves who doesn't know yet she's
crying about dying. Which I know because I'm thirty-five and I read the last line. Took
Hopkins eight lines. Took me two decades. So let's agree the poem's better at this than
we are."

The room laughed. Not all of it. Enough.

While they were laughing he went to the window and cranked it open six inches, which did
nothing and which he did every year anyway, and on the way back down the row he stopped
at the desk under the clock. The boy sitting there had his backpack on both shoulders and
his hands flat on the desk in front of him, square, like a man at a table waiting to be
asked to leave. Everything in front of him was in a line: two pens, parallel, an inch
apart, and a notebook set true with the edge of the desk.

"You want to put that down?"

"I'm okay."

"Sure." Derek moved off without a second look, because the kid had gone the colour of
somebody who needs the moment to be over. "Marcus. Give me another cheat the poem's
pulling. There are four."

He got eleven more good minutes out of them, on the first day, in that heat.

The backpack stayed on for the whole period.

---

At 2:52 the bell went and they poured out, and one of them didn't.

He came up the aisle sideways, holding the index card Derek had them fill out — name,
last thing you read, something true — and put it on the desk instead of in the pile.

"You can just put it with the others," Derek said.

"I know." He was already leaving. "I just didn't want it in the middle."

Derek waited until the room was empty.

Then he read it. The handwriting was small and all caps and pressed hard enough to
emboss the back of the card.

**TOMÁS BEVILACQUA**

*Last thing I read: the manual for my mom's car, cover to cover, twice. Not a joke.*

*Something true: everyone in this class has known each other since kindergarten and I
moved here in March.*

The fans went on turning the hot air over. Out in the hall somebody dragged something
metal a long way and then stopped. Derek sat on the edge of a student desk with the card
in both hands and read it four times, and the building emptied out around him, locker
doors going off down the hall like a row of shots, and then it was quiet, and the light
through the blinds had gone the colour of the inside of a peach.

He took a fresh card out of the drawer and wrote on it and left it face down on the desk
under the clock for the morning.

*Cover to cover, twice, is called close reading and it's the whole job. Thursday at lunch
— I've got something for you and it's too long to explain here. Bring the manual. — DR*

---

He got home at 4:40 and ate cereal standing at the counter.

There were four hours until he had to be a different thing, and he spent them badly. Forty
minutes intending to grade. A stretch flat on the floor between the couch and the coffee
table with his forearm over his eyes, listening to the upstairs neighbour walk from her
kitchen to her bathroom and back, four times. Then, at seven, without warning or any
decision he could point to, a violent hour of competence in which he did all of the
grading, and the thing he'd been putting off since June, and an entirely unnecessary
improvement to the script that pulled his chat logs into a text file — because the
improvement was interesting and the grading was not, and by then the grading was already
done, so it didn't count.

At 8:15 the phone lit up with his brother's name.

He watched it until it stopped. Then he opened the laptop and wrote to Priya that he'd
take fourth period from Wednesday and not to worry about the paperwork, and sent it, and
sat back in the chair.

Six preps. He said it out loud, once, to hear what it sounded like in the apartment.

Then he got up to find something to eat that wasn't cereal.
