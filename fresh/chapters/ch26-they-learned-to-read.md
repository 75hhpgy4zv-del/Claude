# Chapter Twenty-Six
## They Learned To Read

The room off Olive has no windows and they keep it at about sixty-five degrees, which is for the machines, and by the middle of the morning the cold has gotten into the arms of the chairs. Derek had a coffee going soft in his hands for the warmth of the cup rather than the coffee.

Pen found it on the Thursday morning and did the worst possible thing with it, which was to be twenty-four and honest.

"Halina."

"Mm."

"Have you seen—" and then she stopped, because Derek was six feet away with a coffee, and the stopping was louder than the sentence.

"Go on," said Derek.

"It's nothing."

"Pen."

Pen looked at Halina, and Halina, who does not rescue people from things they have started, went on marking a frame.

So Pen turned her phone around.

It is ninety seconds long. A man in his twenties in a car, in the driver's seat, engine off, filming himself with the phone too close to his face. And what he does is read the top of page two hundred and eleven.

She had it on the phone speaker for about three words before Halina said, "No, put it through the room," and Pen said "are you sure—" and Halina said "put it through the room," and Pen plugged it in.

So it came out of the monitors instead, in a treated room with the door shut. There is no reverberation in that room at all. A voice arrives dry and close and about eight inches from your face.

> *There's always a glass. Even when everything is fine. Level One is the day you notice it's there and it doesn't stop you doing anything. You get up, you go, you teach, and the whole time there's a quarter-inch of something between you and the room.*

He read it well.

There was no version of that Derek had prepared for. It was not mockery and it was not an impression. The man in the car read it the way you read a thing that has gotten hold of you — slightly too fast, pushing through the middle of the sentences — and Derek sat in a cold chair in a dead room and listened to a stranger's mouth make the noises he had made at two in the morning in 2021 with nobody in the building.

At the end of it the man said "anyway" and stopped filming.

Nobody said anything. The room does not hold a silence; it just stops.

"He's breathing in the wrong place," said Derek.

Halina turned around in the chair.

"He is," said Derek. "There's a comma after *fine* and he's going through it, so *Level One* arrives as a new thought and it isn't, it's the same thought. And then he front-loads *quarter-inch* like it's the important word and it's the least important word in the sentence. The important word is *something.*"

"Derek."

"It's a good read. I'm saying it's a good read with two mistakes in it."

"Right," said Halina, and let him have it, and did not look at Pen, who had gone extremely still.

He could not afterward account for having said any of that. It had been accurate and it had been fluent and he had watched it come out of himself from somewhere off to the left, the way you watch a man park a car.

They worked for another forty minutes.

Then Halina said, "Pen, go and get me something with sugar in it from the place." She had never once asked for that. Pen went. The door did the thing that heavy doors do, which is not a bang, and Halina put both hands flat on the desk.

"How many of those are there?"

"That's the first one I've seen."

"That is not what I asked you."

The coffee had gone cold enough to be a different drink. He put it down and did not think about it again.

"About nine hundred," he said. "As of this morning. It started on the Friday. There's a girl in a school uniform doing Level Two in a stairwell and there's Level Four read by somebody in a bedroom in Manila, and *the kitchen is twelve feet away* is a font now. It's on a white background. It's been done about nine hundred thousand times."

"And what have you been doing while that happens?"

"Reel six."

"You've been in this room since eight and you've given me five notes and every one of them was good," said Halina. "So I'll ask it a different way. What have you been doing with the nine hundred?"

"I made a rule on Friday," said Derek. "I watch each one once. If I know it's coming I don't watch it at all. I've seen six."

Halina looked at him for a moment.

"That's better than I'd manage," she said, and turned her monitor back on, and then, without turning around: "They're reading it for her, you know."

"I know."

"Nobody in that car gives a damn about you. He wants to know who she is." She scrubbed back a few frames. "And Maya Chen doesn't exist."

"Maya Chen was made up in a chair in Alhambra in 2021 by a man who had never met anybody like that in his life."

"So they'll find nothing."

"They'll find nothing," said Derek. "They found me on the way past."

---

Lucky came on the Saturday with a bag from the place on Valley and let himself in and began in the middle of a story about a lawn.

"—and it is not my lawn. That's the part I need you to hold on to. It has never been my lawn. There is a line, on a *document*, and the line puts nine feet of it on Marisela's side, and she has known this since 2019 and she has said nothing, and now the man has come about the drainage." He was getting plates out. "And the man says to me — to *me*, in my own driveway — *are you the owner.*"

"What did you say?"

"I said *is it your lucky day*," said Lucky, "which was a mistake, and I knew it was a mistake while it was leaving me."

They ate on the floor with their backs against the couch, which is where Lucky has eaten since he was nineteen, and the food was too hot at first and then exactly right and then cold, and Derek ate all of his and could not have told anybody afterward what was in it.

It went for a while and it was ordinary. Deepa's sister. The roof, which now has a second neighbor and a permit problem. A very long account of a lottery shoot in Bakersfield with a horse that had been booked by somebody who had never met a horse.

Then Lucky put the fork down.

"Deepa sent me a thing on Thursday."

"Okay."

"It's nine hundred words and it's about your marriage." He was looking at the plate. "It's very well written, and that's what's been bothering me for two days. Whoever did it isn't a jerk. They've read the book twice, they're careful about it, they're *fair* — and by the end of it the argument is that you were a man who stopped paying attention to a woman and then wrote three hundred pages of being sad about being left."

Derek did not say anything.

"So I read it twice as well," said Lucky, "and then I went and sat in the car for a bit, because I couldn't work out why it was making me feel sick, and then I worked it out."

"Lucky—"

"It's what she used to say."

Derek looked at the plate on his knee.

"It's a fair reading of the book," he said.

"*That's not an answer.*"

"It is an answer. It's a fair reading of the book, because it's all in the book. Every fact in that thread is in there and I put every one of them in there myself." He had not raised his voice. "Nobody's lying about anything. If you read those nine pages and nothing else, that is the conclusion a careful person comes to. I built a thing that produces that conclusion."

"Why would you build that?"

"Because the alternative was writing down what actually happened."

"So write down what actually happened."

"To who?" said Derek.

Lucky put the plate down on the carpet, and the carpet in that apartment is the color of nothing and has a dent in it where a chair used to be.

"Right," he said. "Okay. I'm going to say the thing, and I'm going to say it badly, because I've been carrying it around for four years and I've never found a good way to do it." He wiped his hands on his knees. "I sat in a car outside that apartment for two hours in October. Twice. I don't know if you remember that."

"I remember."

"You came down the second time and you asked me whether you'd been *distant*. That was the word. You asked me, like I'd know, like it was a thing I'd have data on, and you had a face on you like a man asking a mechanic about a noise." Lucky was not looking at him. "And I said no. And you said okay. And then you went back up, and about four days later you asked me the same question again."

Neither of them said anything. Somebody two floors up ran a tap and turned it off.

"She'd been telling you for seven months that you lost it," said Lucky. "And you'd gotten to the point where you were checking. With *me.* From a parking lot."

"That's not—"

"And the thing on the internet is her argument, man. Word for word. It's her argument, made by a stranger who's never met either of you, out of a book you wrote." Lucky picked the plate back up because his hands needed something. "And you just told me it's a fair reading."

Derek sat on the floor of his own living room with his knees up and both hands around his own ankles, which is not a thing a man of thirty-five does in front of anybody.

"It is a fair reading," he said, "and that's why I can't answer it. The only thing that clears me is being cruel about a real person in public in a month when four hundred thousand people are looking for a reason to be interested in something. She'd be findable inside a day."

"So she gets the nine kind pages and you get the nine hundred words."

"Yeah."

"That's a shit trade."

"It's the trade," said Derek.

Lucky ate for a bit.

"Are you being careful," he said, with his mouth full, at completely the wrong moment.

And Derek's phone went on the floor between them, face up, which is how Lucky came to read it upside down at the same time he did.

> **Amma's friend Sudha sent her something about your book. She's upset. I said I'd talk to you.**

Then, a minute later, while both of them were still looking at it:

> **I haven't read it btw**

Then:

> **Is she in it**

Lucky put his fork down for the second time that evening.

"*Is she in it.*"

"Don't."

"He's had that book for three years."

"He hasn't read it."

"*I* hadn't read it," said Lucky, "for four years, and I'm an idiot, and I'm not his *brother*." He got up off the floor, which he did not normally do, and went and stood by the counter. "Is she in it. Not *are you all right*. Not *is this bad for you*. He's had one question in three years and it's about whether he needs to manage your mother."

"Yeah."

"Say something about that."

"There isn't anything to say about it," said Derek, and typed *It's fiction. Tell her it's fiction. If Sudha sends anything else send it to me and I'll deal with it*, and sent it, and put the phone face down.

Lucky stayed until one-thirty.

At the door he said something about the lawn, and about a man he was going to have to speak to about the lawn, and then, with his hand on the frame and no change in volume at all: "Call me on Wednesday."

"I'll call you on Wednesday."

"You won't, and then I'll call you on Thursday," said Lucky, and went down the steps two at a time.

---

She called on the Wednesday at twenty past six in the morning his time, from a car in Fukuoka.

"You've been up," she said.

"I've been up about an hour."

"You're not going to make me do the thing, are you."

"Which thing?"

"The thing where I have to ask you four times." She was moving; he could hear a seatbelt and a road. "Because I've got nine minutes and then a man is going to talk to me about a fragrance."

It was not properly light yet. The window over the sink faces the wrong way for the morning and the yard was still gray out there, and the tree was a silhouette with no detail in it.

Derek stood with a cup in his hand and did not drink from it.

"It's a Three," he said.

She did not go soft, and she did not say *oh, Derek*, and she did not ask whether he was sure.

"Since when."

"Thursday."

"Which Thursday?"

"The one with the man in the car."

"Right. Okay. Is it moving?"

"No."

"Has it moved *at all*, or has it moved and you've decided not to count it—"

"It's sat at a Three since Thursday. It hasn't gone to a Two and it hasn't gone the other way, and I've taken it every morning at the sink and I've written it down." He turned the cup around on the counter. "And I'm eating, and I'm working, and the shelf's in order, and I checked it twice on Sunday, and I'm telling you I checked it twice, because if I don't tell you I checked it twice then the checking twice is the thing I'm not telling you."

A pause.

"*Good*," said Ha-eun.

"Nine minutes."

"Shut up." A door, and then the acoustic changed — she had gotten out of the car and gone somewhere with a hard floor. "Say the whole thing. What does a Three do."

"You know what a Three does. It's in the book."

"It's in the *book*," said Ha-eun. "It's not in you. Rahul said it in a chair on a soundstage and it was very good and it isn't *you*, and I'm not doing the thing where I know you off the page. Say it."

"Things go flat," said Derek. "Food doesn't taste right. I can still do the things I need to—"

"That's the book."

"I know it's the book."

"So say it the other way."

He stood there.

He had two years of work on those sentences. He had built them in a room, alone, at a desk, out of eleven years of taking the reading — and they are the best account of it that exists in any language, and they are the only account he has, and standing at a sink at twenty-five past six in the morning he could not get behind them to whatever they had been made out of.

"I can't," he said.

There was a silence from a corridor in Fukuoka.

"Okay," said Ha-eun.

"I'm not being—"

"I know you're not." Her voice had not changed at all, and that was the frightening part. "Say the arithmetic instead."

"Two weeks at a Three is a Three. Four weeks at a Three isn't a Three any more, it's a slope with a nice view."

"And it's been six days."

"It's been six days."

"So on the twenty-third of June it's four weeks," said Ha-eun, "and I'm going to be in Cologne, and I've just done that in my head and I'm not writing it down anywhere, because if I write it down Ba-da finds it."

"You don't have to—"

"I'm not asking your permission, I'm telling you I've done it." Somebody said her name in Korean, some distance off, and she said something back that was not *coming.* "Derek. If it moves."

"If it moves I'll say."

"Say it properly."

"If it moves I'll tell you the day it moves," said Derek.

"*Good.*"

And he meant all of it, and there was nothing in that morning to suggest it would be a difficult promise to keep.

He did not ring Lucky on the Wednesday.

He had meant to. It was on the list he keeps on the back of an envelope, in his own handwriting, in the order he wrote it in — *Ferreira 4pp / reel 6 notes / L Wed* — and he did reel six and he did the four pages and at eleven at night the envelope was on the counter with the third thing on it, untouched, in his own hand, and he had walked past it about nine times.

Lucky called on the Thursday at twenty past seven in the morning.

"Morning."

"I was going to—"

"I know," said Lucky. "That's why I said Thursday. Right, I've got a thing at eight so this is short. Are you eating?"

"Yes."

"Is it food or is it the thing where you eat the same thing four days in a row and count it?"

Derek looked at the counter.

"It's the second one."

"Right," said Lucky. "Deepa's coming Sunday. She's bringing something with a lid on it and you're going to say you don't need it and she's going to put it in your fridge anyway, and I've told her not to, and she's going to do it regardless, because I have no power in that house." A pause. "Wednesday next week."

"Wednesday next week."

"You won't," said Lucky, and hung up.

She was two minutes late to the fragrance.

He found out three days later from a fan account that was tracking her to the minute that week, which had put a red circle around the time and asked, in Korean and then in English, what she had been doing.

Derek looked at that for a while, sitting down, with a plate next to him that he had made and had not started.

Then he shut the laptop and did four hours on the curriculum, and it was good, and he ate the food at about eleven, cold, sitting on the floor with his back against the cabinet that does not sit flush, and he did not tell her about the red circle.

She had gotten out of a car and stood on a hard floor in a building in Fukuoka for eleven minutes to make him say a thing in his own words instead of Rahul's, and had walked into a room two minutes late to talk to a man about a fragrance, and had not mentioned it and was not going to.
