# 1

The copier on the second floor had been broken since June and the sign taped to it
still said BACK MONDAY, which had been true once.

Derek pressed the button anyway at 7:20 on the first morning, and waited, and listened
to the machine consider it, and watched the panel decide against him. Then he went down
to the first floor, where the good copier lived behind the attendance office, and where
Rosalind looked up at him over her glasses.

"You're early."

"I'm at the end of a long chain of failure."

"Second floor's still out."

"Second floor is a monument, Roz. We should put a plaque on it."

She let him use the good one. She always let him use the good one, because eleven years
ago he had spent a Saturday getting her nephew's college essay into a shape that got him
into Cal Poly, and she had a longer memory than the district did.

Priya found him there with ninety-four warm syllabi in his arms.

"Do you have a second."

"I have four minutes and I'm using them to be smug about paper."

"Rakesh is out." She said it flatly, which was how she said everything that was going to
cost him. "Not out today. Out out. Kidney thing, they're saying December at the
earliest, and the district's given me a long-term sub with a business degree and a
Chromebook."

Derek stopped straightening the stack.

"His sophomores," Priya said.

"You want me to take a section."

"I want you to take his fourth period and let the sub have the rest, because fourth
period is the one that'll eat him, and because you'll say yes."

"That's an unbelievable thing to say out loud."

"I've been doing this nineteen years, Derek. I don't have time to build up to it."

He looked at the copier. He thought about the four hours between 4:40 and 8:40 on a
Wednesday, which was the only structure in his week that was actually his, and about
what a sixth prep would do to it, and about the fact that he had a spreadsheet at home
he had not opened since June.

"Fourth period," he said.

"Thank you."

"I want the good projector."

"You'll get the good projector."

"And I want you to remember this in the spring when I ask you for something."

Priya was already walking. "You never ask me for anything," she said. "That's the
problem with you."

---

Fifth period came after lunch, which meant they arrived warm and loud and slightly
unwell, twenty-nine juniors in a room built for twenty-four. American Literature. Three
of them he already knew. The rest were a wall.

He did not do names first. He waited until they had mostly stopped moving. Then he
picked a book up off the desk and read to them.

*Márgarét, áre you gríeving*
*Over Goldengrove unleaving?*

Somebody laughed, because of the way the accents made him say it. He kept going. He read
it the way it was written, which is to say strangely, with the stresses landing where
Hopkins had hammered them in, and by the fourth line the laughing had stopped, not
because anyone had decided to be respectful but because the noise of it had gotten
interesting.

*Ah! ás the heart grows older*
*It will come to such sights colder*
*By and by, nor spare a sigh*
*Though worlds of wanwood leafmeal lie…*

He read to the end. He let the room sit for a second, which was longer than it sounds.

"Okay. Nobody tell me what it means. That's a trap and I'm not interested. Tell me what
it *sounded* like."

Nothing, for a beat.

"Sad," said a girl in the second row, in a voice that dared him to say it was wrong.

"Sad. Yes. Good. Why — no, sorry, that's the same trap wearing a hat." He put the book
down. "Try this. It sounded sad *before* you knew what any of it meant. Half those words
aren't words. *Wanwood. Leafmeal.* He made them up. And it still landed. So what does
that tell you about how this thing is working on you?"

"That it's cheating," said a boy by the window.

Derek pointed at him, delighted. "It's completely cheating. What's your name?"

"Marcus."

"Marcus says it's cheating and Marcus is right, and that's the class. Everything we read
this year is going to be doing something to you underneath the part you can explain, and
the only real skill I've got to hand you is catching it in the act." He picked the book
back up. "Also, for the record, it's a kid crying about leaves who doesn't know yet
she's crying about dying. Which I know because I'm thirty-five and I read the last line.
It took Hopkins eight lines. It took me two decades. So let's agree the poem is better
at this than we are."

The room laughed. Not all of it. Enough.

While they laughed he crossed to the window and cracked it, which he did not need to do,
and on the way back he stopped at the desk under the clock, where a boy was sitting with
his backpack still on both shoulders and his hands flat on the desk in front of him.

"You want to put that down?"

"I'm okay."

"Sure." Derek moved on without another look, because the kid had gone the specific
colour of somebody who needs the moment to end. "Marcus. Give me a second cheat the poem
pulls. There are four."

He got eleven more good minutes out of them, which on the first day was theft.

The backpack stayed on for the whole period.

---

At 2:52 the bell went and they poured out, and one of them didn't.

He came up the aisle sideways, holding the index card Derek had them fill out — name,
last thing you read, something true — and put it on the desk instead of in the pile.

"You can just put it with the others," Derek said.

"I know." He was already leaving. "I just didn't want it in the middle."

Derek waited until the room was empty. Then he read it.

**TOMÁS BEVILACQUA**, in small print, all caps, like a form.

*Last thing I read: the manual for my mom's car, cover to cover, twice. Not a joke.*

*Something true: everyone in this class has known each other since kindergarten and I
moved here in March.*

Derek sat with that in a room that smelled like floor wax and thirty teenagers.

Then he took a fresh card out of the drawer and wrote on it and left it on the desk
under the clock, face down, for the morning.

*Cover to cover, twice, is called close reading and it's the whole job. Thursday at
lunch — I have something for you and it's too long to explain. Bring the manual. — DR*

---

He got home at 4:40 and ate cereal standing at the counter.

There were four hours until he had to be a different thing, and he spent them badly:
forty minutes intending to grade, a stretch on the floor with his forearm over his eyes,
then a violent hour of competence at seven in which he did all of it, plus the thing he'd
been putting off since June, plus an unnecessary improvement to the script that pulled
his chat logs into a text file, because the improvement was interesting and the grading
was not.

At 8:15 the phone lit up with his brother's name.

He watched it until it stopped. Then he opened his laptop and typed a message to Priya
saying he'd take fourth period from Wednesday and not to worry about the paperwork, and
sent it, and sat back, and looked at the ceiling of the room he had given to the podcast.

Six preps. He said it out loud once, to see how it sounded in the empty apartment, and
then got up to find something to eat that wasn't cereal.
