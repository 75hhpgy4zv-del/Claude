# Chapter 32 — "Five Chairs"
## Sections 9–10 (draft to push against)

*Picks up after Miru's "Hi, Gracie."*

---

Nobody had eaten.

That was Yeong-ju's discovery, and she made it the way she seemed to make most of them, out loud and to the room at large, from inside the catering crate by the door.

"There's rice," she said. "There's the soup. There's — why is there so much fruit."

"Nobody touched it," said Nari.

"Nobody ever touches it." Yeong-ju came out with a container of orange segments, lid half off, and stopped in front of Derek's chair and looked at the problem.

The problem was that both of his hands were in use. One was flat between Ha-eun's shoulder blades, where it had been for eleven minutes. The other was under her knees, because she had arrived in his lap at an angle that required it and had not revised the angle since.

Yeong-ju held up a segment.

"I'm fine," Derek said.

"You flew from Los Angeles."

"I ate on the plane."

"Nobody eats on the plane," said Yeong-ju, and put the orange in his mouth, and he let her, because the alternative was a discussion, and discussions made noise.

---

"Does she eat?" said So-hyun.

She had not sat down. She had taken her own chair — SO-HYUN on masking tape across the back — turned it around, and was leaning on the top of it with her arms folded, which in Derek's experience of rooms full of women who ran things meant the interview from earlier was not finished, only adjourned.

"Yes," he said.

"Properly."

"When she's — " He looked for the word, decided there wasn't one, and used the one he had. "When she's like this, she eats. It has to be cool. Nothing hot. And she'll eat on the floor if there's a floor."

"On the *floor*," said Nari.

"She won't eat anything above about lukewarm. I put ice in the rice. Two cubes, at the edge."

Four people looked at him.

He was aware, the way you are aware of a draft, of having said something that had just gone into a file.

---

Then the hand came out.

It came out of the sleeve of his sweatshirt, which was too long for her and which she had not pushed back, so it emerged the way a small animal leaves a burrow — fingers first, then the rest — and it did not go anywhere near her own face. It went sideways, blind, past his shoulder, and stopped in the air beside Yeong-ju.

Palm up.

It stayed there.

Nobody moved.

"She wants one," Derek said.

Yeong-ju looked at the hand. She looked at the container. She looked at Derek with an expression he could not read at all, and then she took a segment out and put it in the palm, and the hand closed around it and went back into the sleeve, and a moment later, from somewhere near his collarbone, came the small wet sound of somebody eating an orange with her eyes shut.

"Again," said Derek, before the hand came back out, because he knew it would.

It came back out.

---

"She asked," said Nari.

She said it in Korean, quietly, not to him. He answered anyway.

"She does that."

"She doesn't," said Nari. "She doesn't *ask.* She — " and she made a gesture with both hands, a kind of pushing across a table, and it took him a second to understand it was a plate being moved from one person to another. "She gives you hers. Ten years. You say you're hungry in a van and there's a bag of something in your lap before you've finished saying it, and she's already looking out the window so you can't give it back."

"She makes you eat," said Yeong-ju. "She watches your plate."

"She watches *everybody's* plate," said So-hyun.

"Nobody watches hers," said Miru, from the door.

It was the first thing Miru had said since *Hi, Gracie*. She was still standing in the wrong place, half turned to the corridor, and she said it without looking around, the way you say a thing you have known for a long time and never had a reason to say out loud.

The hand came out again. Yeong-ju filled it.

---

So they told him about the dorm.

He hadn't asked. He was careful about that afterward, going over it, because he wanted to be sure. They told him because the hand kept coming out, and each time it seemed to pull something out of one of them with it.

Hapjeong, 2012. Nine girls in a two-bedroom apartment — Nari said "a flat," in the English she had learned from his book, and So-hyun said "*apartment*, he's American," and Nari said "I learned it from *Maya*" and looked mortified — anyway: nine girls, four bunks, one bathroom, and a woman from the company who came on Mondays with a scale.

"Before the scale," said Yeong-ju.

"Before the scale," Miru agreed.

Before the scale, Kang Ha-eun had eaten like a boy. That was the phrase, and three of them used it, and Derek gathered it was the phrase they had used at the time. She ate the ends of everybody's gimbap. She ate standing up. She ate other people's crusts and the skin off other people's chicken, and she had once, notoriously, eaten a family-size bag of shrimp crackers during a single run of a single dance, been sick in the practice-room trash can, and then asked whether anybody was going to finish the bag.

"I was the youngest," said Nari. "I thought she was the most glamorous person alive."

"She had a *rice cooker*," said Yeong-ju.

"Under the bunk," said So-hyun. "Contraband."

"She made rice at two in the morning," said Yeong-ju, "and we ate it under the covers. Cold. With the — " a word in Korean he didn't know, and then, helpfully, "the seaweed in packets. And kimchi from somebody's mother. You couldn't heat anything. It smells. If it smells, they know."

Derek did not say anything.

Somewhere under his collarbone Ha-eun was eating an orange at room temperature, with her eyes shut, in the dark of his sweatshirt.

He had put ice in her rice since February and never once asked her why.

---

"And then the scale," said Miru.

Nobody told that one. They didn't need to. He knew about the Mondays; she had told him once, in a car, in about nine words, and changed the subject. They could see in his face that he knew, and so the story stopped there, at the scale, and nobody went into the part after — which he gathered was the part where the girl who ate like a boy became the girl who gave you hers and looked out the window.

"She stopped," said So-hyun. "We all stopped. But she stopped *first*. And then she made sure we didn't stop too much." She looked at the back of Ha-eun's head for a while. "It took us about three years to notice she never ate in front of us."

"Four," said Miru.

The hand came out. Yeong-ju had run out of orange. She looked at the empty palm with something close to panic.

"Rice," said Derek. "There's rice. It'll be cold by now. That's fine. That's better."

Yeong-ju got the rice.

---

She kicked him around the fourth handful.

Not hard. The heel of one foot against his shin, twice, which meant *slower*, and which he had learned in the kitchen in Alhambra by getting it wrong for about a week.

"Sorry," he said, to the top of her head, and to Yeong-ju: "Smaller."

Yeong-ju made it smaller.

"She *kicks* you," said Nari, delighted.

"Mostly she argues with me."

"About what?"

"Mangoes," said Derek. "Mainly. It's a long story and it's my fault."

---

Then she was asleep.

He felt it before anybody could see it: the grip in his shirt letting go, the whole weight of her arriving at once, the way it arrives when somebody stops holding any of themselves up. Her mouth was open against his collar. The hand that had been coming out of his sleeve for twenty minutes stayed out, palm up, empty, on his knee.

Yeong-ju fetched a coat off the rail — long, black, SONDER in white across the back — and laid it over the two of them, and tucked it in at Ha-eun's side with two fingers, and then stood there with her hands hanging as though she had run out of things to do with them.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

"She doesn't sleep like that," said So-hyun eventually.

"How does she sleep?"

"Sitting up. In the van. Earphones." So-hyun had not taken her eyes off her. "If you say her name she's awake. You don't even have to say it loud."

"Here she sleeps like that," said Derek.

---

Then they asked him the thing he had been waiting for.

It was Nari. It would be; she had read his book with a dictionary. She said, in English, very carefully: "Why is she so thin?"

The room went still in a different way.

He could have answered. He knew the dates. He knew the day she'd stopped being able to get there over a phone. He knew about Fukuoka. He knew a parking lot in Warsaw.

"That's hers," Derek said.

Nari looked at him.

"I'm sorry," he said. "It isn't mine. You'll have to ask her."

He waited to find out whether that had cost him the room.

It was Yeong-ju who answered, and she did not answer him. She was looking at Ha-eun asleep under the coat, and she said, very quietly, in Korean, as though finishing a conversation none of them had started:

"Of course she didn't tell us."

So-hyun turned her chair back around the right way and sat in it.

"Sorry," she said to Derek. "Before. The questions." She looked at her hands. "We just needed to be sure that she was okay."

At the door, Miru looked at Ha-eun for a long moment with her face doing nothing at all.

"She is," said Miru.
