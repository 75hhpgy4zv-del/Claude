# 80/20 — Part One: Chapters 1–7


## Chapter One: The Broadcast

The apartment was the kind of quiet that happens after ten o'clock on a Tuesday in a city that is otherwise always making noise: the freeway a low surf in the distance, the upstairs neighbor's television a muffled heartbeat through the ceiling, the refrigerator cycling on and then off again with the particular domestic sigh of something that has been keeping things cold for a very long time. Derek Krishnamurthy sat at his desk in the amber cone of an Elgato key light, and he was by all accounts, to the eighty-seven people watching him through the internet, having the time of his life.

"Okay, okay, okay. Listen," he said, leaning forward so that his dark eyes caught the light in a way that looked practiced but suprisingly was not, "I need you all to understand that Robocop is not a movie about a robot. That is a surface-level reading that the studio used to sell tickets and that the culture has been lazy enough to accept for forty years. Robocop is a movie about identity. What survives when everything that makes you inconvenient has been stripped away." He paused, picked up his mug of tea, and let the pause breathe the way a good teacher lets silence be it's own event. "Murphy is the empathy. The machine is the institution. The whole film is asking: what does a corporation do to a human soul? And we keep calling it the one with the robot cop."

In the chat, the names scrolled:

> *FilmNerd_Priya: FINALLY someone says it*

> *GunplaGuy88: ok but the ED-209 walking scene alone is cinema*

> *Lumen442: what about terminator 2 tho*

"Lumen," Derek said, reading the screen with the ease of someone who had learned to carry on two conversations simultaneously without losing the thread of either, "Terminator 2 is a masterpiece and I will defend it until the day they put me into the ground, but that's a different thesis. That's about the the line between a human who has been stripped of empathy meeting a war machine learning it for the first time. Tonight we're in the Verhoeven lane. Stay in the Verhoeven lane with me."

He genuinely laughed at something in the chat that had already scrolled past, and a few of the eighty-seven people watching him in their own dark rooms in their own cities probably felt, for a moment, that they were in on something.

This was what Derek was good at. This specific thing.

He had known it since his first year teaching, when he'd stood in front of a class of ninth graders who had every cultural and hormonal reason to be somewhere else, and had managed, through the particular alchemy of caring about the material more than he cared about appearing cool, to make them care too. He could thread a room. He could find the live wire in a text and follow it until the light came on behind someone's eyes. In a different era, in a different family, this might have been called a gift. In his family it was called talking too much.

* * *

He ended the stream at eleven forty-two. He said his goodbyes the way he always did, unhurried, actually looking at each of the names as he said them.

"Goodnight FilmNerd_Priya. Goodnight GunplaGuy. Lumen, I love you but you are wrong about Ant-Man and we've discussed this. Everyone else: genuinely, thank you. This is the good part of my week."

He meant it. That was the thing he would have had trouble explaining to anyone who didn't already understand it: he meant it completely. It was also not the whole truth.

The stream ended. The key light stayed on for a moment, as it always did, and then he reached up and clicked it off.  The room reassembled itself in the ordinary lamplight of a man's apartment at midnight: the shelves of steelbooks in their obsessive rows, spines facing out like the spines of a library curated by someone with a specific and unapologetic inner life. The Criterion editions. The art prints in their matching frames: a Mondo illustration of Chinatown, a Bottleneck Gallery print of Mulholland Drive, a hand-numbered Ghost in the Shell variant he had paid too much for and did not even slightly regret. On the shelf beside them, in a modest disorder: a Raspberry Pi mid-project, a ring dish from his ex-wife's mother that he had kept because it was beautiful and she had given it to him before everything had become what it became.

He did not look at the ring dish. He had become an expert at not looking at it.

He opened the refrigerator, found it in its usual state: half a container of leftover dal, some plain yogurt, a mango in a state of ambiguous ripeness, and a bottle of Topo Chico.  He poured the mineral water into a glass with the slightly ceremonial attention he gave to small things when no one was watching, which was one of the ways the gap reopened between the version of himself he broadcast and the version that remained.

The broadcast version was confident. He was confident. Both things were true, but they were not the same size.

He sat back down at the desk, just because the chair was there and the lamp was warm and the alternative was the bedroom, which at midnight had a quality of accusation he was not in the mood to negotiate with. He pulled up YouTube.

Maya Chen's face appeared in the recommended feed the way it always eventually did. He clicked before making the decision about clicking.

The video was titled: "I tried to explain parasocial relationships to my mom (she did not get it)" and it had four point two million views.

Maya Chen was the biggest female streamer in the world. She had eleven million subscribers on YouTube, four million on Twitch, a podcast, a merchandise line. The kind of online presence that had achieved critical mass.  In other words, she was at the point at which the person and the persona become, to the external world, interchangeable.

Derek clicked play.

She was sitting on her couch in what appeared to be a well-lit but deliberately casual arrangement, the kind that costs a significant amount to look effortless, laughing at something her editor had cut in, and the laugh was both the thing Derek had noticed the first time he'd watched her content and the thing that had made him keep watching. It was not a streamer laugh. It arrived sideways, unannounced, the kind of laugh that happens when something is genuinely funny rather than when you are performing the experience of something being funny. 

"She kept asking me," Maya was saying, wiping at her eye with the heel of her hand, "she kept asking me, 'But are they your real friends?' And I'm like, Mom, it's not... that's not quite the... it's more complicated than that." She composed herself with visible effort. "And she looked at me and she said, 'Maya, I think you need real friends.' And I was like, okay. Okay, Mom. Thank you. Very helpful."

She said it without bitterness, a light smile on her face. There was a quality to the way she said it, head cocked, the absurdity and the genuine sting held at the same time, that Derek found himself watching twice.

He closed the laptop at twelve forty.

He lay in bed with the ceiling above him and the familiar weight that was neither sadness nor loneliness, but lived in the same neighborhood as both, and thought about what she'd said.  The real friends question.  About the eighty-seven people who had watched him tonight and the warmth he had felt for them, and the specific silence that waited when the stream ended.  Just him, and the ring dish, and the mango of ambiguous ripeness.

He was thirty-five years old. He had been cheated on by everyone he'd loved enough to stay with. He lived alone in an apartment full of things he had chosen with enormous care, which is what you do when the people you chose did not choose you back in the same way. He taught English to teenagers who would grow up to remember him, if they remembered him at all, as the teacher who made them read things they hadn't wanted to read and who had, when they finally read them, been right about the thing that those things were about.

He was not unhappy. He needed to be precise about this, because imprecision in the other direction was its own kind of dishonesty. Not unhappy.  The particular condition of a person who has arranged their life with considerable intelligence and care and arrived at something almost but not quite sufficient.

He had been doing the thing where you hold open a space and wonder what might eventually fill it, patient and undestructive, for two years now: since Priya, since the divorce, since the winter after that he didn't discuss.

He had built things since then: the podcast, the classroom, the apartment full of chosen things, and the things were real and they mattered and they were not the same as having someone to call when you found the Coltrane pressing you had been looking for.

He looked at the ring dish on the shelf. He had kept it because it was beautiful and because she had given it to him before everything became what it became, and he had kept it and not thought about why he had kept it, and on some nights, like tonight, the keeping of it said something about him that he was not fully ready to examine.

He fell asleep, eventually, with the fan on and the city continuing its muffled business outside and the ring dish on the shelf and the wondering in the room, patient, as it always was, and uninterested in being hurried.


## Chapter Two: The Performance

Maya Chen had a pre-stream ritual that took forty-seven minutes. She had never described it to anyone. Not because it was secret (it wasn't) but because describing it would make it strange, and she needed it to not be strange. Just a thing she did. Neutral and repeatable.

Like brushing your teeth.

She started with the ring light off and the room in the ordinary afternoon light of her apartment in Burbank, which was the color of November regardless of what month it actually was. She made tea, always loose leaf, always in the same blue ceramic pot her grandmother had sent from Guangzhou two Christmases ago, and she sat with it at the island in her kitchen and she did not look at her phone. This was the hardest part.

This was the part that required the most discipline, because the phone was a current and if she stepped into it before she was ready she would arrive down stream already depleted, already having spent the first increment of herself on someone else's emergency.

She was twenty-six years old. She had been doing this since she was nineteen. She had grown up in the camera.

At forty minutes she went to the second bedroom, the stream room.

No one who visited her apartment ever entered it. No one who visited her apartment was someone she'd met in person before they'd met her online.

She knew this was a fact about her life. She was still deciding how she felt about it. She sat in the chair and looked at the dark monitor and the offline camera and she breathed.

Then she turned the ring light on. And she became, in the illumination, slightly more than herself.

Not fake. She was careful about this distinction. A version of herself: the one that had learned, through seven years of practice, which parts of her were interesting and how to lead with those parts without abandoning the rest. Every person did this. Every person walked into a room and calibrated. The difference was merely the scale.

"Hello hello hello," she said to the camera as the stream went live, and the chat exploded in its predictable and delightful way, three thousand people arriving in the first sixty seconds, the number climbing toward fifteen thousand as the notification rippled out through her community. She smiled, and the smile was real but it was also the smile she had developed specifically for this: slightly warmer than her ordinary smile, slightly more open, the smile of someone who is genuinely glad to see you and has practiced being genuinely glad.

"Okay so I lied," she said immediately, settling into the chair with her tea, "I said we were going to play Balatro tonight but I got into a thing earlier and I kind of want to just talk for a little bit first. Is that okay?"

The chat said it was okay. The chat was always okay with whatever Maya wanted to do, which was something she appreciated and also something she was aware was a form of power that required careful management.

So I had this conversation with my mom," she said, and as she talked she kept one eye on the chat

"She kept asking me," Maya said, "she kept asking me, 'But are they your real friends?' And I'm like, Mom, it's not... that's not quite the... it's more complicated than that." She composed herself, visibly, which was not performance — it was just that even now the memory had a small hook in it. "And she looked at me and she said, 'Maya, I think you need real friends.'" In the chat: a thousand messages arriving in four seconds.

> _RainyDayVibes: SHE DIDN'T_
>
> _Mochi\_Lemon: omg mom chen era_
>
> _xXSilentShadowXx: we are your real friends maya 🫶_

"And I was like," Maya said, letting the chat run another second, "okay. Okay, Mom. Thank you. Very helpful."

 She delivered it without bitterness, because she meant it without bitterness, which was the thing that was hard to explain even to herself: the hook was not resentment. It was something smaller. The particular sting of being known and not quite understood at the same time. "I know, I know," she said, laughing, holding up a hand. "And I love you guys, genuinely. What we have is real." She let that land, let the chat receive it. Then: "It's just, it's a specific kind of real. And I think about that sometimes." She kept it there, didn't pull the thread further. There was a version of that sentence that went somewhere true and useful, and there was a version that turned into content, and she had learned, slowly and not always gracefully, that she couldn't always tell which was which until it was already out of her mouth and in front of fifteen thousand people.

She said it casually, the way you can say true and difficult things when you are in the rhythm of speech and the rhythm is carrying you. The chat received it. The chat always received her.

She played Balatro for two hours after that. She was genuinely good at it, and she lost a run in a way that made her laugh so hard she had to take her hands off the keyboard, and that laugh filled the stream room and the chat erupted and for a long stretch of time she was not aware of herself at all, which was the best thing streaming could do for her and the thing she kept coming back for.

At nine forty-five she said her goodbyes.

"Okay," she said finally, "okay, for real this time. I love you guys. Get some sleep. Drink water. Be nice to someone tomorrow who doesn't expect it." She paused. "Goodnight."

She cut the feed.

The stream room went quiet in the particular way that rooms go quiet when something very loud has just stopped. Not silence exactly. The negative shape of what had been there. The ring light buzzed faintly.

Outside, a car passed on the street, its headlights sliding across the ceiling of the adjacent room in a slow arc, and then it was gone.

Maya sat in the chair for a moment without moving.

The ring light was still on. She reached up and clicked it off.

She was good at this part. The coming down. She had a system for it: ring light off, then the overhead, then the stream clothes into the hamper, then whatever she felt like eating. It was important to have a system.

Without it the end of a stream could sprawl for an hour, checking the clip queue, the comments, the numbers, and then it was midnight and you hadn't eaten and the night had the specific quality of rooms that have held a lot of people and are now empty.

She changed into the oversized UCLA sweatshirt that had belonged to her college roommate. She had never returned it. She and the roommate had lost touch the way she had lost touch with most people from before, not through anything, just the ordinary drift. This was fine. This was how it went. You moved to LA and you built something and the people from before became people you liked on Instagram and that was a kind of connection.

The friend from sophomore year who had screenshotted her texts had found her again a few years ago, slid into the DMs to say she was sorry, that she hadn't understood what she was doing. Maya had written back something warm and meant it. She did not think about it often.

There had been someone she thought she was in a relationship with who had mentioned her, later, in a podcast interview, framed as an anecdote about creative burnout. She had heard the episode driving to a brand meeting and had turned it off. She didn't think about it much.

She ate the leftover scallion pancakes cold, standing at the kitchen counter with her phone face-down. She had a rule about the phone after streams, thirty minutes of not looking at it, which she observed approximately sixty percent of the time. The other forty percent was not weakness. It was the undertow of the thing: the current that wanted to keep going, keep connecting, keep generating the reciprocal warmth that the stream produced. The warmth was real. That was the thing she needed people to understand, or needed to understand herself, on the nights when the gap between the stream ending and the apartment settling was more pronounced than usual. The warmth was real. Fifteen thousand people in a room with her, all of them choosing to be there, and the choice was genuine and so was her gratitude for it and so was everything she said to them. She was not performing. She was a version of herself, yes, but the version had grown from something true and she had been tending it for seven years and she believed in it the way you believe in a garden you planted: the ground is yours, the seeds were yours, the growing was real.

What was also real was the quiet afterward. The negative shape of something that had been there. The room remembering the stream the way a room remembers a party. Without the system the end of a stream could sprawl for hours, checking the clip queue, the comments, the numbers, the secondary warmth of the metric, which was not the same as the primary warmth of the room but was available at any hour and asked nothing in return.

The apartment was nice. Genuinely, thoughtfully nice. She had made it a place where she felt like herself, which was true and also the kind of thing you could say about a very well-designed room that no longer required anything of you. The right rugs. The grandmother's teapot on the shelf, which she used every morning and which was the clearest line between her life now and the life before it. The print she'd spent two weeks choosing, which she still liked, which was the right choice, which sat on the wall and asked nothing. Everything exactly where she'd put it. Everything exactly as she'd arranged it. The apartment full of evidence of her taste and her care and her particular self, and no evidence of anyone else's, because there was no one else's, and she had made it that way, and it was good.

She stood in the kitchen eating the scallion pancakes and she was fine. True fine, not stream fine. Just ordinarily, genuinely fine. The slight hollow quality that fine sometimes had after a very good stream — the room she had filled with fifteen thousand people returned to just her and the counter and the cold food.

Her phone buzzed. Jess.

> *Jess: okay so I just saw the clip of the Balatro run where you lose

and then just kind of stare into space for like four seconds and maya i am obsessed with that clip*

> *Jess: they're calling it the void stare*

> *Jess: "maya chen seeing the abyss" is already a meme*

Maya laughed. Alone in her kitchen, at a phone, she laughed a real laugh and typed back: "that was a $10 run and I was invested."

She sent it and they responded and she sent back and it was good, the easy warmth of Jess, the shorthand of someone who knew her in the way that predated the numbers, or had arrived alongside them, which was the closest she usually got to the same thing. The chat went quiet and she put the phone face-down and finished the scallion pancakes and rinsed the container and stood for a moment in the kitchen in the quiet apartment in the correct and empty room she had made for herself, and then she went to bed.


## Chapter Three: The Actual Person

The Creator Summit happened every year in February at a hotel in downtown Los Angeles that had been renovated recently enough that it still smelled faintly of fresh paint beneath the cedar diffusers that someone had placed in every corridor in an attempt to make ambition smell like a spa. It was three days of panels and networking and sponsored activations and the particular social pressure of being in a room full of people who were all, simultaneously, performing a version of themselves they hoped would be perceived as the most authentic version of themselves, which was, Derek had noted in his podcast two weeks before attending, the most exhausting possible definition of networking.

He was there because a producer he knew had called in a favor. He did not belong here in the way that belonging here was commercially defined. He was aware of this. He wore it lightly, or tried to.

He had dressed with the care of someone who has good taste and has also learned not to care too much about being seen to have good taste: dark wool trousers, a fitted white Oxford, a vintage blazer from Silver Lake that technically was cut for a woman and fit him exactly right, a detail he would not volunteer. His hair did what it wanted, which he had made peace with in his late twenties.

He spent the first day going to two panels and leaving halfway through both. He ate excellent tacos from a truck outside. He had one genuinely good conversation, with a woman who made documentary content about insect biodiversity, which was the kind of conversation that happened when you stopped looking for the professionally useful conversation and just talked to the next person.

On the second evening there was a reception. Open bar, passed appetizers, a DJ playing something inoffensive at a volume calibrated to require leaning in slightly to be heard. Leaning in creates the physical grammar of intimacy. A deliberate social engineering choice. He got a drink and stood near a column, which was a good place to observe from, and observation was one of the things he did with a room before he decided what he wanted to do with it.

Maya Chen arrived with two other people and there was the slight gravitational shift that happened when someone famous entered a room that was full of people pretending not to notice famous people. Derek noticed it without performing noticing it. He had watched enough of her content that he had the strange double knowledge of knowing her face completely and not knowing her at all, which was the specific epistemology of parasocial relationship, and which he was intellectually clear on even as it produced its predictable small effect.

She looked tired. That was the first thing he noticed. She was dressed simply, a cream blazer over a dark top, and she was performing the social duties of arrival with the practiced ease of someone who had done this a thousand times. But underneath the efficiency there was something, not unhappiness, just a kind of low-grade expenditure, like watching a phone that had been at forty percent battery for a while.

He did not approach her. He got a second drink and drifted toward the balcony because the room was warm and the February night air had a quality of almost remembering something cooler.

She was already there.

Not performing. She was out there alone, not on her phone, just standing at the railing and looking at the skyline, which was doing what the Los Angeles skyline does at night, looking like something assembled without a plan by entities that each had excellent individual taste.

He almost went back inside. But the balcony was large and he was within his rights to stand on it, and so he went to the railing about ten feet away and looked at the skyline from his section of it.

A minute passed.

"It's weird, right?" she said.

He looked over. She was looking at him, not the way you look at someone you recognize in a room but the way you look at someone who is doing exactly what you are doing and you have decided to acknowledge it.

"The skyline?" he said.

"The skyline," she agreed. "It looks so deliberate from here. Like somebody planned it. And then you're in it and it's just a CVS next to a parking structure next to a thing they're definitely calling luxury apartments."

"The view is the lie the city tells about itself," Derek said, and immediately felt the slight internal wince of someone who has said a thing that could read as showing off, and then decided it didn't.

Maya looked at him for a moment. "That's a good line."

"I teach English. I'm required to have a certain line-per-hour output."

She laughed, the real one, the sideways one, the one he recognized from screen. The recognition was strange: knowing the sound of it in advance and then hearing it in three dimensions, where it was exactly the same.

"Are you here as a..."

"Teacher who also podcasts in his apartment to about ninety people," he said. "Derek."

"Maya."

"I know," he said. Then: "I've watched some of your content."

"Some," she repeated, with an expression that was entertained rather than offended.

"A modest and non-obsessive amount," he said. "Your bit about the parasocial thing and your mom. I thought it was honest."

She looked at him for a moment. Most people either asked her how that felt or told her she was brave. He had just said what he thought of it.

"Thanks," she said, with the slight flatness of someone who is used to deflecting compliments and is not sure this was one.

"What do you podcast about?" she asked.

"Film, mostly. But I dress it up as film and then it's usually about something else by the end. Last week I spent forty minutes on Robocop and somehow arrived at a discussion of what institutions do to the parts of people that are inconvenient to them."

"I mean," Maya said, "that's what Robocop is about."

Derek looked at her. He felt the specific and slightly ridiculous pleasure of being understood about a thing.

"Yes," he said. "Exactly. Yes."

"My brother was obsessed with Robocop when we were kids," she said. "I watched it with him like six times. I always thought Murphy's wife was the saddest part. Because she doesn't get him back. She gets the thing that looks like him."

The thing that looks like him. Derek held that for a moment. "That's better than anything I said in forty minutes."

"I had better source material. I had a brother who cried at it." She sipped her drink. "Do you like doing it? The podcast."

"I like the part that's just thinking about movies and then talking about what I think. The part where I have to also be a brand is less interesting to me."

"Yeah," she said, with a quietness that had weight in it.

"How long have you been doing this?" he asked. "The streaming."

"Seven years."

"That's a long time."

"It's most of my adult life," she said. "I'm good at it. I like it. It's just also..." She stopped. Started again. "My mom asked me if I have real friends and I put it in a video because when you don't know how to sit with something, you make it content."

She said it with the same tone she'd used in the video, but here, without the audience, it arrived differently. It arrived as the thing it was.

"That's honest," he said.

"I'm trying to be." She said it and it came out slightly more than she'd meant, and she had a brief impulse to qualify it somehow, and then she didn't because qualifying it would be worse.

He didn't make anything of it. Just nodded once and looked back at the skyline.

"Most people," she said, after a moment, "when they meet me, they either go very casual to prove they're not impressed, or they want to establish who they were before they knew who I was. Some version of I- knew-of-you-but-I-wasn't-affected. You didn't do that."

"I watched some of your content," he said. "It just didn't seem like the most interesting thing about you being here."

She considered that. It was a strange thing to say and she wasn't sure she believed it and she also didn't entirely not believe it.

"I should go back in," she said.

"Probably," he said. Neither of them moved.

"There's a Taiwanese place," she said after a moment. "On Ventura.

They have scallion pancakes that I think you would find unreasonably correct."

"Unreasonably correct is a contradiction in terms," he said. "Either they're correct or they're unreasonable. They can't be both."

"Those are just correct," she said. "There's no affront."

"That's a deeply wrong position and I look forward to arguing it with you at length."

She looked at him. She could tell he meant it, which was different from the usual social performance of meaning it, and she felt the familiar small alarm that went off when something was going to cost her something and she couldn't yet tell what.

"Derek," she said.

"Maya."

"I have eleven million subscribers," she said. "Just so you know what you're getting into, with the arguing at length."

"I have eighty-seven listeners," he said. "We're clearly compatible."

The real laugh came sideways, the way it always did. She didn't try to stop it.

"Scallion pancakes are correct," she said. "And this is not a debate."

"It is absolutely a debate," he said. "It just hasn't started yet."

They stayed on the balcony for another twenty minutes, arguing about Robocop and then the scallion pancake question at greater length than it warranted, and neither of them went back inside until someone came to tell her she was needed for a photograph with a sponsor.

When they separated, she to the photograph and him to his jacket, they had exchanged numbers with a pragmatism that was its own kind of statement. No performance around it. Just: this seems worth continuing.

She did not think about it on the way to the photograph. She thought about it on the way home.

He got his jacket from coat check. Outside, the February night was cool and the city doing its indifferent thing, and he stood on the sidewalk for a moment before calling a car. He got in it. The city went past the windows in its usual way, bright and sprawling and indifferent to individual narratives, the way cities have to be to accommodate so many of them at once.

He was already thinking about what he would say in the next episode.

* * *

Three days later his phone buzzed.

A contact saved as Maya Chen (scallion pancakes are correct and this is not a debate).

The message read: "I looked up your podcast. I'm on episode three.

You were right about Robocop."

He was at his desk grading papers. He read the message twice.

Something that he did not yet have a word for shifted very slightly in the direction of open.

He typed back: "Fair warning: episode nine is about Twin Peaks.

You're not there yet but you will be."


## Chapter Four: The Record

The school week after the summit was the usual kind of week, which is to say it was relentless in the particular way that other people's needs are relentless when it is your job to meet them. He taught four sections of eleventh grade English and one elective on film, and he was good at all of them, and being good at them cost something, the way any genuine expenditure does, so that by Thursday afternoon he was operating on the specific reserves that experienced teachers learn to locate in themselves, not energy exactly but the memory of energy, which functions almost as well when the students are also tired and just need someone to care about the thing they are supposedly all caring about together.

He drove to Silver Lake after school because he had told himself for two weeks he was going to stop at that record store on Sunset and had not done it yet, and the not-doing had accumulated into a small but genuine weight, the kind that only lifts when you finally just go.

The store was the kind that no longer had any right to exist in the economic sense and did anyway, which Derek respected deeply. Bins of records organized by a logic that was mostly genre and partly the proprietor's personal philosophy. A cat on the counter. The particular smell of old cardboard and vinyl and the dust of other people's afternoons.

He was in the jazz section for twenty minutes before he found it. The Coltrane he had been looking for, not a rare pressing but a specific one, the kind of specific that doesn't make sense to explain to anyone who doesn't already feel it, and he stood there holding it with the particular stillness of a man who has been waiting for something small and is now experiencing the small thing, and it was, in the way these moments are, genuinely and completely good.

He had nobody to text about it. That was the thought that arrived immediately after, uninvited but not unfamiliar, the thought that arrived after every good thing and some of the ordinary things and occasionally at random in the middle of a Tuesday: I want to tell someone. He had the Coltrane in his hands and the afternoon was doing what Los Angeles afternoons did and the record store had the smell of its own and it was, by any available measure, a good moment. He wanted it to land somewhere.

He wanted it to be witnessed, not by fifteen thousand people in a stream, just by one person who would understand what the pressing meant and why he had been looking for it and what it felt like to be standing here holding it.

Rajan would understand but Rajan was at work. His streaming community would understand but he wasn't streaming. He stood on the sidewalk with the record under his arm and felt the shape of the wanting.

He bought the record. He stood on the sidewalk with it under his arm in its paper bag and took his phone out and looked at the contact saved as Maya Chen (scallion pancakes are correct and this is not a debate) and typed: Found the Coltrane I've been looking for for two years. Just wanted to tell someone who might understand why that matters.

He sent it before he could revise it into something safer and less true.

This specific day, these specific hours. This is a good day.

## Chapter Five: Back Catalog

She had a system for the podcast. She didn't like to come in the middle of something. She had an instinct about architecture — years of watching people enter her own streams mid-context and miss what was being built.

So she had gone back to episode one on the Monday after the summit and was working through them in order. One per morning commute. One per evening when the day felt long enough.

She was on episode nine. He didn't know that.

What she hadn't told him, in the text thread, was what episode nine was doing to her specifically. It was about Twin Peaks (he had warned her) but it was about Twin Peaks in the way all of his episodes were about something else under the surface, which was the thing she had said to him and which she had meant and which she was now experiencing herself as a listener: the feeling of someone working a thing out in real time, following a thread not because they know where it goes but because the thread is warm in their hands.

In episode nine he talked for twenty minutes about Agent Cooper : about the specific quality of Cooper's attention, the way he moved through a world that was horrifying and strange and responded not with armor but with wonder, with a full and open presence that should have been naive and wasn't. And then, almost imperceptibly, the episode turned, and he was talking about what it cost to be that kind of person, that open, in a world that reads openness as weakness and responds accordingly. He didn't make it personal. He never made it personal, not explicitly. But she heard it.

She had listened to that section twice.

She streamed on Wednesday. It was a good stream. She was in a good place that week, lighter than she'd been in a while for reasons she hadn't examined too closely, and her community felt it and gave it back to her amplified. She got deep into the game in the way she got deep into things when no one was measuring, including herself, which the chat recognized because she went quiet, actually quiet, actually playing, forgetting for fifteen-minute stretches that fifteen thousand people were watching.

Afterward, in the gear-down, in the sweatshirt, she sat at the kitchen island and opened the podcast app. Episode ten was forty-three minutes.

She put her earbuds in. She sat in the quiet apartment with her tea going cold and she listened to him talk about Ferris Bueller's Day Off, which was, he said at the outset, his favorite film.

He was talking about Cameron.

She had seen the film, everyone had seen the film, but she had never considered it from Cameron's angle, had never thought about the film as Cameron's film, about Cameron as the person to whom the day actually matters, for whom the risk is real, who has the most to lose and the most to gain and who has been standing at the window of his own life for so long that the window has started to feel like the thing itself.

Derek said, on the recording, his voice slightly lower than usual, the way it went when something was close to him: "Cameron has organized his entire interior life around the conviction that he is not the kind of person things happen to. He's the kind of person who watches. The tragedy isn't that he's wrong about himself. He's been told the right thing by the wrong people for so long that the wrongness has become the furniture. You stop seeing furniture after a while. You just live in the room."

She paused the episode.

You stop seeing furniture after a while. You just live in the room.

Something in it snagged. A small catch in the middle of her chest. The feeling you get when a sentence is about something and you know the something but you can't look at it directly. She reached for it. It moved. She reached again and it moved again — the way a word loses its meaning when you stare at it too long, keeping the shape but not the thing inside.

She looked around her apartment.

The right rugs. The grandmother's teapot on the shelf, blue ceramic, the thin crackling of the glaze she had run her thumb over a thousand times. The print she had spent two weeks choosing and that she still liked, that was still the right choice, that sat on its wall where she had hung it and had become, somewhere in the last year, simply part of the wall. The kitchen island where she ate most of her meals and answered most of her messages and spent most of her alone time. The stream room door, closed, the dark behind it. Everything exactly where she had put it. Everything arranged and tended and correct.

She had made this room. She had chosen everything in it. The version of herself that lived here had good taste and understood light and knew what she needed around her. She was comfortable here. This was her place.

You stop seeing furniture after a while. You just live in the room.

She sat with that for a longer time than she meant to. The cold feeling was still there, formless, not landing anywhere. Then she reached up and unpaused the episode, and the voice came back on, and she let it carry her forward.

Later she was on the couch scrolling without intention, and she realized she was waiting for him to text. She put her phone face-down on the cushion beside her.

Then she picked it up and called Jess instead.

"How are you," Jess said.

"I'm fine. I've been listening to this podcast."

"The English teacher."

"He's also a film critic. Technically."

"Maya." The way Jess said her name when she was bypassing the surface.

"I know." Maya pulled her knees up. "He sees things, Jess."

"You said that before."

"He listens to a film and he finds the thing underneath it and he follows it, and it's never what you thought it was going to be, but it's always more true than what you thought." She paused. "And I think he does that with people too. I think that's just how he moves through the world."

"Is that scary?"

"No," Maya said. And she meant it. She wasn't scared. She tried to locate the word. Interested. That was the word. She was interested in someone in a way that felt simple and uncomplicated, which was not usually how she felt interested in people, but maybe this was just what it was like when there was no professional overlap, no audience to manage around it, nothing at stake. Simple. Uncomplicated. Fine.

"He's probably just good at that thing," Jess said. "The reading-people thing."

"He's not doing it as a thing," Maya said. "That's what I'm saying. He's not performing interest, he's just present. It's different."

"Maya."

"What."

A pause. "Nothing," Jess said. "I'm glad you had a nice time."

Something in the careful neutrality of it made Maya look at her phone for a second. "Why do you sound like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like you're choosing your words."

"I'm just listening," Jess said. And then, after a moment: "I just wonder sometimes, if you've thought about what it costs someone to be that open.

To live the way he seems to live, where everything arrives at full volume and he can't turn it down. And what that asks of someone who gets close."

"I don't know what that means."

"I think you do," Jess said. Not unkindly. "You're very good at seeing things. You're not very practiced at being seen."

Maya held the phone. The instinct came up — find the articulate thing, the processed version, the one with a shape and a conclusion. She clocked the instinct. Didn't follow it.

"Okay," she said. "I hear you."

"Good," Jess said, and they talked about other things for a half hour, and when she hung up Maya sat in the apartment that was exactly as she had arranged it, in the chair she always sat in, in the quiet room, and something Jess had said was still there, not resolved, not attached to anything, just present in the way things are present when they have been true long enough to stop feeling like information.

One episode left in the back catalog before current.

She saved it for the morning. She told herself it was because she wanted to be rested. She wasn't sure that was the reason.


## Chapter Six: The First Drop

The phone call started because she had a question about episode ten.

She texted: can I call you? He said yes. She called. He was in the kitchen making tea and he put his phone on the counter and they talked with the easy unselfconsciousness of people who have already established that they are interested in what the other person thinks.

"Cameron," she said. "You made me think about the whole film differently."

"That's the goal."

"But here's what I want to push on — you say Cameron's been told the wrong thing by the wrong people. But I think the film is also arguing that Ferris is the wrong person to be told the right thing by. Because Ferris doesn't actually see Cameron either. He sees what Cameron is capable of.

But he doesn't see what it costs Cameron to be Cameron. And that's a kind of not-seeing too."

Derek stood very still in the kitchen. The kettle finished. He didn't pour it.

"Say more," he said.

"Ferris drags Cameron through this whole day and it is transformative, Cameron does change, but Ferris gets to go home and slide back into his life and Cameron is the one who has to stand in front of his father's car. The transformation costs Cameron and Ferris is not gone exactly. Downstream. Watching. And there's something about that dynamic that I find —" She paused. "I find it more complicated than the film wants me to find it."

"The film wants you to find it triumphant," Derek said.

"Right. But I watch Cameron kick the car and I think: who is going to be there tomorrow? When the adrenaline is gone and it's just a kid who destroyed something and has to explain it?"

He poured the tea. He sat down at the kitchen table and they talked for another two hours and twenty minutes, at which point it was nearly three in the morning and Maya said she had to be up for a brand call at nine and he said he had first period and neither of them had said goodbye yet.

"I'm going to start episode eleven tomorrow," she said.

"That's the David Lynch survey. It's long."

"How long."

"An hour and eight minutes."

"Derek."

"I had a lot to say."

She laughed, the real one, the sideways one, audible even through the phone at three in the morning — and he was in his kitchen in the dark with his cold tea and the lamp on and the Coltrane still on the shelf where he had put it and everything was, in this specific moment, almost uncomplicated.

"Goodnight," she said.

"Goodnight."

He sat for a while after she hung up. Outside it was past three and the city had stripped itself down to the freeway and the occasional car, and the apartment was warm and full of the things he had chosen and it was, genuinely, a good place to be. He went to bed and fell asleep quickly. The last thing he thought before he went under was the Cameron question. Who is going to be there tomorrow. It sat in him not as a wound but as a good open thing, a question that had air in it.

* * *

He woke up wrong.

That was the only language he had for it: he woke up wrong. Not badly, not painfully, not in any dramatic way that would have been legible from the outside. The alarm went off. He reached for it. He lay there. The ceiling was the ceiling. The room was the room. Everything was exactly as it had been the night before, when the night had been good.

And yet.

He registered this with the flat precision of long familiarity. He knew it was not the ceiling's fault, not the room's fault, not the fault of anything that had happened the night before. It didn't work like that. It arrived on its own logic, its own interior weather system, without consulting the previous twenty-four hours, and his job, the only job available to him, was to notice it and not catastrophize it and get up.

He got up. He showered. He made coffee. He drove to school.

First period was a discussion of the end of The Great Gatsby. He stood at the front of the room and he taught it. He was good at it. He asked the questions he always asked and when the students answered he responded with the genuine attention that was one of the things he actually had to offer, real attention, the noticing of the specific thing a specific student was reaching for, and from the outside he was, by any observable measure, fully present.

From the inside he was watching himself teach.

This was the thing he had never adequately explained to anyone. Not emptiness. This wasn't emptiness. More like a lag. Like a half-second delay between the thing happening and his experience of the thing happening. He would say something and it would be the right thing and then he would register that he had said the right thing, and the registration would arrive with the slight flatness of information received from a distance. He was competent. He was present in the technical sense. He was somewhere slightly behind his own eyes, watching the hands move, watching the mouth form the right words.

He got through the day.

He did not text Maya. This was not a decision. He had not decided not to text her. The phone was there, her contact was there, and he simply did not. The way a body that is hungry will sometimes simply not eat. The signal arrives and nothing departs.

He drove home. He made dinner with the automatic competence of someone who has cooked the same things enough times that the hands know the process without the mind's involvement. He ate and the taste was in the middle distance, the way taste goes when the machinery is running but the occupant is not fully home.

He sat on the couch. He did not put anything on. The television was off, the dark glass of it returning a dim version of the room, a dim version of him, a man on a couch sitting very still in a lamp-lit apartment that was full of beautiful things.

The light outside changed. This was how he measured the time, not the clock but the quality of the light as it moved through the windows from the thin amber of late afternoon to the thicker amber of early evening to the absence that came after, when the only light was what the apartment itself provided. He watched this happen. He was present for it. These were facts.

At some point he was hungry. He knew this the way you know something happening in an adjacent room, audible through the wall, not quite present. He thought: I should eat something. The thought was clear and correct and complete. He did not move. The couch was there. He was on it. The kitchen was twelve feet away. These were also facts.

He thought about calling Rajan. He did not call Rajan. He thought about texting Maya. He did not text Maya. He thought about putting on a film, any film, something to give the room a purpose, and he did not do that either. The not-doing was not resistance. It was the absence of the thing that converts intent to action. The severed signal between knowing and going. He sat with the absence of it the way you sit with a sound you can't locate: aware, unable to do anything about the awareness.

Later he went to bed. He had not eaten. He lay in the dark and the familiar flatness was all around him and he was in it but he was also, somewhere beneath it, still the person who had talked until three in the morning about Ferris Bueller and Cameron and the car. He held onto that the way you hold onto something in the dark when you cannot see it but know where it is.

He fell asleep.

He texted Maya on the drive home: Thank you for this morning.

He put the Coltrane on when he got home. He made dinner. He ate it.

It tasted like itself.


## Chapter Seven: Silver Lake

She suggested his neighborhood because she was tired of being the place people traveled to.

This was the honest reason, not the polished one. Her life had a center of gravity, and the center of gravity was her, and everything and everyone in it orbited accordingly. She was aware of this in the abstract way you are aware of things about yourself that are also just how things are.

She drove herself. Just the 101 south on a Saturday morning with the windows cracked and the podcast in the car speakers, episode twelve, the one she had told him she wanted to argue about. She wore sunglasses and a hat and a jacket that was nondescript enough to do the job. She had learned that most people in most situations were not looking for her specifically, and that the anxiety of being recognized was frequently worse than the recognition itself.

He was waiting outside the record store. He had texted when she was ten minutes out and had simply said: I'm here. She had appreciated the simplicity of it. No instructions, no excessive helpfulness. Just locating himself in space.

He was wearing a dark jacket over a worn gray henley and he looked, she thought, like himself. Which was a strange thought to have, recognizing a person by their quality of presence, but she had now spent enough hours with him across enough formats that she had developed a sense of the shape of him that the visual confirmed rather than constituted. He looked up and saw her and he smiled, which was just the expression of someone who is glad.

"You found it," he said.

"I have GPS like a normal person," she said.

He held the door open. She went in.

The store was everything he had implied it was: the cat, the organized-by-philosophy bins, the smell that was its own argument for the analog. She followed him into the stacks with the easy pace of someone with nowhere else to be, which was itself unusual. She was almost always running slightly late, always one thing behind, always conscious of the next thing on the list. This morning there was no list.

She flipped through a bin without direction. He was one bin over and she could see the shift in him from the corner of her eye. He moved differently in here, with a kind of slow and total attention. He pulled a record out, looked at the cover, put it back. Pulled another. His face did something.

"What?" she said.

"Sorry. Nothing. It's just, there's an Ennio Morricone collection here I've only ever seen once before and that was at an estate sale in Pasadena and I didn't have cash and I have thought about it approximately a hundred times since."

"How much?"

He flipped it over. "Eighteen dollars."

"That's not a hundred-times-thought-about price. That's just buy it."

"I know. I'm going to buy it. I just needed a moment." He was already holding it against his chest with the careful unconscious protectiveness of someone who has found a thing they were afraid they'd lost.

She laughed and went back to her bin. She pulled out a record at random: a twelve-inch with a cover that was abstract shapes in ochre and black. She turned it over. The label meant nothing to her.

"What's this?" She held it up.

He came over and looked. Something happened in his face. "Where did you find that?"

"In the bin."

"That's a Shuggie Otis record. Inspiration Information. 1974. This is one of the loneliest records ever made. He recorded most of it alone in his bedroom over three years and barely anyone heard it when it came out and it became a cult thing decades later when people figured out he'd invented three or four sounds that everyone else thought they'd invented."

He handed it back to her. She looked at it again with the different attention you give to something once it has a story.

"Why lonely?" she said.

"Because you can hear the room. The bedroom, the isolation. He built the whole thing in private and it sounds like that, like something made in a space where no one else was. But it's also gorgeous. The loneliness is in the sound but the sound is beautiful. It's the best argument I know for the idea that making something in private, for no one, is still worth doing."

She bought it. She bought it without deliberating, just took it to the counter with his Morricone and paid for both while he was protesting, which she ignored.

"You didn't have to do that," he said on the sidewalk.

"I know. I wanted the Shuggie Otis. You gave me a reason to want it."

He looked at her. She looked back. in this particular moment, entirely herself and entirely comfortable with it.

"Lunch," she said. "You're taking me somewhere unglamorous."

"I know exactly where," he said.
