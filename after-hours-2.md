## Chapter 1: The Announcement

The email arrived at 9:47 a.m. on a Tuesday.

It was addressed to all cast and senior crew of *Enemies with Benefits*, CC'd to the GMMTV production office, the GMM 25 scheduling department, and someone in Legal whose name JingJing didn't recognize. The subject line read: **EWB SPECIAL EPISODE — PRODUCTION GREENLIGHT & EMBEDDED PRESS NOTIFICATION.**

JingJing read it on her phone, sitting cross-legged on Jan's couch, wearing Jan's oversized Sizzy reunion tour t-shirt and nothing else. Noo-Noo was in her lap. Her hair was still wet from the shower — the shower she had taken alone, because Jan was in the kitchen making Thai tea and had resisted all provocation, which meant JingJing had spent ten minutes under hot water thinking about Jan's hands instead of Jan's actual hands, which was *worse*.

She read it again.

"P'Jan."

"Hmm."

"Come look at this."

Jan appeared in the doorway. Grey joggers. White tank top. Hair tied up in the messy bun she wore at home — the one that showed the clean line of her jaw, the tendons in her neck, the small mole below her left ear that JingJing had catalogued with the obsessive specificity of a cartographer mapping contested territory. She was holding two glasses of Thai tea, the orange so vivid it looked irradiated.

"What?" Jan said.

JingJing held up the phone.

Jan set both glasses down on the side table — carefully, because JingJing had yelled at her twice about water rings on the wood, which was rich coming from someone who left boba cups on every horizontal surface like a breadcrumb trail — and took the phone. Her eyes moved. JingJing watched them move. She always watched Jan's eyes when Jan was reading, because Jan's face did something specific when she was concentrating: the slight furrow, the way her lips parted a millimeter, the unconscious tilt of her head toward the right. It made JingJing want to climb her.

Most things about Jan made JingJing want to climb her. This was a pre-existing condition.

"*Married to My Enemy*," Jan read aloud. "Special episode. Wedding arc. Lal and Wine." A pause. The furrow deepened. "Embedded documentary crew. Journalist assigned to cover the production process from pre-production through broadcast."

"Keep reading."

Jan's eyes dropped further. "The journalist is — Sirin Atthayakorn. She's doing a feature piece for —" Jan looked up. "I don't know who that is."

"She wrote that thing about GL actresses and fan performance. 'The Ship and the Shore.'"

"I didn't read it."

"It was good. It was annoyingly good. She's smart."

"Okay."

"She's going to be on set. Every day. For the entire production."

"Okay."

JingJing stared at her. Jan stared back. There was a quality to Jan's stare that JingJing had never successfully described — patient, faintly amused, waiting for JingJing to arrive at the point while already knowing what the point was but choosing to let JingJing get there on her own, because Jan found the journey entertaining.

"P'Jan," JingJing said, slowly, "we are *engaged*."

"I'm aware."

"We are engaged, and there is going to be a journalist on set, and we are going to be filming a *wedding show*, and —"

Jan sat down next to her. Close. Their thighs pressed together. Jan's hand found the inside of JingJing's knee — casually, automatically, the way Jan always touched her, like JingJing's body was an extension of her own and the boundary between them was an administrative fiction.

"And we'll be fine," Jan said.

"I know we'll be fine. I'm not worried."

"You sound worried."

"I don't worry. I'm not a worrier. I'm a — a problem identifier."

Jan's thumb drew a slow circle on the inside of her knee. "And the problem is?"

JingJing opened her mouth. Closed it. She looked down at Jan's hand on her leg, at the ring on Jan's finger — simple white gold, no stone, because Jan didn't wear stones, because Jan's aesthetic was clean lines and quiet authority and the kind of understated elegance that made JingJing want to ruin her — and then at her own ring, currently on the chain around her neck because she couldn't wear it outside, the diamond warm against her sternum where it lived now, next to her heartbeat.

"There is no problem," JingJing said.

"Correct."

"We just won't do anything stupid."

"We never do anything stupid."

This was — objectively, by any measure, using any definition of the word "stupid" — a lie. They had done something stupid in the dressing room. They had done something stupid under the craft services table. They had done something stupid in a hallway, and on a balcony, and against the wall of a hotel bathroom in Chiang Mai with the window open. "Stupid" was the load-bearing wall of their entire relationship, and removing it would cause the structure to collapse.

But JingJing didn't say that. Because Jan's hand was moving higher — past the knee, along the inner thigh, fingertips tracing the hem of the t-shirt that had ridden up when JingJing sat down — and JingJing's brain was doing the thing it always did when Jan touched her there, which was to short-circuit entirely and redirect all available processing power to the question of whether Jan was going to keep going or stop.

Jan stopped.

"We should call Prae," Jan said, withdrawing her hand like she hadn't just been two inches from changing the entire trajectory of the morning. "Find out the production timeline."

JingJing made a noise. It was not a word. It was the sound a kettle makes when you turn off the heat one degree before boiling — a high, strangled, profoundly unsatisfied noise that communicated everything JingJing would never admit out loud.

Jan smiled. Picked up her Thai tea. Drank.

"I hate you," JingJing said.

"You love me."

"I love you and I hate you and those are the same thing and you know it."

"I do know it." Jan leaned over and pressed her lips to JingJing's forehead — slow, warm, deliberate, the kind of kiss that wasn't about desire but about ownership, about the quiet, daily, unglamorous act of claiming someone. Jan's forehead kisses were JingJing's Achilles' heel and Jan knew it and deployed them with strategic precision.

JingJing's eyes closed. Her whole body softened. Every inch of brat drained out of her like water, and what was left was the other thing — the quiet thing, the soft thing, the thing that curled into Jan's chest and held Noo-Noo and needed nothing except this, exactly this, for the rest of her life.

"We're going to be fine," Jan said against her hairline.

"I know."

"I'll take care of it."

"I *know*."

"Good girl."

JingJing melted. She couldn't help it. She never could. Those two words in Jan's voice — low, warm, with the faintest edge of authority, like a hand on the back of her neck — did something to her central nervous system that eight years of formal education had not equipped her to explain. She pressed her face into Jan's shoulder. Noo-Noo was squished between them. Jan's hand came up to cradle the back of her head, fingers threading through wet hair, and for a long moment the email and the journalist and the special episode didn't exist.

There was only this. The couch. The tea going cold. The rabbit. The ring on its chain. Jan's heartbeat under her ear.

Then Jan's phone rang.

"That's Prae," Jan said, checking the screen. She answered with one hand, the other still in JingJing's hair. "Sawadee kha, Khun Prae. Yes, I saw it. Yes, she read it too. She's — she's right here."

JingJing could hear Prae's voice, tinny and precise, laying out the timeline: pre-production starting next week, table reads the following Monday, costume fittings Wednesday, the journalist arriving Thursday. Sirin Atthayakorn. Three months on set. Full access.

Jan's hand kept moving in JingJing's hair. Slow strokes. Patient. Like she was petting a cat.

"Understood," Jan said. "We'll be professional." A pause. "Khun Prae, when have we ever been anything but professional?"

JingJing — who was currently wearing no underwear, sitting in Jan's lap, with a hickey on her inner thigh from last night that she'd had to cover with concealer this morning — said nothing.

The same desks, the same coffee machine that made sounds like a dying animal, the same wall planner — now blank, waiting for the new production schedule to fill it like a prophecy.

Fah was the first person they saw.

She was standing by the elevator when the doors opened, holding a clipboard and wearing the expression of someone who had been up since 5 a.m. and had already made seventeen decisions before breakfast. Her hair was in a high ponytail. Her lanyard said SENIOR PA in letters that were slightly too large, as if she'd printed them herself to make a point.

"P'Jan. P'JingJing." Fah pointed at Jan's hand on JingJing's back.

Lin closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had shifted — the nonchalance was gone, and what was underneath it was something older, more specific: the memory of a second-floor balcony in Chiang Mai, the sound of a headboard through a hotel wall, the image of two figures pressed together in the dark that she could not unsee and had not mentioned to either of them because mentioning it would mean acknowledging that she'd been standing there, frozen, unable to look away, for longer than she was comfortable admitting.

In the space between their eyes, an entire conversation happened — a silent, panicked, high-speed exchange that covered every near-miss of the past four months, every dressing room lock, every hallway close call, every time Lin had rounded a corner and seen something she had to immediately un-see. *They are going to get caught.*

Lin's hand was shaking around her iced coffee. Three months. Ninety days. Two thousand, one hundred and sixty hours of keeping Jan and JingJing alive despite their best efforts to destroy themselves with the sheer, gravitational, catastrophic force of being in love.

## Chapter 2: Day One

Sirin arrived on a Thursday.

She came quietly — no entourage, no camera crew trailing behind her, just a woman in her early thirties with short hair and wire-rimmed glasses and a canvas shoulder bag that was slightly too heavy on the left side, as if she carried her notebooks stacked on one side and her laptop on the other. She wore a press lanyard that said PRODUCTION ACCESS — DOCUMENTARY in blue letters, and she walked into the production office with the unhurried confidence of someone who had been inside a hundred production offices and understood that the best way to observe a space was to enter it without disturbing it.

Fah met her at the door. Fah was wearing the expression she wore when she was being professionally welcoming while internally running seventeen contingency plans: a smile that was fractionally too wide, eyes that were fractionally too alert, the general energy of a golden retriever who was also a secret agent.

"Khun Sirin! Welcome. I'm Fah — senior production assistant. I'll be your point of contact for scheduling, set access, and any production queries."

"Thank you, Khun Fah." Sirin's voice was warm. Measured. She shook Fah's hand with a grip that was firm but not aggressive — the handshake of someone who didn't need to prove anything. Her eyes moved around the room the way a camera lens rotates: smoothly, systematically, missing nothing.

"I have your schedule here —" Fah began, holding up a laminated sheet that she had clearly spent several hours designing, with color-coded blocks and a legend and a section at the bottom labeled RESTRICTED AREAS (CONSTRUCTION/SAFETY) that was actually a list of places Jan and JingJing tended to be unsupervised. "—and I've reserved a workspace for you in the east wing, which has great natural light and is very close to the —"

"To the coffee machine?" Sirin said, smiling.

"To the coffee machine! Yes. Exactly."

The east wing was also, not coincidentally, the furthest point in the building from the dressing rooms.

"I prefer to float, if that's alright," Sirin said. "I find I get better material when I'm not anchored to one spot. If I could just — move through the building? Sit where there's a free chair? I promise I'm very quiet."

Fah's smile held. Behind it, something cracked.

"Of course," Fah said. "Of course. Absolutely. Float away."

Sirin floated.

She spent the first hour in the production office, sitting in a corner with her laptop, not writing, not recording, just — being present. Watching the rhythm of the space. The way people moved. The way conversations started and stopped. The way the room rearranged itself around certain gravitational centers.

The first gravitational center was Prae, who arrived at 10, black coffee in hand, and began the production meeting with the clipped efficiency of a woman who had directed twelve series and knew exactly how many minutes she was willing to spend on each agenda item, which was fewer minutes than anyone else wanted.

The second gravitational center arrived at 10:07.

Jan and JingJing came through the door together. This was normal — they always came through doors together now, because they always arrived at the building together, because they lived together, because JingJing had moved into Jan's apartment four months ago in a process she still described as "temporarily consolidating logistics" despite the fact that her entire wardrobe, her skincare routine (eleven steps, non-negotiable), and Noo-Noo had been permanently installed in Jan's space since July.

They came through the door, and the room did something.

It was subtle. If you weren't watching for it — if you weren't, say, a journalist who had spent a decade learning to read the invisible language of group behavior — you might not have noticed. But if you *were* watching: a ripple. A collective micro-adjustment. Sixty people, all at once, becoming fractionally more careful. Backs straightening. Eyes recalibrating. The air tightening by one degree, the way it does before a storm that might not come.

Sirin noticed.

She didn't write it down. Not yet. She just filed it — the way a good journalist files a first impression, loosely, without interpretation, the way you pin a photo to a board before you know what the board is about.

Jan was wearing a white button-down, sleeves rolled to the elbow, tucked into high-waisted black trousers. Her hair was down. She looked like an off-duty CEO who had wandered into a creative office and was tolerating it with grace. She was carrying two coffees — one iced Americano (hers), one iced mocha with extra whipped cream and a caramel drizzle (JingJing's), because Jan knew JingJing's coffee order the way she knew her own name: automatically, permanently, without the need for confirmation.

JingJing was wearing an oversized lilac hoodie and denim shorts and platform sneakers that added two inches to her height and still left her four inches shorter than Jan. Her hair was in two small buns — a style she'd started wearing after the engagement, though she would never admit the correlation. She looked like someone who had been up since 7, which she had, and like someone who had been thoroughly kissed in the car on the way here, which she also had, though no one except Jan and the rearview mirror could confirm this.

Jan handed JingJing the mocha. Their fingers touched during the transfer — not accidentally, not intentionally, just *naturally*, the way all touches between them happened now: in the borderland between deliberate and automatic, where everything was both a choice and a reflex.

"Thanks, Daddy," JingJing said.

She said it quietly. Under her breath. Barely a whisper. A private frequency, meant for an audience of one.

But Lin, who was sitting six feet away and had developed the auditory hypervigilance of a combat veteran over the past four months, heard it. Her pen stopped moving. Her eyes closed. A muscle in her jaw twitched.

Sirin, who was twelve feet away, did not hear it. But she saw Lin's pen stop. She saw the jaw twitch. She filed that, too.

"Good morning, everyone." Prae's voice cut through the room like a blade through soft fruit. "Let's get started. Sirin, welcome — I'll do proper introductions after the meeting. For now: we have a production schedule to build and a special episode to shoot, and I would like to do both without losing my mind. Page one."

The meeting covered logistics. Shooting schedule: six weeks, starting mid-September. Location work: the original EWB office set, plus a new wedding venue set being built in Studio B, plus exterior shots at a garden in Nonthaburi — a detail that made JingJing's hand tighten around her coffee cup, because the garden in Nonthaburi was also where she and Jan were planning to get married for real, a coincidence so pointed that it felt like the universe was either testing them or laughing at them.

Jan noticed the hand tighten. Under the table, she pressed her knee against JingJing's. *I know. I've got you.*

JingJing pressed back. *I know you do.*

Sirin noticed the knee press. She noticed it because she was watching Jan's posture change — the barely perceptible lean to the right, toward JingJing, like a plant turning toward light — and she saw the reciprocal lean, JingJing tilting left, the two of them creating a tiny, private geometry that excluded the rest of the room without meaning to.

She wrote something in her notebook. One word. She underlined it.

After the meeting, Prae made the introductions. "Sirin, this is Jan Ployshompoo — she plays Lal. And this is JingJing Prariyapit — she plays Wine. They are the leads. They are very professional. They are very dedicated to their craft."

Prae said this while looking directly at Jan, with an expression that communicated, in the dialect of directors who have been through too much: *Be professional. Be dedicated to your craft. For the love of god, keep your hands where I can see them.*

Jan received the look and did not decode it, because Jan's attention was on Sirin — assessing, the way Jan assessed everyone, with the quiet, competent gaze of someone who took the measure of every room she entered. Jan saw: intelligence. Warmth. The wire-rimmed glasses that were slightly too stylish to be accidental. The canvas bag. The notebook, already open, a pen clipped to the spine.

"Khun Sirin," Jan said, extending her hand. "Welcome to the madhouse."

"Thank you, Khun Jan. I'm a fan of the original series. The dynamic between Lal and Wine is — it's special. There's a quality to your chemistry on screen that's hard to fake."

"We're good actresses," Jan said, smiling.

"Obviously," Sirin said, smiling back.

There was a beat. Not long. The kind of beat that exists between two people who are both very good at reading subtext and are, in this moment, reading each other.

JingJing appeared at Jan's elbow. "Hi. I'm JingJing. I play the cold one."

"You don't seem cold," Sirin said.

"I'm a good actress," JingJing said, echoing Jan's line, and Sirin laughed — a real laugh, not a polite one — and JingJing felt a flush of the competitive warmth she always felt when she made someone laugh, the bratty satisfaction of *I'm charming and I know it and you can't help liking me*.

"I'd love to do individual sit-downs with both of you," Sirin said. "No cameras for the first round — just conversation. Getting a sense of who you are outside the characters. Would that be okay?"

"Of course," Jan said.

"Absolutely," JingJing said.

Under the table, JingJing's fingers found the chain around her neck. The ring was there, warm from her skin, hidden under the lilac hoodie. She touched it without thinking — a gesture so automatic that she didn't register doing it, the way Catholics touch a rosary, the way a soldier touches a photograph, the way anyone touches the thing that means *mine, ours, real*.

Sirin's eyes tracked the movement. The hand going to the chest. The fingers finding something under the fabric. The fleeting softness on JingJing's face.

She didn't write it down. But she noticed.

She always noticed.

The rest of Day One was a masterclass in collective stress management — specifically, the kind of stress management where one side is managing stress and the other side doesn't know there's stress to manage.

Jan and JingJing moved through the building the way they always did: together. Not performatively together — not linked at the hip, not finishing each other's sentences, not doing any of the things that couples in dramas did to signal coupledom. Just *together*, in the way that two people who have been sharing a bed and a bathroom and a life for months are together: in sync without trying, orbiting each other without conscious effort, their bodies making a hundred tiny adjustments per hour to maintain proximity — an arm shifting to make room, a step sideways to fall into pace, a tilt of the head to hear better, each one a small, private act of devotion disguised as spatial logistics.

They didn't notice they were doing it. They never noticed.

Everyone else noticed.

Fah noticed from the production desk, where she was tracking Sirin's movement through the building on a mental map that was growing more detailed and more paranoid by the hour. Fah had already identified three sightline corridors where Jan and JingJing's typical path overlapped with Sirin's documented patrol pattern, and she had texted both Lin and Prae about each one, with diagrams.

Lin noticed from the rehearsal room, where she was running lines with the secondary cast while keeping one eye on the hallway window, through which she could see the back of Jan's head and the side of JingJing's face and the distance between them, which was — Lin checked — approximately four inches. ## Chapter 4: The Fitting

## Chapter 8: Sirin Notices

Three weeks in, Sirin had a board.

Not a physical board — she didn't pin things to walls like a conspiracy theorist or a TV detective. She had a document. A running file on her laptop, organized by date, cataloguing observations the way a biologist catalogues specimens: with precision, without judgment, each entry a data point waiting for a pattern to emerge.

The observations were not about Jan and JingJing.

That was the thing. That was the thing that made Sirin better at this than most people would have been — she had spent three weeks watching the leads, watching them on set and off set, watching them rehearse and eat and walk and talk, and what she had observed was: nothing. Or rather — everything that was consistent with two actresses who had extraordinary chemistry and a warm professional relationship and the comfortable intimacy of colleagues who spent a lot of time together.

Jan and JingJing did not behave like people hiding something. They behaved like themselves. They touched each other constantly — but GL lead pairs often did. They arrived together — but they'd been open about carpooling since the original series. They shared food — but that was Thai. They looked at each other with an intensity that bordered on combustion — but that was acting. Maybe. Probably.

If Sirin had been looking at Jan and JingJing alone, she might have filed the story under "compelling chemistry, plausibly professional" and moved on.

She was not looking at Jan and JingJing alone. She was looking at the ecosystem around them. And the ecosystem was *screaming*.

The document:

*Sept 15: Fah appears at the dressing room door three times in one hour. Each time, she knocks, waits, and enters only after Jan responds verbally. No other talent's dressing room receives this protocol.*

The question I asked was: "They seem very close." This should not be a dangerous question.*

Sirin closed her laptop. Sat back. Looked at the ceiling of the small workspace she'd claimed in the production office — the one Fah had offered her in the east wing, far from the dressing rooms, far from the hallway, far from everywhere Jan and JingJing tended to be alone.

Far from everything.

That, too, was a data point.

On Thursday, Sirin changed her approach.

She stopped watching Jan and JingJing. She started watching Lin.

Lin was the weak point — not because Lin was careless, but because Lin was *honest*. Lin's face did not know how to lie. Lin's body did not know how to pretend. But the timing was specific: Lin left when Sirin arrived, and only when Jan and JingJing were also present. When Jan and JingJing were absent, Lin stayed.

The inference was simple: Lin was not avoiding Sirin. Lin was avoiding the *combination* of Sirin and Jan and JingJing. She was removing herself from the equation that she considered most dangerous: journalist + subjects + witness.

A witness to what?

Sirin didn't know yet. But she knew the shape. She could feel it — the contour of the unspoken, the architecture of the secret, defined not by what people said but by what they didn't say and by how carefully they didn't say it. Sixty people in a production, all performing the same coordinated ignorance, all flinching at the same cues, all moving with the same synchronized avoidance pattern.

Sirin had spent a decade writing about the space between performance and truth. She knew what performance looked like from the inside — the effort of it, the seams, the places where the stitching showed. And what she was seeing on the set of *Married to My Enemy* was not two performers hiding something. It was an entire production hiding something *about* two performers who didn't seem to know there was anything to hide.

That was the strangest part. That was the piece that didn't fit.

Jan and JingJing were not performing. They were not careful. They were not calibrated or controlled or any of the things people were when they were protecting a secret. They were — natural. Easy. Unselfconscious in a way that made the crew's hypervigilance even more conspicuous, because the contrast was so stark: the leads gliding through the building like they were in their own private world, and the crew orbiting them like a defense system, intercepting threats the leads didn't even see.

If Jan and JingJing were hiding something, they were doing it without trying. Which meant either they were the best actors Sirin had ever encountered — better on screen than off, which was impossible — or they weren't hiding anything at all.

Or — and this was the possibility that kept Sirin up at night, that made her stare at the ceiling and turn the stone over and over — they had something to hide but they didn't *know* they needed to hide it. They were walking through a minefield and they thought it was a garden.

And sixty people were trying to clear the mines before they stepped on them.

The picture was almost complete.

## Chapter 9: The Rehearsal

The table read for the vow scene was scheduled for Tuesday at 10 a.m. in the main rehearsal room — a large, windowless space with good acoustics and bad lighting and a conference table that seated twenty and a whiteboard where Prae had diagrammed the blocking in three colors of dry-erase marker.

The vow scene was the centerpiece of *Married to My Enemy*. Twelve pages of script. Lal and Wine, standing in a garden — the garden, the Nonthaburi garden, the same garden where Jan and JingJing would stand in seven weeks — exchanging vows they had written themselves. The script called for emotion but not sentiment, honesty but not melodrama, the specific kind of vulnerability that came from saying something true in front of people who mattered.

Everyone was there. Prae at the head of the table. The secondary cast — including Kapook, who was reading Tangkwa's reaction shots, and Ciize, who was reading Proud's and who had arrived from the Ditto set across the lot with the harried energy of someone who was living in two shows at once and sleeping in neither. Lin, reading her supporting role. Fah, in the corner with the clipboard, vibrating at her baseline frequency of controlled crisis. The script supervisor. The continuity editor. Two camera operators who needed to understand the emotional arc for their shot planning.

And Sirin. In her usual spot. Chair against the wall. Notebook open. Pen uncapped.

Lin had tried to exclude Sirin from the table read. She had made the case to Prae — privately, in a supply closet, at 7 a.m. — that table reads were sacred, that actors needed to feel safe to be vulnerable, that the presence of a journalist would compromise the emotional authenticity of the performance.

Prae had listened. Considered. Said: "You're right. And we can't exclude her. Her access agreement specifically includes table reads. If I block her now, she'll know I'm blocking her, and she'll wonder why, and wondering why is worse than watching."

"Worse for whom?" Lin had asked.

Prae hadn't answered.

Now Lin sat at the table with her script open and her hands folded on top of it and the specific, rigid composure of someone who had taken a Xanax at 6 a.m. and was hoping it would last until noon.

Jan and JingJing arrived together. As always. Jan carrying both coffees. JingJing half a step behind, wearing an oversized cardigan that swallowed her hands and made her look — as Jan privately, silently, hopelessly noted — like a child being swallowed by a blanket. The keychain Noo-Noo was clipped to the inside of her bag, which was on the floor next to her chair, positioned so the bag was open and the keychain was visible — to JingJing — if she looked down.

She would look down. During the hard parts.

"Good morning," Prae said. "Let's begin. Page forty-seven. The garden. Exterior. Day. Lal and Wine face each other. The officiant has stepped back. The vows are unscripted — the characters have written them privately, and neither knows what the other will say. This is the emotional apex of the special. I need truth. I need rawness. I need you to forget the room is here."

Jan opened her script. The page was marked — a small pencil star in the margin, the kind of mark Jan made when a passage mattered to her more than professionally. The stage direction read: *Lal takes Wine's hands. Holds them. Looks at her. Speaks.*

Jan looked up from the script. Across the table. At JingJing.

JingJing was looking at her script. Reading the words. Her lips were moving slightly — a silent run-through, the actor's habit of tasting the lines before saying them aloud.

"When you're ready, Jan," Prae said.

Jan was ready. Jan had been ready for this moment since the beach at Hua Hin, since the ring, since the day she understood that the vows she was writing for Lal were the vows she was writing for JingJing and the only difference was the name.

She took a breath. And she read:

"Wine. I spent a year pretending I didn't want you. I was — I was good at it. I was professional. I was controlled. I stood on my side of the room and you stood on yours and the distance between us was a performance I gave every day, a show I put on for everyone including myself, and the whole time — the *whole time* — the distance was a lie. Every argument was a love letter. Every insult was a confession. Every time I said 'I hate you,' what I meant was 'I want you so badly it feels like hate, because wanting someone this much is supposed to be painful, it's supposed to cost something, and the cost was pretending I didn't.'"

Jan's voice was steady. Clear. The voice of someone who had rehearsed this — not the lines, but the truth underneath them, the truth she had been rehearsing since the day she met JingJing and understood, in the way you understand a natural law, that her life was about to be reorganized around this person.

The room was silent.

"And now I'm standing here," Jan continued, "and I'm done pretending. I'm done performing distance. I'm done being controlled and professional and careful, because careful is another word for afraid, and I'm not afraid anymore. I'm not afraid of wanting you. I'm not afraid of people seeing that I want you. I'm not afraid of any of it, because the only thing I was ever really afraid of was losing you, and you're here, and you're staying, and I—"

Jan stopped. The script said more. The script had four more sentences — careful, crafted, balanced sentences that Snow Leopard had written with precision and care.

Jan didn't read them.

Jan looked at JingJing across the table and said something that was not in the script. That had never been in the script. That came from the place below the character, below the performance, below the architecture of acting, from the bedrock where Jan lived:

"I never hated you. I wanted you so badly it felt like hate."

The room didn't breathe.

JingJing's hands were flat on the table. Her script was forgotten. Her eyes were full — not the performative wet, not the actor's shimmer, but the real thing, the tears that came from the place where JingJing was not JingJing-the-performer or JingJing-the-brat or JingJing-the-brand but JingJing-the-woman who had said *don't you dare stop* in a dressing room and had meant it for the rest of her life.

"That's not in the script," JingJing whispered.

"No," Jan said.

"P'Jan—"

"Read your vows. Wine's vows. Read them to me."

JingJing looked down at her script. The words swam. She blinked — once, twice — and the words clarified, and she read:

"Lal. I was cold because warm was too dangerous. I was strict because loose meant falling, and falling meant — meant trusting someone to catch me, and I didn't trust anyone. I built walls. I organized my life into spreadsheets and protocols and rules, and every rule was a way of saying 'don't touch me, don't see me, don't get close enough to find out what's underneath the cold, because what's underneath the cold is—'"

JingJing's voice cracked.

"'—is someone who loves you so much she doesn't know how to say it without the walls. Someone who needs the walls because without them the love is too big. It's too much. It fills every room I'm in, it takes up every thought I have, it makes me want to do things I've never done — be vulnerable, be soft, be *seen* — and those things terrify me, Lal. Being seen terrifies me. But you see me anyway. You've always seen me. Through the cold and the walls and the spreadsheets and the bullshit, you've always — you've always seen the person I actually am, and you loved her, and that's—'"

JingJing stopped reading. Her hand went to her chest. Found the chain. Held the ring through the fabric.

"That's the bravest thing anyone has ever done for me," she finished. The words were in the script, but the way she said them was not. The way she said them was the way she said *I love you* at 3 a.m., in the dark, in their bed, with Noo-Noo under her arm and Jan's heartbeat under her ear — small, honest, undefended.

The room was silent. Not the silence of a rehearsal pause. The silence of a room full of people who had just witnessed something they weren't supposed to see — something private, something real, something that had used the script as a vehicle but had been driving toward its own destination the whole time.

Prae was the first to move. She looked at her own hands. Then at Jan. Then at JingJing. Then at the script, which was irrelevant now, which had been made irrelevant by whatever had just happened between her two leads across a conference table.

"That," Prae said carefully, "was very good."

"Thank you," Jan said. Her voice was normal. Her ears were red.

"We'll work with that in blocking. The off-script line — 'I wanted you so badly it felt like hate' — I'm keeping it. I'll talk to the writers."

"Okay."

"Let's move to the reaction shots. Lin — page fifty."

Lin did not respond. Lin was sitting very still at the end of the table, her script untouched, her pen motionless, her face the specific color of someone who was either going to cry or faint and hadn't decided which. Her eyes were fixed on a point somewhere between Jan's left ear and the whiteboard, a neutral zone where she didn't have to look at either of them directly.

"Lin," Prae said.

"I need — I'm sorry. I need a minute."

Lin stood. Her chair scraped. She walked toward the door — not fast, not slow, the careful, measured pace of someone whose knees were not entirely reliable — and left.

Sirin watched her go.

In her notebook, she wrote: *Lin left the room during the vow scene rehearsal. She was not acting. She was REACTING — to something the vows meant to her, something that exists outside the script. Her response was not aesthetic (moved by good writing) but personal (moved by something true). She knows something. She is carrying something. It is heavy.*

Sirin closed her notebook.

And wrote one more line: *"I never hated you. I wanted you so badly it felt like hate." Off-script. Jan's own words. Note where this line appears in the final documentary.*

That night, in the apartment, Jan and JingJing wrote their real vows.

They did it separately. Different rooms. This was the tradition — the only tradition they'd agreed on, the one point of wedding planning where they had been absolutely, inflexibly unified: the vows would be private until the moment they said them. No drafts. No previews. No sneaking looks at each other's notebooks. The vows would be heard for the first time in the garden, in front of twelve people, and the surprise was sacred.

Jan sat at the kitchen table. A notebook open. A pen in her hand. The pen was from the hotel in Chiang Mai — she'd taken it without thinking, a souvenir from the trip where everything changed, from the balcony where Lin almost caught them, from the night Jan realized that what she felt for JingJing was not a manageable fire but a wildfire, the kind that remade landscapes.

She wrote slowly. Carefully. Each word weighed, tested, held up to the light.

*JingJing. I —*

She crossed it out. Started again.

*The first time I saw you, I thought —*

No. Too sentimental. Too expected. JingJing would roll her eyes. JingJing would say "P'Jan, you're not a greeting card" and she'd be right.

*JingJing. You are the worst person I've ever met.*

Jan stopped. Looked at the sentence. Smiled.

*You are the worst person I've ever met, and I mean that as the highest compliment I know how to give. You are loud when silence would be easier. You are soft when hardness would be safer. You argue with me about things that don't matter and refuse to argue about things that do, which means I always know what you actually care about, because the things you go quiet about are the things that mean the most.*

*You stole my favorite t-shirt and you've never given it back. You eat cereal out of the box with your hands. You can't be quiet — during anything, ever, for any reason — and I used to think that was a flaw and now I think it's the bravest thing about you, because being loud means being heard, and being heard means being known, and you have never, not once, been afraid of being known.*

*I have been afraid of being known my entire life. And then you said "don't you dare stop" and I didn't, and I haven't, and I won't.*

*I love you. I love Noo-Noo. I love your eleven-step skincare routine and your platform sneakers and the way you cry at things that aren't sad. I love the way you say "Daddy" — yes, that way, and also the other way, and also every way, because every time you say it you're giving me something you don't give anyone else, and I will spend the rest of my life earning the trust that word contains.*

Jan put the pen down. Read it back. Changed nothing.

*Jan. P'Jan. Daddy. I have three names for you and they're all the same person and that person is my whole world and I want that on the record.*

*I didn't know I was looking for you. I didn't know I needed someone who could hold both my wrists in one hand and also hold me when I cry and also make me coffee exactly how I like it without asking and also not flinch when I tell them I love them in front of other people. I promise to say your name — all three of them — every day, in every way, until the word itself is worn smooth from use and all that's left is the feeling, which is: home.*

"Did you?"

"I would never."

"Liar. You peeked at my Christmas list."

"I did."

"No."

Jan held her and listened to the rhythm and felt the ring on the chain pressing against her chest — JingJing's ring, warm from JingJing's body, the small, hard proof of a future they were building one hidden day at a time.

"Goodnight, Daddy," JingJing murmured, already half gone.

"Goodnight, baby."

"Goodnight, Noo-Noo."

Noo-Noo, on the headboard shelf, faced the wall. Seeing nothing. Knowing everything.

## Chapter 10: The Close Call

It happened on a Wednesday. 2:17 p.m.

Later, Fah would pinpoint the exact time because the security cameras had timestamps and Fah had, without telling anyone, begun reviewing the hallway footage each evening with the obsessive thoroughness of a detective building a case — not against Jan and JingJing, but against the *possibility* of their exposure, a probability matrix she was constructing in real time, adjusting for variables like Sirin's position, crew traffic patterns, and the statistical likelihood that any given ten-minute window would be the one where everything fell apart.

2:17 p.m. Wednesday. The dressing room.

Jan and JingJing had been filming the scene where Lal surprises Wine with a prenup — a comedic scene, light, the kind where Wine's control fractures into disbelief and Lal's charm fractures into nervousness and the audience fractures into laughter. They'd done six takes. Prae wanted a seventh. Jan asked for a five-minute break.

"Five minutes," Prae said. "Five. Not ten. Not fifteen. Five."

"Five," Jan confirmed.

They went to the dressing room. Together. As always.

The dressing room was a small space — eight by ten, mirror-lined, one loveseat, one vanity, a garment rack with their costumes, and a door with a lock that Jan engaged approximately seventy percent of the time when they were alone, a percentage that had been declining steadily since the engagement because Jan's attention to locks had been replaced by her attention to JingJing.

Today, the lock was not engaged.

JingJing sat on the loveseat. Jan stood by the vanity, drinking water, checking her phone. Normal. Professional. Two colleagues taking a break.

"That last take was good," JingJing said.

"The third one was better. You did the thing with your eyes."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at me like you're trying to decide between kissing me and killing me and you can't tell the difference."

"That's called acting, P'Jan."

"That's called Tuesday."

JingJing threw a makeup sponge at her. Jan caught it without looking — a reflex, the hand-eye coordination of someone who had been catching things JingJing threw at her for a year and had developed the peripheral awareness of a fighter pilot.

"Come here," JingJing said.

"We have four minutes."

"I need two."

"Two minutes is not enough for what your face is suggesting."

"My face isn't suggesting anything."

"Your face is suggesting things that would get us arrested in several provinces."

JingJing bit her lower lip. She did this on purpose — she had learned, through careful experimentation, that the lip bite was Jan's specific weakness, the visual stimulus that bypassed Jan's formidable self-control and went directly to the part of Jan's brain that made decisions with parts of her body that were not her brain.

Jan's jaw tightened.

"JingJing."

"Hmm?"

"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't — *that*. The lip thing. We're on set."

"I'm just sitting here." JingJing's voice was pure innocence. Spun sugar. The voice of someone who had never had a sinful thought in her life, despite the fact that she was currently calculating the exact angle at which to tilt her head to give Jan a clear view of the spot below her ear that Jan liked to bite.

She tilted.

Jan crossed the room in two steps.

The kiss was immediate — not gradual, not building, but the full, urgent, time-compressed kind that happened when they had four minutes and zero impulse control. Jan's hands cupped JingJing's face, thumbs against her cheekbones, tilting her up, and JingJing's mouth opened for her because JingJing's mouth always opened for her, because there was no resistance that survived first contact with Jan's intent.

JingJing's arms went around Jan's neck, pulling her down onto the loveseat, and then Jan was on top of her, the full weight, the solid press of Jan's body against hers, and JingJing's legs wrapped around her and her hips rolled — not subtle, not ambiguous, a full, deliberate grind against the hardness she could feel through Jan's costume trousers.

"We have three minutes," Jan breathed against her mouth.

"Then be fast."

## Chapter 11: The Magazine

The studio was in Thonglor — a converted warehouse with exposed brick and polished concrete floors and floor-to-ceiling windows that let in the kind of natural light that photographers described as "golden" and that JingJing, watching from a folding chair in the far corner behind a rack of styling options, described as "the light in which a man is going to hold my fiancée's hand while I sit here and develop an ulcer."

The magazine was *L'Officiel Thailand*. The spread was a six-page feature — three editorial shots, two portraits, one cover image — themed around "Modern Elegance, Unexpected Pairings." Feminine and masculine. Structured and fluid. The electricity of opposites.

The male model's name was Golf.

Golf was six foot one. Golf had cheekbones that looked computer-generated. Golf had the kind of body that suggested he had a personal relationship with both a gym and a deity. Golf was twenty-four years old and had been modeling since he was seventeen and had the easy, detached professionalism of someone who had spent his adult life being positioned next to beautiful people and holding poses and then going home and not thinking about any of it.

Golf was, by every objective measure, gorgeous.

JingJing hated him.

Not personally. JingJing had no personal feelings about Golf whatsoever — Golf had said "nice to meet you" and shaken her hand with the limp, pleasant grip of a man who met a hundred people a week and forgot them all by Tuesday, and JingJing had said "nice to meet you too" and smiled her professional smile and then gone to her corner and opened her chat with Love and begun typing with the controlled fury of someone defusing a bomb.

## Chapter 12: The Article Draft

Prae read the draft on a Thursday afternoon, in her office, with the door closed and a glass of iced Americano sweating on a coaster she hadn't looked at in thirty minutes.

Sirin had emailed it at 2 p.m. with a note: *Khun Prae — attached is the first section of the documentary feature. It covers the first three weeks of production. I'd welcome your thoughts before I continue. As discussed, this is a first pass — the final piece will include the full production arc through the premiere. No rush, but I'd love to talk before end of week.*

The piece was titled "Between the Lines: A Season Inside *Married to My Enemy*."

Prae read it once. Read it again. Put the glass down. Picked it up. Put it down again.

The writing was exceptional. That was the first problem.

Sirin wrote the way she spoke — with precision, with warmth, with the specific, devastating clarity of someone who saw things other people didn't see and could describe them in a way that made the reader see them too. She had the instinct for detail that separated great journalists from good ones: the ability to identify the single observation in a crowded room that told you everything, to isolate the moment that contained the truth even when the truth was hiding.

The piece opened with the set itself. A paragraph about the *Married to My Enemy* production design — the garden set being built in stages, the costume department's interpretation of Lal and Wine's wedding looks, the collaborative energy of a crew returning to a world they'd already built once for *Enemies with Benefits*. Standard feature material. Well-written, richly observed, completely safe.

Then it shifted.

*The chemistry between Ployshompoo Supasap (Jan) and Prariyapit Yu (JingJing) is, by now, well-documented. Two seasons of fan accounts, trending hashtags, and viral compilations have established them as one of GL's most compelling pairs — on screen and in the public imagination. What is less documented, because it is harder to photograph, is the way the space around them changes when they are together.*

*I do not mean this metaphorically. I mean it literally, physically, as observable fact. When Jan and JingJing are in a room, the room reorganizes itself around them. Not because they demand attention — they are remarkably unselfconscious, more comfortable with each other's proximity than most married couples I've observed in two decades of reporting. It is the people around them who change. The crew. The production staff. The director herself.*

*They move differently when Jan and JingJing are together. Not cautiously — protectively. There is a choreography to it — a wordless, coordinated shifting of bodies and sightlines that I have never seen in any production of this scale. It is as if the crew has developed a collective instinct — a sixth sense for the moments when Jan and JingJing's natural intimacy reaches a frequency that might, in another context, raise questions.*

*The questions, I should note, are the obvious ones. The ones the fans ask. The ones the press asks. The ones the internet asks with increasing urgency every time a new fan-taken photo surfaces of two women who look at each other the way most people look at a sunset — with awe, and wonder, and the quiet awareness of something beautiful that won't last.*

*I am not asking those questions here. I am asking a different one: what does it mean when sixty people organize their days around the emotional weather of two?*

Prae stopped reading. Looked at the ceiling. The Americano was now room temperature.

She read the next section. It was about the table read.

*The vow scene rehearsal is, in twenty years of arts reporting, one of the most remarkable pieces of acting I have witnessed — if "acting" is the right word. I am not certain it is.*

*Jan read Lal's vows with the steady, amber-eyed intensity that has made her one of the most compelling performers of her generation. And then she went off-script. She said something that was not in the screenplay, something that carried a weight the written vows did not: "I never hated you. I wanted you so badly it felt like hate."*

*The line is beautiful. It is also specific in a way that fictional dialogue rarely is — specific to a particular kind of longing, a particular kind of revelation, the kind that comes not from imagination but from memory. Whether the memory belongs to Jan or to Lal is, perhaps, the wrong question. The better question is whether there is still a difference.*

*JingJing's response — her hand going to her chest, finding something under her clothing, holding it — was not in the stage directions. Neither was Lin's departure from the room. Neither was the silence that followed, which lasted longer than any scripted pause I have ever witnessed and which no one, including the director, broke.*

*What I remember most is not the words. It is what happened in the room after the words. Sixty people held their breath. Not because the writing was good — it was — but because something true had entered the space, something that exceeded the script, something that the room recognized without naming.*

*Something the room was protecting.*

Prae closed the laptop. Opened it. Closed it again.

The piece went on for another three pages — observations about the costume fittings, the fan meet, the Instagram strategy, the careful curation of a public image that Sirin described as "the architecture of ambiguity." Each section was the same: beautifully written, deeply observed, technically about the production, and circling — always circling — the thing at the center that Sirin never named.

She didn't name it. That was the second problem. That was the problem that was worse than naming it would have been, because naming it would have been a fact to deny, a claim to counter, a line in the sand. What Sirin had done was something more dangerous: she had described the *shape* of the secret without saying what the secret was, and the shape was so precise, so clearly rendered, that any reader with a functional brain would fill in the blank themselves.

She hadn't written *Jan and JingJing are together*. She had written around it — had drawn the outline and left the center empty and trusted the reader to color it in. And the outline was unmistakable.

Prae called Jan.

"Can you come to my office?"

"Now?"

"Now."

Jan arrived in four minutes. Still in the Lal costume — the tailored blazer, the pencil skirt, the heels that made her two inches taller and that JingJing, three studios away, was pretending not to think about. She sat in the chair across from Prae's desk and crossed her legs and looked at Prae with the easy, attentive focus of someone who had no idea she was about to be given bad news.

"Sirin sent me a draft," Prae said.

Jan's expression didn't change. This was not the controlled non-change of someone suppressing a reaction — it was the genuine non-change of someone who did not understand why this information should produce a reaction. Sirin was writing a piece. Sirin had sent a draft. These were facts about the world, and they ranked approximately equal in Jan's emotional hierarchy to the fact that it was Thursday.

"Okay," Jan said.

"It's good. It's very good. It's one of the best pieces of entertainment journalism I've read in years."

"That's good."

"Jan." Prae leaned forward. "It's good in a way that should concern you."

"Because she sees you."

Jan frowned. Not confused — just not tracking. "She's been on set for a month. She sees all of us."

"She sees the crew, Jan. She sees the way the crew *moves*. She's not watching you and JingJing — she's watching what happens around you. She's watching Fah appear at doors. "The crew is *terrified*. "What?"

Prae stopped. "It was true."

"That's — Jan, that's exactly the problem."

"Why?"

"Is it a good piece?" Jan asked.

"It's a beautiful piece."

"Does it respect us? Does it — does it treat us like people and not content?"

Prae thought about the passage about the fan meet. About Sirin's description of JingJing holding the shaking girl's hands: *a moment of unperformance — the mask falling not because it cracked but because the moment was too true to hold one up*. About the way Sirin had written about Jan's off-script line with the reverence of someone describing a prayer.

"I want you to be careful."

Jan looked at her. The amber eyes. Steady. Warm. The eyes that Sirin had described as containing *a general, undirected intensity* and that Prae knew — because Prae had been watching Jan for two years, had directed her through a love story that had become real on her watch — contained nothing general and nothing undirected.

They contained JingJing. They always contained JingJing.

She would not be careful. Prae knew this. Jan knew this. The Americano, room temperature and abandoned on the coaster, knew this.

Prae opened the desk drawer. Took out the gin she kept for emergencies.

This qualified.

## Chapter 13: The Secret Venue

The garden was in Nonthaburi, twenty minutes north of the city center, down a side road that turned into a dirt road that turned into a gate that opened onto something JingJing was not emotionally prepared for.

She knew what they were coming to see. She had examined each venue with the critical eye of someone who had spent six months imagining this day and who had specific, non-negotiable requirements: it had to be private. It had to be small. It had to be beautiful in a way that felt *theirs* — not magazine-beautiful, not Instagram-beautiful, but the kind of beautiful that made you stop breathing for a second because the world had arranged itself into something that matched the feeling inside your chest.

The garden in Nonthaburi had been unanimous.

But photos were photos. Photos were flat surfaces that contained light and color and composition and told you nothing about the way a place *felt* — the temperature of the air, the weight of the silence, the specific quality of the green when sunlight came through the canopy and landed on stone and grass and turned everything the color of a promise.

JingJing stepped through the gate and stopped.

Frangipani trees lined the perimeter, their branches forming a loose canopy overhead, the white-and-yellow flowers scenting the air with something sweet and clean that JingJing would associate with this moment for the rest of her life. But right now: the ring was on her finger. In a garden. In the sun.

"Can we taste the cake?" JingJing asked, and the request came out in the small voice — the Noo-Noo voice, the 3 a.m. voice, the voice that Jan heard when JingJing was being her most undefended self, the self that existed underneath every other version and was, Jan believed, the most beautiful thing in the world.

Khun Noi smiled. "I have three options. Come."

The tasting was set up under the mango tree, on a table covered in white linen: three small cakes, each plated with a fork and a card describing the flavors. Coconut-pandan. Mango-passionfruit. Thai tea with white chocolate.

JingJing tasted each one with the seriousness of a food critic and the emotional volatility of someone who was eating cake at the place where she was going to get married and was not, despite her earlier prediction, handling it well.

"The coconut-pandan," she said.

"That one?" Jan tried it. "It's good."

"It's not *good*, it's *perfect*. It tastes like — it tastes like my grandmother's kitchen. Like Saturday mornings. Like—" she stopped. Her eyes were full. "Like the kind of thing you serve at a wedding that means something."

Jan put down her fork. Wiped the corner of JingJing's eye with her thumb — gently, carefully, the way she handled JingJing's tears: as sacred objects, things to be caught and held, not dismissed.

"Coconut-pandan," Jan confirmed to Khun Noi, who nodded and wrote it down as if couples cried over cake in her garden every day, which, given the nature of her business, they probably did.

The flowers were next. Khun Noi's garden had its own greenhouse — a small, humid space filled with orchids and roses and the jasmine that would drape the pergola. JingJing wanted frangipani. Jan wanted jasmine. They compromised on both — frangipani for the bouquets, jasmine for the pergola, white roses for the table.

"And a peony," JingJing said. "One peony. For the centerpiece."

"A peony?"

"Love's favorite flower. She's going to be there and she's going to cry and she needs a flower to hold."

Jan's mouth curved. "Love can hold Milk's hand."

"Love needs BOTH. A flower and a hand. That's Love's minimum crying equipment."

"One peony," Jan told Khun Noi.

They walked the garden again. Slowly this time — not as visitors but as occupants, people learning the geography of a place that would hold the most important hour of their lives. JingJing paced the stone path from the gate to the pergola — twenty-two steps, she counted — and tried to imagine walking it in a dress, in front of twelve people, toward Jan.

"I'm going to trip," she said.

"You won't."

"I trip on flat surfaces in sneakers. I'm going to trip on a stone path in heels."

"I'll catch you."

"You always say that."

"I always do it."

JingJing stood at the pergola and looked back down the path. The garden stretched before her — the mango tree, the koi pond, the frangipani, the gate at the far end through which she would walk in thirty-seven days. In her imagination, the walk was already happening — she could see herself, in a dress she hadn't chosen yet, carrying frangipani she hadn't held yet, walking toward a woman she'd been walking toward since the day they met in a production office and argued about line readings and didn't know that the argument was the first sentence of a story that would end here, in a garden, with jasmine and cake and a stuffed rabbit that Fah was going to strap to a pillow because Noo-Noo was the ring bearer and this was real, this was *happening*, they were getting married in this garden in thirty-seven days.

JingJing cried.

Not the quiet kind this time. The full, ungoverned, mascara-destroying, nose-running kind — the cry that came from a place deeper than sadness or happiness, from the place where the two emotions merged into something that had no name because languages hadn't gotten around to naming the specific feeling of standing in the place where your life was about to change and realizing, with your whole body, that you were ready.

Jan held her. Of course Jan held her. Jan held her in a garden in Nonthaburi with the afternoon sun coming through the frangipani and the koi turning gold in the pond and the jasmine not yet blooming but promising to, and JingJing pressed her face into Jan's shoulder and cried until the crying became laughing and the laughing became crying again and the whole spectrum of human emotion played out in the space of ninety seconds against the cotton of Jan's t-shirt.

"I'm a mess," JingJing said.

"You're beautiful."

"I have snot on your shirt."

"Beautiful."

"I'm going to cry this much at the actual wedding and Love is going to cry *more* and Milk is going to pretend she's not crying and Fah is going to cry because she always cries and Lin—" JingJing stopped. "Is Lin coming?"

"Lin doesn't know about the wedding."

"She should be there."

Jan was quiet. The quiet of someone weighing a risk — not the risk of exposure, not the risk of Sirin, but the deeper risk of leaving someone out, of not inviting the woman who had spent months running interference, developing insomnia, sitting in her car trying not to throw up from the stress of protecting a secret she hadn't asked to carry.

"We'll tell her," Jan said. "Soon. She deserves to be there."

"She deserves to be there more than anyone."

"Then she'll be there."

JingJing pulled back. Wiped her eyes on Jan's already-ruined shirt. Looked up at her with the post-cry face — swollen, red, devastated, luminous, the face of someone who had just emptied herself of every emotion she'd been carrying and was now standing in the cleared space, light and ready.

"Take a picture of me," JingJing said.

"Here?"

"Here. Under the tree. In the place where — where it's going to happen."

Jan took out her phone. JingJing stood under the mango tree — tear-streaked, ring-wearing, wearing Jan's snot-stained shirt over her own tank top because she'd gotten cold and Jan had given her the shirt without being asked, the way Jan gave her everything without being asked. JingJing smiled. Not the lighthouse — the private one, the one that lived in the space between the performer and the person, the one that was for Jan and for the garden and for the koi pond and for no camera that the world would ever see.

Jan took the photo.

Then JingJing took Jan's phone and took a selfie of both of them — Jan behind her, chin on her shoulder, arms around her waist, both of them in the frame with the pergola and the jasmine and the future. JingJing held up her left hand. The ring caught the light.

"We can never post this," JingJing said, looking at the photo.

"No."

"It's the best photo anyone has ever taken of us and we can never show it to anyone."

"Thirty-seven days."

"Thirty-seven days until I can show the world the best photo of us." JingJing saved it. Opened her photo app. Created a new album. Named it "groceries."

"Groceries," Jan said.

"If anyone ever asks to see my grocery list, they'll open this album and see the garden where I'm getting married and have a very confusing experience."

"No one is going to ask to see your grocery list."

"You don't know that. What if Sirin asks? What if Sirin says, 'JingJing, I noticed you have a locked photo album, may I see it for the documentary?' and I have to say 'it's just groceries, very boring, nothing to see' and she opens it and it's us under a mango tree with matching rings?"

"Then we'll deal with it."

"'We'll deal with it' is not a strategy, P'Jan."

"It's our only strategy. It's always been our only strategy."

JingJing couldn't argue with this. Their entire relationship had been conducted on a plan that consisted of *we'll deal with it* — the dressing room, the balcony, the engagement, the ring, every near-miss and close call and intercepted disaster managed not by careful planning but by the basic, unkillable, structurally irrational conviction that love was enough and the details would work themselves out.

The details had, against all odds, worked themselves out so far. Mostly because Fah was a logistics genius and Lin had the stress tolerance of a fighter jet and Prae could redirect a conversation faster than most people could start one. But still. The details had worked out.

"Come on," Jan said. "We need to go. I told Prae we'd be back by four."

"One more minute."

"JingJing—"

"*One more minute.*"

JingJing walked to the pergola. Stood in the spot where they would stand — she on the left, Jan on the right, Fah somewhere nearby probably having a silent breakdown while holding the clipboard. She closed her eyes. Felt the jasmine. Heard the koi pond. Let the garden hold her for sixty seconds.

Then she opened her eyes. Walked back to Jan. Took her hand.

"Okay," she said. "I'm ready."

In the car, JingJing took the ring off her finger. Threaded it back onto the chain. Dropped it inside her shirt. The metal was warm from her skin — warm from the garden, warm from the sun, warm from the thirty minutes of being worn openly in the only place in the world where it could be.

She felt it settle against her sternum. The familiar weight. The tether.

Thirty-seven days.

In the group chat — the wedding one, not the crisis one:

**JingJing:** We picked the cake. Coconut-pandan. And the flowers. Frangipani for bouquets, jasmine for the pergola, white roses for the table. And one peony.

**Fah:** One peony?

**JingJing:** For Love. She'll need something to hold while she cries.

**Love:** I'm not going to cry.

**JingJing:** Love, you cried at the ANNOUNCEMENT. You cried at the announcement and then you called Milk and cried at Milk and then you called me back and cried again.

**Love:** Those were SITUATIONAL tears.

**JingJing:** They were ARCHITECTURAL tears. You cried for forty minutes. That's not a reaction. That's a construction project.

**Love:** The peony is appreciated. What color?

**JingJing:** Pink. Your favorite.

**Love:** 🌸💕😭

**JingJing:** See?? ALREADY CRYING. Three EMOJIS of crying.

**Fah:** I have the ring pillow prototype ready. Noo-Noo fitting is scheduled for Sunday. I need to take measurements.

**JingJing:** You are not putting a MEASURING TAPE on my RABBIT.

**Fah:** I need to know the dimensions to build the pillow harness.

**JingJing:** A HARNESS?

**Fah:** A SMALL harness. Decorative. Ribbon-based. Noo-Noo will look beautiful.

**Jan:** This is the most unhinged wedding planning conversation in human history.

**JingJing:** You proposed on a BEACH with NO PLAN. You don't get to judge unhinged.

**Jan:** I had a plan.

**JingJing:** Your plan was "bring the ring and hope for the best."

**Jan:** It worked.

**JingJing:** ...it worked.

**Love:** I'm not crying. I'm WATERING MY FACE.

**Fah:** Noo-Noo fitting: Sunday. 3 pm. Non-negotiable.

JingJing put her phone down. Looked out the window. The city was coming back — the expressway, the billboards, the skyline, the world that didn't know about the garden or the cake or the jasmine or the ring. The world that saw two actresses in a car on a Thursday afternoon and thought: colleagues. Friends. Costars returning from a location scout.

The world was wrong, and the world would stay wrong for thirty-seven more days, and then the world would be right, and the rightness would be permanent, and the garden would be the place where it happened — the place where the lie ended and the truth began and Noo-Noo sat on a ribbon harness holding two rings and twelve people watched and the jasmine bloomed and the cake tasted like Saturday mornings and JingJing walked twenty-two steps down a stone path toward the rest of her life.

She was ready. She had been ready since the dressing room. She would be ready for the rest of her life.

Jan's hand found hers on the center console. Their fingers interlaced. The ring on the chain pressed warm against JingJing's chest. The city moved outside. Inside: certainty.

## Chapter 14: Noo-Noo

JingJing brought the rabbit on a Monday.

She didn't plan it. She didn't decide, the night before, to smuggle a stuffed animal onto a professional set in direct violation of the brand guidelines Khun Dao had established during the first week of production (bullet point seven: "personal items that could generate off-brand social media content should be kept at home"). She didn't wake up that morning with a mission. She woke up, as she always did, curled against Jan's side with Noo-Noo under her arm, and when she got out of bed to shower and dress and become the version of herself that the world was allowed to see, she looked at the rabbit on the pillow — lopsided, button-eyed, the left ear slightly more floppy than the right from years of being held — and she could not leave her behind.

It was a feeling. Not a decision. A feeling like thirst or gravity — something that came from the body, not the mind, something that bypassed reasoning and went straight to the hands, which picked up the rabbit and put her in the bag, in the inside pocket, where the keychain Noo-Noo usually lived, and zipped it shut.

JingJing didn't tell Jan. She didn't tell Fah. She held the bag a little closer as she walked through the GMMTV campus and felt the solid, familiar weight of Noo-Noo against her hip and thought: *I need you today. I'm sorry. I know I'm not supposed to bring you. But I need you.*

The morning was a location shoot — exterior scenes at the garden set, Lal and Wine choosing a venue for their fictional wedding, a scene that would air on television and mean nothing to most viewers and mean everything to two women who had tasted cake and chosen flowers at a real garden thirty-six days from a real wedding. The irony was total. The irony was always total. JingJing was getting used to the irony the way you got used to a persistent low-grade fever: not by the fever breaking, but by your body adjusting its definition of normal.

They shot until noon. Jan in the Lal blazer, JingJing in the Wine dress — a fitted navy sheath that the costume department had tailored to the millimeter and that Jan had opinions about that were not appropriate for a professional environment. Between takes, JingJing sat in her chair and reached into the bag and touched Noo-Noo's ear — the left one, the soft one — and felt the contact like a current closing, the small electric comfort of the only object in the world that had been with her since before she was old enough to remember being given it.

The break came at 12:30. Lunch. The crew dispersed to craft services. Jan went to review footage with Prae. The set emptied in stages, the way sets always emptied — the big groups first, then the stragglers, then the last person, which was JingJing, who was sitting in her chair because she wanted three minutes alone with the thing in her bag.

She unzipped the pocket. Took Noo-Noo out. Held her.

Not dramatically. Not desperately. Just — held. The way she held her at home, at 3 a.m., when the world was too loud or the lie was too heavy or the gap between who she was allowed to be and who she actually was had widened enough to hurt. Noo-Noo in her lap, button eyes looking up, the left ear draped over JingJing's thumb. The rabbit smelled like home — like their apartment, like the laundry detergent Jan used, like the specific, unnameable smell of a life built between two people and a stuffed animal that had been part of the infrastructure since the beginning.

"I miss you at set," JingJing told the rabbit. Softly. The way you speak to something that can't hear you but that you've been speaking to since you were four and whose silence has never felt like absence.

"I miss you and I hate that I can't bring you because Khun Dao said you're 'off-brand' and Fah said Sirin would notice and everyone keeps saying it's temporary, it's just until the documentary is done, but—"

She stopped. Swallowed.

"But you're not temporary. You've been with me longer than anyone. Longer than Jan. Longer than Love. You were there before any of them and you're going to be there after — not after them, they're permanent, but after the show and after the documentary and after all of it, you're still going to be in my bed and I'm still going to hold you and I just—"

Her throat closed. She pressed Noo-Noo against her chest. Breathed into the softness. Felt the button eyes against her collarbone, the cotton body warm from the inside of the bag.

"I just need you today," she finished.

"That's a beautiful rabbit."

JingJing's head snapped up.

Sirin was standing at the edge of the set. Ten feet away. Notebook in one hand, iced coffee in the other, the gentle, observational stance of a journalist who had perfected the art of being present without intruding — a skill that was, JingJing realized with a spike of something that was not fear but something adjacent to it, the most dangerous skill in the world.

JingJing looked at Sirin. Sirin looked at Noo-Noo. Noo-Noo looked at the ceiling.

"She's—" JingJing started, and her voice came out wrong. Too soft. Too unguarded. The Noo-Noo voice, which was the same as the 3 a.m. voice, which was the same as the voice she used with Jan when the walls were down and the brat was asleep and the real JingJing was the only one in the room. "She's just — a toy. A prop."

Sirin walked closer. Not aggressively. With the careful, unhurried approach of someone who understood that she had walked into a private moment and was choosing to treat it with respect rather than capitalize on it.

"She doesn't look like a prop," Sirin said. "She looks loved."

JingJing's hand tightened on Noo-Noo. The word — *loved* — detonated somewhere in her chest, in the place where she kept the things that were real, the things that predated the performance, the things that Khun Dao's brand guidelines couldn't touch because they existed in a layer of JingJing that no strategy could reach.

"She's—" JingJing tried again. "She's from when I was little. She's just — I keep her at home usually. I don't know why I brought her today."

This was a lie. She knew exactly why she had brought her. She had brought her because the gap was too wide today — between who she was on screen and who she was in the apartment, between Wine-in-a-wedding-dress and JingJing-who-is-actually-getting-married, between the performance of a love story and the living of one — and Noo-Noo was the bridge across that gap, the one object that existed in both worlds, the thread that connected the actress to the child to the woman to the fiancée, and without that thread, JingJing felt untethered. Floating. A character without a ground wire.

"Does she have a name?" Sirin asked.

"Noo-Noo."

Sirin smiled. The smile was warm. Genuine. The smile of a person who was not performing interest but actually feeling it, and that made it worse, because Sirin's genuine warmth was the weapon that made her impossible to defend against — you couldn't hate someone who meant it, couldn't dismiss someone who cared, couldn't build a wall against someone who stood on your side of it and looked at you with kindness.

"Noo-Noo," Sirin repeated. "That's a good name." She paused. "You know — in the interviews I've done with GL actresses over the years, many of them talk about the objects that ground them. A scarf. A ring. A piece of jewelry from someone important. The physical anchors that keep them connected to who they are when the cameras aren't rolling." Her eyes were steady. Not probing. Observing. "Is Noo-Noo that kind of anchor for you?"

JingJing felt the question land. Not like a blow — like a hand on a bruise. Accurate. Precise. The exact place where the truth lived, pressed with exactly enough force to make her flinch.

*A ring. A piece of jewelry from someone important.*

The chain around her neck felt suddenly heavy. The engagement ring pressed against her sternum. Under the costume, under the fabric, in the space between performance and person, two anchors sat: the rabbit in her arms and the ring on her chest, both of them real, both of them sacred, both of them things she was being asked to hide.

"She's just a toy," JingJing said again. And this time the lie tasted different. Not like ash — the lies about being friends tasted like ash. This one tasted like betrayal. Like standing in a courtroom and denying someone you loved. Like being asked *do you know this person?* and saying *no*.

Noo-Noo was not a person. Noo-Noo was cotton and stitching and button eyes and a left ear that was softer than the right. But Noo-Noo was also the most real thing JingJing had ever owned, the constant in every variable, the witness to every version of JingJing that had ever existed — the four-year-old who couldn't sleep, the seventeen-year-old who auditioned for her first role, the twenty-three-year-old who fell in love in a dressing room. And calling her "just a toy" in front of a stranger felt like cutting a thread.

Sirin seemed to sense it. The shift. The fracture. She stepped back — not physically, but energetically, the journalist's instinct for knowing when a subject had reached the edge of what they could give.

"She's clearly special to you," Sirin said. Simply. Without pushing. "I'll let you have your break."

She turned. Walked away. Her shoes were quiet on the studio floor.

JingJing sat very still. She held Noo-Noo against her chest and breathed and felt the tears come — not the crying kind, not the kind that made sounds and drew attention. The silent kind. The kind that leaked out of the corners of your eyes and ran down the sides of your face and dripped onto the cotton body of a rabbit who had absorbed a lifetime of tears and never once complained.

This was new. This feeling. The other costs of the closet — the lies about being friends, the male model, the PR strategy, the social media curation — those costs were external. They happened in the space between JingJing and the world. They were the price of admission to an industry that wasn't ready for the truth, and JingJing paid them the way she paid taxes: resentfully, but with the understanding that the system required it.

This was internal. This was being asked to deny not the relationship, not the engagement, not Jan — but *herself*. Her softness. Her childishness. The part of her that needed a rabbit to get through the day. The part that Khun Dao called "off-brand" and Sirin would call "interesting" and Jan called "the best thing about you."

Being told to hide the ring was about the relationship. Being told to hide Noo-Noo was about her.

The distinction mattered.

JingJing didn't know how long she sat there. Long enough for the tears to stop and start and stop again. Long enough for the lunch break to be half over. Long enough to hear footsteps — familiar ones, the quick, purposeful stride of someone who had been told by Fah, who had been told by a PA, who had seen JingJing sitting alone in a chair holding a rabbit and crying — that something was wrong.

Jan came around the set wall and saw her and stopped.

JingJing looked up. Her face was wrecked — the silent tears had done their work, the mascara had done its usual surrender, and the eyes that looked at Jan were not the brat's eyes or the performer's eyes but the little-girl eyes, the four-year-old eyes, the eyes that said *I need you* in a language that predated speech.

Jan didn't ask what happened. She didn't say "are you okay" or "what's wrong" or any of the things people said when they encountered someone crying and didn't know how to help. She crossed the distance in three steps and knelt in front of the chair and took JingJing's face in both hands and pressed her forehead against JingJing's and said:

"I'm here."

JingJing broke.

The full cry. The shaking kind. The kind she saved for the apartment, for the bed, for the 3 a.m. darkness where the walls were down and the only witnesses were Jan and Noo-Noo and the headboard shelf. Except they weren't in the apartment — they were on set, on a soundstage, in a building full of crew members and a journalist, and Jan was holding her face and pressing their foreheads together and JingJing was sobbing into the space between them and neither of them cared, because this was the real emergency, the real risk, the one that mattered: not that Sirin would see them touching, but that JingJing was hurting, and Jan could not allow that to continue for a single second longer than it took her to cross a room.

"She asked about Noo-Noo," JingJing managed. "Sirin. She saw her. She asked if she had a name and I said — I said she was just a toy, P'Jan. I told her Noo-Noo was just a toy."

Jan's thumbs wiped JingJing's tears. Slowly. One cheek, then the other, with the meticulous care of someone performing a task that was simultaneously trivial and sacred.

"Noo-Noo is not just a toy," Jan said.

"I know."

"Noo-Noo is the ring bearer at our wedding."

JingJing laughed. It was a terrible laugh — wet, broken, the laugh of someone who had been crying hard enough to hiccup — but it was a laugh, and Jan received it like a signal, a confirmation that the worst had passed and the rebuild was beginning.

"She asked — she asked if Noo-Noo was an anchor," JingJing said. "If she was the thing that kept me connected to myself when the cameras aren't rolling. And I wanted to say *yes*. I wanted to say *yes, she's my anchor, and the ring on my chest is my anchor, and YOU are my anchor, and I'm tired of pretending that none of these things exist, I'm tired of calling my rabbit a toy and my wife a friend and my engagement ring a necklace, I'm tired of—*"

"I know."

"—of being a version of myself that doesn't get to have the things I actually have. I don't — P'Jan, I'm not scared. I'm not scared of Sirin. I'm not scared of being caught. I'm — I'm *sad*. I'm sad because they're asking me to erase the parts of myself that I—"

She couldn't finish. Jan pulled her forward, off the chair, onto the floor, into her lap. JingJing went — all bones and crying and the rabbit clutched against her chest — and Jan held her the way Jan always held her: completely, structurally, as if Jan's arms were the walls of a room and JingJing was the only thing the room was built to contain.

"Thirty-five days," Jan said against her hair. "Thirty-five days and then Noo-Noo comes to set. Noo-Noo comes to set and the ring goes on your finger and everyone knows and you never have to say 'just a toy' again."

"Promise?"

"I promise."

"I said it to her face, P'Jan. I denied her. Noo-Noo has been with me since I was *four* and I looked at a stranger and said she was just a—"

"Noo-Noo doesn't know. Noo-Noo doesn't have feelings."

"Noo-Noo has FEELINGS."

"Noo-Noo has button eyes and cotton stuffing and—"

"If you finish that sentence I will never speak to you again."

Jan kissed the top of her head. The slow one. "Noo-Noo has feelings. Noo-Noo knows you love her. Noo-Noo understands the situation and Noo-Noo forgives you."

"How do you know?"

"Because I asked her this morning."

JingJing pulled back. Looked at Jan with the tear-swollen, skeptical, desperately hopeful face of someone who needed this to be true even though she knew, rationally, that rabbits made of cotton did not participate in conversations.

"You asked her?"

"I ask her every morning. While you're in the shower. I check on her. I straighten her ears. I tell her you'll be home by seven."

JingJing stared. "You talk to my rabbit."

"I talk to our rabbit. She's mine too now. That's how marriage works — I get half of everything, including the stuffed animals."

JingJing pressed Noo-Noo against Jan's chest. Hard. The rabbit's button eyes stared at Jan's chin.

"Tell her you love her," JingJing said.

"JingJing—"

"TELL HER."

Jan looked down at the rabbit pressed against her chest. At the button eyes. At the left ear, soft and floppy, draped over JingJing's thumb. At the evidence of a childhood and a heart and a woman who needed something this small to be taken seriously because the world had spent her entire adult life telling her that the things she loved most were off-brand.

"I love you, Noo-Noo," Jan said. To the rabbit. Sincerely. With the full, unhesitating, gravity of someone who understood that this was not a performance but a vow — a vow to the rabbit and to the woman holding the rabbit and to the part of that woman that the world kept trying to make her hide.

JingJing cried again. But these tears were different — warmer, softer, the tears that came not from sadness but from the specific, devastating relief of being understood by someone who could have laughed but didn't, who could have dismissed it but wouldn't, who was kneeling on a soundstage floor talking to a stuffed rabbit because the woman she loved needed the rabbit to be real, and so the rabbit was real, and that was that.

JingJing's phone buzzed. She looked at it. Love.

**Love:** Milk says Fah texted Ciize who texted Milk who texted me. Are you okay?

JingJing looked at the phone. Love had not been told what happened. Love had received information through a chain of four people and had texted immediately. Love had known — the way Love always knew — that JingJing needed her.

**JingJing:** Sirin saw Noo-Noo. I told her she was just a toy.

**Love:** Oh, babe.

**JingJing:** I denied my rabbit, Love.

**Love:** I know.

**JingJing:** I feel like I denied a FAMILY MEMBER. In a COURT OF LAW.

**Love:** Noo-Noo is a family member. This is not a controversial position.

**JingJing:** Jan told Noo-Noo she loves her.

**Love:** On the set??

**JingJing:** On the FLOOR of the set. She is currently holding me and the rabbit and she SAID THE WORDS.

**Love:** I'm not crying.

**JingJing:** You're crying.

**Love:** I am ABSOLUTELY crying. Milk just looked at me and asked what was wrong and I said "Jan told the rabbit she loves her" and Milk said "of course she did" like it was the most normal thing in the world and now Milk is ALSO crying and we are two adults crying in the Ditto greenroom about a STUFFED RABBIT.

**JingJing:** Tell Milk the rabbit says thank you.

**Love:** Milk says "the rabbit can come to set with us anytime. The Ditto set has no journalists and no brand strategy and Ciize has a stuffed bear and nobody cares."

**JingJing:** I love Milk.

**Love:** Get in line.

**JingJing:** 🐰

**Love:** 🐰💕

JingJing put the phone down. Wiped her face. Looked at Jan, who was still on the floor, still holding her, still being the thing she always was — the surface JingJing leaned against, the wall JingJing pushed against, the arms JingJing fell into when the performance got too heavy to hold alone.

"Take me home tonight," JingJing said.

"I take you home every night."

"Take me home and — and be soft. I need soft tonight. Not — not Daddy. Not the game. Just — Jan. Just you. Just forehead kisses and my name and — and Noo-Noo on the shelf and—"

"I know what you need."
