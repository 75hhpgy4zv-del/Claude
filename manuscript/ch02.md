# 2

The apartment had one good room and Derek had given it to the podcast.

Not the bedroom, which had a mattress and a lamp and a stack of library books that
constituted a moral failing. The second bedroom, which a normal thirty-five-year-old
would have made into an office or given to a child. It had the shelf, the projector, the
disc player he had bought used from a man in Fontana who had cried a little handing it
over, and a chair that cost more than his first car and had been, he maintained under
questioning, the correct decision.

Wednesday. 10:12. He was doing the pre-flight.

The pre-flight had eleven items and he did them in the same order every week, and the
order was not optional in a way he had made his peace with. Lamp on, other lamp off.
Disc in the tray, even though he'd be streaming from a file, because he wanted the disc
in the building. Water and tea, the tea in the mug from the Alamo Drafthouse that had a
chip in the handle he was pretending not to have noticed for two years. Levels. Levels
again. Phone face-down in the drawer, not on the desk. The drawer mattered.

Then he sat in the chair and did the thing he never told anybody about, which was
nothing, for two minutes, with the light on his face and the camera off, coming down
out of the day.

Because that was the trick nobody warned you about. You cannot get on a microphone
carrying fifth period. It gets into your voice as a kind of low hum, and the hum is
audible, and about four hundred people had unsubscribed over eleven years for reasons
they'd probably have described as *he seemed tired lately* if anyone had thought to ask
them.

At 10:40 he went live.

---

"Hi. Hey. Okay."

Nine people. Then thirty-one, then fifty-something in the way it always went, the
graph's little hockey stick as the notification went out and people came in off it.

"So it's the first week of school, which means I have taught for three days and I have
the personality of a wrung-out sponge, and I made a decision this afternoon that this
was going to be a Verhoeven year." He drank the tea. "Because I need it. Selfishly. I
need to spend the fall with a Dutchman who thinks America is a lunatic asylum with good
teeth."

*mothlight: LETS GOOOO*
*it's verhoeven season*
*kaiju_dad: my wife has left the room already*
*mothlight: tell her it's about labor*
*kaiju_dad: it IS about labor*

"It is about labor. Kaiju, you're doing my job for me and I resent it."

*PERSIMMON_77: sir the disc*

"The disc is in the tray. The disc is always in the tray. I don't have to justify the
disc to you people."

He set up *RoboCop* for eighty minutes without once looking at the notes he'd made,
because the notes were a comfort object and he knew it. He talked about the ED-209 scene
and about what it means that the movie's funniest joke and its most upsetting image are
the same forty seconds of footage. He read out, aloud, in full, the text of an actual
1985 Detroit city council resolution he had found in an afternoon he should have spent
doing something else, and then read the film's fake commercial copy after it, and let
the room sit with how little daylight there was between them.

That was the move. He had been doing that move for eleven years. Read the real thing
aloud, read the made-up thing aloud, shut up.

Eighty-eight people.

Somewhere in the second hour a name he half recognised typed *derek have you seen the
new one* about a film currently in theatres, and he said, "I have not, because I've been
in a building with no windows since Monday, and also I want to see it in a room with
strangers, and also — hold on, I'm going to be a lot about this for thirty seconds —"

*here we go*
*mothlight: buckle up*

"— I'm not going to have an opinion about it until Sunday, and that is a *choice*, and
the choice is the only thing I've got. There is an entire economy now that pays people
to have the opinion first. I make four hundred dollars a month. Do you understand what
that buys me? It buys me the ability to be *late*. I'm the last man in America who can
afford to still be thinking about something on Sunday."

*PERSIMMON_77: he's monologuing*
*mothlight: he's monologuing and he's right*
*kaiju_dad: four hundred dollars of pure freedom baby*

"It's four hundred and change and I'm very proud."

*so does that mean ant-man is still off the table*

Derek put the mug down.

"Ant-Man," he said, "is not off the table. Ant-Man is not *on* the table. Ant-Man does
not interact with the table. In 2015 I said, on a podcast that had eleven listeners, one
of whom was my mother, that I would never watch that film. I said it about a movie I had
no strong feelings about, on a Tuesday, to nobody. And I have not watched it."

*THE BIT*
*it's not a bit is the thing*
*mothlight: it's genuinely not a bit*

"It's not a bit. Bits are for people with an audience. In 2015 I had eleven listeners,
so it can't be a bit, it can only be a promise." He shrugged, on camera, at ninety
people. "And I keep those. It's the one thing that's free."

---

He signed off at 12:26 and did the outro badly, the way he did every week, because
endings required a version of himself he had never been able to build.

Then the light went out and the room was a room again.

He sat in the expensive chair with his hands on his knees for a while. This was the
worst ten minutes of any Wednesday and he had never once found a way around it: the
drop, the sudden absence of ninety people, the apartment reasserting itself. His ears
rang faintly. On the shelf behind him, in the dark, the steelbooks stood in their row
like a small municipal building.

He did the numbers, because he always did. Peak 94. Average 81. Four new subs, one gift
sub, chat log saved automatically by the script he'd written in an evening in 2021 and
improved eleven times since for no reason anybody had asked for.

Ninety-four people had spent part of a Wednesday listening to him, and he knew a fair
amount about several of them. He knew kaiju_dad's daughter had started kindergarten. He
knew mothlight was writing a dissertation and hated it. He knew PERSIMMON_77 had been
there since 2019 and had never once said anything about themselves.

They knew things about him too. That was the arrangement and he had thought hard about
it, more than most people in the business ever bothered to, because it was the kind of
problem that fit his head exactly. They knew his voice at midnight. They knew he had a
day job, no details. They knew what he thought about roughly nine hundred films and, if
they were paying attention, exactly what he was afraid of, because a man cannot talk
about art for eleven years without leaving a map.

He rinsed the mug. He put the disc back in its case, in the right place, in the row.

Then he took his phone out of the drawer, where there were two messages from his brother
and one from his father, and stood in the kitchen in the dark reading them with his
thumb, and the day did not end so much as stop.
