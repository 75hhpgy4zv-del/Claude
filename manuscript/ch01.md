# 1

The copier on the second floor had been broken since June and the sign taped to it
still said BACK MONDAY, which had been true once.

Derek stood in front of it at 7:20 on the first morning holding ninety-four syllabi that
existed only as a file, and performed the ritual: pressed the button, waited, listened
to the machine consider it, watched the panel decide against him. Then he went down to
the first floor, where the good copier lived behind the attendance office, and where
Rosalind, who ran attendance and had outlasted four principals, looked up at him over
her glasses.

"You're early."

"I'm at the end of a long chain of failure."

"Second floor's still out."

"Second floor is a monument, Roz. We should put a plaque on it."

She let him use the good one. She always let him use the good one, because eleven years
ago he had spent a Saturday getting her nephew's college essay into a shape that got him
into Cal Poly, and she had a longer memory than the district did.

The syllabus itself he no longer believed in. He had written it in 2017 with real
conviction and had been revising it downward ever since, the way you let out a hem. It
had a grading breakdown and a late-work policy and a paragraph about *the classroom as a
community of readers*, which was the kind of sentence he could no longer say aloud
without hearing how it sounded. He handed it out anyway. It was a document that existed
to be filed by fourteen hundred people, and pretending otherwise was its own kind of
vanity.

Then he taught for six hours.

---

Fifth period came after lunch, which meant they arrived warm and loud and slightly
unwell, twenty-nine juniors in a room built for twenty-four. He had them for American
Literature. Three of them he already knew. The rest were a wall.

He did not do names first. He had stopped doing names first in about year four, when he
noticed that the first ninety seconds of a school year were the only ninety seconds in
which a room would give you its whole attention for free, and that he had been spending
them on attendance.

So he waited until they had mostly stopped moving. Then he picked up a book off the desk
and read to them.

*Márgarét, áre you gríeving*
*Over Goldengrove unleaving?*

Somebody laughed, because of the way the accents made him say it. He kept going. He read
it the way it was written, which is to say strangely, with the stresses landing where
Hopkins had hammered them in, and by the fourth line the laughing had stopped, not
because anyone had decided to be respectful but because the noise of it had gotten
interesting.

*Ah! ás the heart grows older*
*It will come to such sights colder*
*By and by, nor spare a sigh*
*Though worlds of wanwood leafmeal lie…*

He read to the end. He let the room sit for a second, which was longer than it sounds.

"Okay," he said. "Nobody tell me what it means. That's a trap and I'm not interested.
Tell me what it *sounded* like."

Nothing, for a beat.

"Sad," said a girl in the second row, in a voice that dared him to say it was wrong.

"Sad. Yes. Good. Why — no, sorry, that's the same trap wearing a hat." He put the book
down. "Try this. It sounded sad *before* you knew what any of it meant. Half those words
aren't words. *Wanwood. Leafmeal.* He made them up. And it still landed. So what does
that tell you about how this thing is working on you?"

"That it's cheating," said a boy by the window.

Derek pointed at him, delighted. "It's completely cheating. What's your name?"

"Marcus."

"Marcus says it's cheating and Marcus is right, and that's the class. That's the whole
class, for a year. Everything we read is going to be doing something to you underneath
the part you can explain, and the only real skill I have to give you is the ability to
catch it in the act." He picked the book back up. "Also, for the record, it's about a
kid crying about leaves who doesn't know yet that she's crying about dying. Which I know
because I'm thirty-five and I read the last line, and it took Hopkins eight lines and
took me two decades, so let's all agree the poem is better at this than we are."

The room laughed. Not all of it. Enough.

He got about eleven good minutes out of them after that, which on the first day was
theft.

---

Here is what Derek was actually doing while he taught, which he had never told anyone
and could not have explained without sounding either mystical or unwell.

He was counting the room.

Not the bodies. The temperature of them, one at a time, in a rolling scan he had no
control over and had long ago stopped fighting. The girl in the second row had answered
loudly to get it over with. Marcus by the window was going to be a problem in the good
way, and the problem would be that he'd stop trying by October if Derek didn't hand him
something hard. Two kids in the back were together and would not be by November. The
boy under the clock had not taken his backpack off.

It came in whether he asked for it or not. It had done so at every job he had ever had,
including four years at Magnolia selling speakers to men who wanted permission to spend
six thousand dollars, and it was the reason he was good at that too. There was one door
and everything came through it: the kid drowning quietly, the false note in a movie's
third act, the exact shade of his brother's voice on the phone. He had never worked out
how to keep it open at work and shut everywhere else, and by 3 p.m. most days there was
nothing left of him, because the machine had no off switch and he had been running it
since 7:20.

The boy under the clock kept the backpack on for the whole period.

---

At 2:52 the bell went and they poured out, and one of them didn't.

He was small for a junior, sixteen, with the posture of somebody who had recently gotten
taller than he was prepared to be. He came up the aisle sideways, holding the index card
Derek had them fill out at the end — name, what you read last, something true — and he
put it on the desk instead of in the pile.

"You can just put it with the others," Derek said.

"I know." He was already leaving. "I just didn't want it in the middle."

Derek waited until the room was clear. Then he read it.

**TOMÁS BEVILACQUA**, in small print, all caps, like a form.

*Last thing I read: the manual for my mom's car, cover to cover, twice. Not a joke.*

*Something true: everyone in this class has known each other since kindergarten and I
moved here in March.*

Derek sat with that for a while, in a room that smelled like floor wax and thirty
teenagers.

Then he took a fresh index card out of the drawer, and wrote on it, and put it in the
seat under the clock for the next day, which was the seat Tomás had sat in without a
backpack and with his hands flat on the desk the entire period like a man waiting to be
asked to leave.

The card said: *Cover to cover, twice, is called close reading and it's the entire job.
See me Thursday, I have something for you. — DR*

He did not think of it as an intervention. He never did. It was closer to an itch.

---

He got home at 4:40, which was early, and ate a bowl of cereal standing at the counter
because he had not planned dinner and because cooking for one had a sound in his
apartment that he preferred to avoid on Mondays.

There were four hours until he had to be a different thing.

He spent them badly, which was normal: forty minutes intending to grade and not
grading, a stretch of lying on the floor with his forearm over his eyes, then a sudden
violent hour of competence at 7 p.m. in which he did all of it, plus the thing he'd been
putting off since June, plus a small unnecessary improvement to the script that pulled
his stream's chat log into a text file, because the improvement was interesting and the
grading was not.

At 8:15 his phone lit up with his brother's name.

He looked at it until it stopped.
