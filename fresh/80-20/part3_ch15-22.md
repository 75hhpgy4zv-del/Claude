# 80/20 — Part Three: Chapters 15–22


## Chapter Fifteen: The Other Guy

His brother announced the visit two weeks in advance, which was generous by Vijay's standards. He was a man who treated other people's schedules as approximate rather than fixed, who believed that the spontaneity of an unannounced arrival was a sign of intimacy rather than a failure of consideration. That he had given two weeks meant he wanted something.

Derek noted this and filed it and went about his days.

The cold started coming back four days before the visit, which was not the visit's fault. These things had their own logic, didn't always sync with external events. By the Thursday Vijay arrived Derek had been managing it for days with the minimum-requirements method and was tired in the specific way of someone who has been holding something heavy and has not put it down.

Vijay was fifty-two and looked fifty-two in the way of men who have never questioned whether their habits were serving them, which is to say: solidly, with confidence. He was a civil engineer in New Jersey. He had a wife and two teenage children and a large house and opinions about all of these things and about Derek's things as well, which he shared with the warmth of a man who genuinely believed that sharing opinions was a form of care. He was not wrong that it was a form of care. He was wrong about whose frame of reference the care was organized around.

Derek made dinner. He cooked the dal from scratch, which took an hour, because the cooking was also the thinking, and the thinking that day needed somewhere to go. Vijay sat at the kitchen island with a beer and watched and commented on the process with the cheerful expertise of someone who had watched their mother cook this many times and had absorbed the expertise without the practice.

"You still doing the streaming thing?" Vijay said.

"Yeah."

"And the podcast."

"Yeah."

"How many people listen?" He said it without cruelty. This was the thing about Vijay: there was no cruelty in him, not intentional cruelty. Just the cruelty of someone who has spent his life in the language of measurables: load-bearing capacity, materials cost, project timeline. He asked about listeners the way he would ask about the tensile strength of a cable. Just wanting the number.

"Varies," Derek said.

"Ballpark."

"For the podcast, maybe three hundred regular listeners. The stream is smaller."

Vijay made a sound that wasn't quite dismissive but that landed like it, the sound of someone who has heard a number and found it informative in a direction they were already facing. "You should think about monetizing it better. I was reading about these guys who make six figures off YouTube.

You have the skills."

"I know."

"It is good content. I listened to one. The movie one."

Derek looked at him. "You listened to an episode?"

"The Robocop one. Amma said you had mentioned it." He shrugged.

"It was good. You know your stuff. You just need to..." He made a gesture that meant: scale it, professionalize it, make it into something the world would recognize as success. "You could be doing more with it."

You could be doing more with it. Derek stirred the dal and heard the sentence the way he had been hearing variations of it his whole life, not as an attack, not as a dismissal of what the thing actually was, but as a redirect toward the version of the thing that would make sense in Vijay's vocabulary. You could be doing more. What you are doing does not quite count.

"I like it the way it is," Derek said.

"I know you do." Vijay finished his beer. "I just think, at thirty-five, you should be building toward something. The teaching is fine, but fine is not a plan."

The teaching is fine.

Derek put the lid on the dal and turned the flame to low and stood at the stove for a moment. He was already colder than he had been an hour ago and he knew it, and the knowing was a familiar kind of knowing, like recognizing a weather pattern from the look of the sky before the rain arrives. He said nothing. He served dinner.

They ate. Vijay talked about the house, the kids, a project at work. He asked about Derek's students with the genuine-ish interest of someone who believed in education in the abstract. He did not ask about Maya. Derek had not offered her, and the not-offering was its own kind of information about what Derek expected from the conversation.

After dinner Vijay scrolled his phone and they watched something middling that was on and that neither of them cared enough to change. At ten Vijay said he was tired and went to the guest room, and Derek sat on the couch in the quiet with the television off.

The cold had arrived in full, the way it always arrived when he was already tired, not dramatically, just completely, like a room that has been cooling all evening and you only notice when you stop moving. He sat in the quiet. The room was his room: the steelbooks, the prints, the Morricone on the shelf beside the Coltrane. The ring dish on the shelf near the Mondo Chinatown, where it had always been, through every version of this apartment and this life. He looked at it tonight. He had not looked at it in a while. He looked at it now and it was just an object, a pretty thing from a woman who had given it to him before everything became what it became, and he was aware that it was still here and she was not and that this was a fact about his life that he had arranged himself around so carefully that the arrangement had started to feel like who he was.

The fine is not a plan sentence sat in him.

He thought about all the things Vijay did not know. About the three hundred listeners and what it meant to them and what it meant to him.

About the record store and the Morricone and the Shuggie Otis and the woman who had bought both and gone home and listened and not posted about it. About the couch in the cinema dark. The sidewalk. The next time.

None of this would fit in Vijay's vocabulary. None of it would compute in the language of measurables. And Derek could not explain it in a way that would make it compute, and had stopped trying, and the stopping had cost something that he had paid without looking at the bill.

His phone buzzed. Maya had sent a photo: the Shuggie Otis sleeve against her apartment wall, lit by the lamp from the side. No caption. Just: this.

He was cold all around him and he looked at the photo. He typed: I can see the room in it from here.

She responded with a small heart. He put the phone down and went to bed.

* * *

Vijay left the next morning. The goodbye was warm in the surface way and complicated underneath it, and Derek stood at the door and watched his brother carry his bag down the corridor and felt the mixture he always felt at the end of Vijay visits: relief and the small dark residue of the relief, which was the knowledge that the relief was necessary.

He closed the door.

He stood in his apartment and looked at the steelbooks and the prints and the ring dish and thought about what Vijay had seen: a younger brother who taught English and made a podcast for three hundred people and lived in a one-bedroom apartment at thirty-five and appeared, by Vijay's instruments, to be underperforming at the business of adult life.

He thought about what Vijay had missed: all of it. All of the actual thing.

He was very cold now. The coldest he had been in a while.

His phone rang. Maya.

He answered.

"Hey," she said. "How was it?"

"He left this morning."

"How bad is it right now?"

She asked it directly, without preamble. He said: "Bad. Worse than usual."

A beat. "Okay. I'm coming over."

"You don't have to."

"I know. I'll be there in an hour."

* * *

She arrived with food and let herself in and he was on the couch and she set the table and said come eat, and he came. She had not gotten the tofu thing he always ordered and he noticed that she hadn't and it went unmentioned.

The food was warm. He ate.

She did not make him talk. She just ate and was there. He was aware of her in the way you are aware of warmth from across a room, indirectly, through its effect on the surrounding air.

Later she put something on and he was half-watching it and the food was in him and she was beside him and he fell asleep.

He did not know he had fallen asleep until he woke at two in the morning. She was gone. The apartment was quiet. There was a blanket over him that had been on the chair. On the couch cushion, her absence had a shape.

He went to the kitchen. The refrigerator. He stood at the open door for a moment looking at the second shelf.

The mango was gone.

He did not know when she had done it, or whether she had said anything to herself while she did it, or whether she had thought about it first. He just knew it was gone and she had made it gone and she was not here anymore and the apartment was quiet and he was still cold but less so and the incremental return had already started, warming by degrees, the slow reliable thing.

He went back to bed. He lay in the dark and he did not think about anything in particular. The room was his and she had been in it and now it was his again and the mango was gone.

That was enough to hold.


## Chapter Sixteen: Through Glass

She had known, after the second day, that the tired thing was not tired.

She had been attending to him long enough now to know the texture of his responses when he was himself and the texture of them when he was not. It was not obvious from the outside. He was good at the maintenance of apparent okayness, had clearly had a long time to practice it, and the short answers and the I'm fines could have passed for busyness or distraction if she had wanted them to. She did not want them to.

She had a cousin with ADHD. She had a roommate in college who had depression, a real depression, the kind that arrived in October and left in April and made the winter something to be managed rather than lived. She knew the specific quality of the managed voice, the way it stayed level and present and technical, and she knew that the level and present and technical quality was itself the signal. The effort of the maintenance visible to someone who knew where to look.

She knew where to look.

She did not push. She had watched people push at her college roommate during the bad months and seen what it did, the way it added guilt to the distance, made the person inside it responsible for the discomfort of the people outside.

But she was uncomfortable. She could see him, could see the real him, and he was not there, and the absence of him was specific and had weight.

She was texting someone who was wearing his responses and she missed the person inside them and she was not sure she had earned the right to miss him yet.

She sent the small things. The photo of the record bin. The Twin Peaks meme. A text about something oblique she had been thinking about.

She wondered after each one if it was too much input, the way he had said once, gently, that the photo had been too much input when he was under.

She was trying to calibrate to something she couldn't fully see from here.

On the fourth day she made the decision to go over. She made it at noon and spent an hour interrogating it before she committed: was she going because he needed her, or because she needed to feel useful, or because the discomfort of the passive approach had exceeded her threshold? All three, probably. She was not going to be precious about her motives. She was going because he had been inside this for four days and she did not think adding another day to it served either of them.

She texted. He said okay, the quietest okay he had sent her. She stopped at the Taiwanese place on his corner; she had noted it on one of her earlier drives, filed it as useful, the way she filed things, and got two containers of what she was fairly sure he would eat, which turned out to be two of the three things she should have gotten because she forgot the tofu thing again, and she knocked on his door at seven.

He opened it.

She took him in quickly without showing that she was taking him in: clean, present in the technical sense, the lights on. The effort of the maintenance visible. She said: "I brought food." And stepped inside.

She set the table. She did not make it a production. Making it a production would have required him to have feelings about it being a production, and that was not what she was here for.

He ate. She watched this with a relief she did not show. She talked about something low-stakes; afterward she couldn't remember exactly what, some small absurdity from her week, something that gave the room a frequency to occupy without demanding anything from him. She did not require him to be funny or present or grateful. She just sat across from him in his kitchen and ate her food and let the air between them settle.

She did the dishes after. She had discovered in college that she liked doing dishes, the satisfaction of a task with clear completion. She ran the water and washed the bowls and was in his kitchen and he was on the couch and the apartment had the quality of a place that had been alone for several days and was now, marginally, less alone.

She opened the refrigerator to put the leftover dal in.

The mango was on the second shelf. She looked at it. Past every threshold. Not visibly dramatic, just thoroughly done. She stood at the open refrigerator for a moment. She thought about leaving it. His mango, his refrigerator, his thing to handle when he came back to himself. She thought about the alternative.

She threw it out. She did this without ceremony, without looking over her shoulder, without making any gesture of it at all. Just: the mango is past where it should be, and I am here, and I can do this one small thing.

She closed the refrigerator.

She went and sat on the couch and after a while he fell asleep, the sudden boneless sleep of someone who has put something down. She found the blanket on the reading chair and put it over him. She turned the lamp down. She sat for a while longer in the dim room, not doing anything in particular, just being there in the way that being there was sometimes the whole of it.

Then she texted Jess a photo of the mango on the way out, captioned: the mango situation, because she had been inside her own feelings for three hours with no one to process them with and she needed somewhere to put it and Jess would understand without requiring an explanation.

She regretted it in the car on the way home. Not a lot. But a little.

She sat in the parking structure of her building for a few minutes before going up. She was thinking about the evening. About what it had cost him to say bad, worse than usual. About the mango. About the blanket on the reading chair that she had found without looking for it because she had been paying attention to his apartment the way she paid attention to things she cared about, quietly, over time, without making it legible.

She had not asked for anything tonight. She had not made him watch her do the kind thing. She had just done it and left.

She thought: this is what it looks like when it's real.

The thought arrived without announcement and she sat with it for a moment before she understood what it meant: not the it of Derek, but the it of this, of the whole shape of an evening in which she had shown up for someone and not made it about showing up, had done a small domestic thing in a kitchen that wasn't hers and not needed it witnessed.

She had not known she was capable of that. Or she had known, maybe, in the abstract, the way you know things about yourself that you have never tested.

She went upstairs. She made tea. She sat in her apartment in the specific quiet of a night that had been full of someone else and was now hers again, and she did not stream it and she did not call Jess back and she did not open her phone.

She just sat with it.


## Chapter Seventeen: Telling Her

He came back in increments, the way he always did, not a switch but a gradient, the glass thinning over days, the world reacquiring its temperature by degrees. Day five was better than day four. Day six was better than five. On the morning of day seven he stood at the window with his coffee and the street was itself and he was watching it and actually there for the watching, actually receiving the information of the street, the dog walker and the woman with the grocery bag and the truck making a delivery, all of it arriving at full resolution, and he thought: there it is.

He texted Maya: I think I'm back.

She responded immediately: I know. You texted me about a film review at 11pm last night.

He stared at this. He had not remembered doing that.

> *Derek: what did I say*

> *Maya: you said the AV Club review of the new Yorgos Lanthimos
> was missing the entire point of the film*

> *Maya: for about six paragraphs*

> *Derek: was I wrong*

> *Maya: no you were completely right which is how I knew you were
> coming back*

> *Derek: the AV Club review is genuinely bad*

> *Maya: I read it. you're right. also welcome back*

Welcome back. He read it again. She had said it before, in the middle of the Four days, in the text thread, the small things she'd sent, the meme, the fish line, but this was the first time she'd said it directly, and it landed differently than he expected. Not with the weight of someone noting an absence. Just: simply, gladly. Welcome back.

He called her that afternoon. He was in his apartment, sitting in the reading chair, back in use now, and she picked up on the second ring.

"I want to tell you something," he said.

"Okay."

"About what that is. What happens when I go — when I'm not —" He paused. The language was harder than he expected. He had described it to himself, had named the levels for himself, but had never given it to another person in real time. "I've never really explained it to anyone."

"Take your time," she said.

He took a breath. "It comes in levels. There's a baseline, and then there are degrees below it. And the way I know which level I'm at is by the glass."

"The glass."

"There's always a glass. Even when everything is fine. I know it's there. Most of the time it's so thin it doesn't matter. Just the awareness of it.

And then sometimes it gets thicker."

He paused. She did not fill the pause.

"Two is where things feel slightly delayed. Like the world is happening just a step ahead of me and I'm playing catch-up. I can function normally. Nobody can tell. But everything costs a little more than it should."

"Okay," she said. The receiving okay. The one she did.

"Three is the muffle. Things go flat. Food doesn't taste right. I can still teach, I can still do the things I need to do, but I'm doing them from a distance. Like I'm watching myself do them. The competent version of me is running. I'm not in it."

He stopped. Started again.

"Four is the Room. I can see everything perfectly clearly. I just can't reach it. You know you're hungry. The kitchen is twelve feet away. You know that. It's clear, it's obvious, it requires nothing. And you just sit there."

He could hear her breathing. Present, quiet.

"You sit there watching yourself not go to the kitchen and you think: this is insane, you idiot, it's twelve feet, just stand up. And you don't. And then the light changes and you've been sitting there for three hours and you still haven't gone and the only thing that's different is now it's dark."

He paused.

"The worst part isn't the hunger. The worst part is that you stop being surprised by it. At some point it just becomes the arithmetic of bad days.

You account for it. You plan around it. You send emails to your students about personal matters. You say you're tired when people ask."

Silence. Long enough that he wondered if he had said too much, if he had done the thing he always did and followed the thread too far, given someone more than they came for.

"Thank you for telling me that," she said.

He stopped.

Not: I'm sorry. Not: that sounds so hard. Not: what can I do. Just: thank you for telling me that. With the specific emphasis that meant she was not saying it to say something, she was saying it because she meant it, because she was genuinely grateful that he had trusted her with the architecture of the thing.

He looked at the window. The street was still doing its afternoon thing. A delivery truck had stopped and a woman was walking a child past it and the child was trying to look at the truck while also keeping pace and the whole small negotiation of it was visible and real.

He thought about Priya. About the patient smile. About the way he had learned to minimize before the minimization was requested. About the fact that he had never, in six years of marriage, described the levels to her.

Not because he had hidden it. She had known, in the general sense, that he had dark periods. But not the specific language, not the glass, not the interior map of it: he had never given that to anyone.

He had given it to Maya on a Thursday afternoon, four months after meeting her on a balcony in downtown Los Angeles, and she had said thank you.

"It's not —" he started.

"It is," she said simply. "It is hard. You don't have to qualify it."

"I was going to say it's not all of me."

"I know it's not all of you." Her voice was steady and warm in the particular way it was when she was saying something she had decided was true. "I've met all of you. The all-of-you is remarkable. The four days of not- quite-there doesn't change the all-of-you. It's just one of the rooms in the house."

He was quiet for a long time.

"Nobody has ever said it like that before," he said.

"Like what."

"Like it's just a room. Like there are other rooms and the house is still the house."

She said: "Because it is."

He sat with the reading chair and the window and the street and the phone warm in his hand and something in the interior of him that he did not have perfect language for shifted in the direction of open. Not resolved. Not fixed. Just: open. The way a door is open, which is not the same as the room being finished, but is the beginning of the room being available.

"I should let you go," he said.

"You don't have to."

"I know. I want to grade some papers. I'm behind."

"Okay." A pause. "Derek."

"Yeah."

"The AV Club review is still bad and you were right."

He laughed, the real laugh, the full one, the one that arrived without looking for it, and it was the first of that quality in seven days.

"I'll call you tonight," he said.

He called her at nine. They talked until midnight. He graded six papers in the afternoon, good comments, alive comments, the real version, and ate dinner that tasted like dinner, and the lamp was good, and the Coltrane was good, and the glass was thin.

Almost not there.


## Chapter Eighteen: The Other Side of the Camera

She invited him on a Saturday in March, two weeks after the telling- her phone call, because she had been thinking about it since then and had not been able to stop thinking about it and had eventually decided that the not-stopping meant she should do it.

She texted: do you want to come to Burbank.

Not a question exactly. He understood the difference. He said yes.

She had cleaned the apartment the night before, then stopped herself mid-cleaning and stood in the hallway holding a dust cloth and thought about what she was doing and why. She was not cleaning for him, she did not need to clean for him, he had seen her at her worst several times now and had not required cleanliness. She was cleaning because having someone come to her space for the first time was doing something to her that she had not anticipated, a specific quality of exposure that was different from anything she had felt on camera, which was counterintuitive and also, when she sat with it, made complete sense. The camera was a frame. The frame was a choice she made. This was just: here is the room.

Here is where I live. Here is what it looks like when no one is watching.

She put the dust cloth away and left the apartment as it was, which was already clean, because she was someone who kept things clean, which was itself a thing he would see.

* * *

He arrived at eleven. She buzzed him up and stood in the open doorway of the apartment and watched him come down the hall. He was in the usual jacket, the good one, carrying a paper bag from a bakery she recognized by the printing on the side.

"You didn't have to bring anything," she said.

"I know," he said. He looked past her into the apartment. Not rudely.

Just taking it in, the way he took things in, with the full attention that was one of the real things about him, that saw rather than glanced.

"Come in," she said.

He came in.

She watched him look at it. He didn't move quickly — he stood just inside the door and let the room be the room, the right rugs and the grandmother's teapot on the shelf and the print on the wall and the kitchen with the island where she ate most of her meals. He was reading it the way he read everything, the way he read a film or a record sleeve or a shelf of books: not cataloguing, connecting. Looking for the thread.

"It's quieter than I expected," he said.

"What did you expect?"

"I don't know. More evidence of the work. More camera equipment in the living room."

"The stream room is separate," she said. And then, before she had decided to: "Do you want to see it?"

He looked at her. Something in his expression that was careful and also very present. "If you want to show me."

She did. She found, standing in her hallway, that she did.

The stream room was the second bedroom, the door usually closed, the threshold that no one in her physical life crossed. She opened the door.

From the outside it looked like exactly what it was: a well-configured small room, the chair at the desk, the ring light off and therefore just a large circular object, the camera on its arm, the acoustic panels on two walls, the monitor and the peripheral tangle of cables that she kept meaning to organize and hadn't. Nothing magical about it. Nothing dramatic. Just a room built for a specific purpose, functional and considered.

She watched him look at it from the doorway. She was aware of what he was seeing: the room where the version of her that eleven million people knew was produced. The room where she had been nineteen years old and scared and had turned the ring light on anyway. The room she had spent the last seven years in, more hours in this chair than any other chair she owned, more time in this room than any other room, building the thing that was both her and not entirely her and that she had never been fully sure she could explain to anyone who hadn't watched it from the inside.

"You stream from in here," he said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Every stream."

"Every stream. I have a ritual. Forty-seven minutes before I go live.

Tea, the chair in the dark, breathing. Then the ring light."

He was quiet. He was looking at the ring light, the dark ring of it, the way it would look from behind the camera rather than in front of it.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"Yes."

"When the light is on and the camera is running — is it different? Is there a switch? Or is it just —" "It's me," she said. "That's what I'd tell you. The version in there is me. She's real. The version of me that learned what was worth giving to a room full of people, and leads with those things." She paused. "But lately I've been less sure about the line. Between the leading-with and the only- having."

He turned from the ring light and looked at her. The look that meant something had landed.

"That's the most honest thing you've said about it," he said.

"I know."

She pulled the door mostly closed. They went back to the kitchen and she made tea, the grandmother's pot, loose leaf, the ritual that preceded the other ritual. He sat at the island and watched her make it with the attentive quiet he brought to watching her do things, which she was still not entirely used to.

"The apartment is like you," he said. "The real version."

She handed him a mug. "What does that mean?"

"It means it's specific. Everything in it is a choice that makes sense.

The teapot and the print and the rugs, they're not decorator choices, they're your choices. There's a self in here." He looked at the shelf with the teapot.

"It's the self that exists before the camera comes on."

She sat across from him at the island. She looked at her apartment through his eyes, which was strange and clarifying. She had not thought of it as a self before. She had thought of it as a place she had arranged correctly. The difference between those two things was, she realized now, exactly the line she had been trying to locate.

"Nobody comes here," she said. "You're the first person I've let in in about eight months. I had a friend from college come for dinner in July and before that I can't remember."

"Why?"

"Because it's mine," she said. "Because outside is where the version of me lives that everyone expects, and in here is just me, and I haven't been sure until recently whether there was enough of the just-me to put in front of someone."

He held his mug. He didn't say anything for a moment. Then: "There is."

She looked at him across the kitchen island, in her apartment, on a Saturday in March, and felt the specific quality of being seen in the right kind of light, not the ring light, not the public light, just the ordinary warm lamp-light of her own home, and the feeling was not comfortable exactly, was not the warm click of the machine engaging, was something less smooth and more real than that. It was the feeling of a door open. The room available.

She refilled his tea.

"I'm going to ask you something annoying," she said.

"Okay."

"When you're having a bad week — the cold ones — does it help to have someone here? Or does it make it harder?"

He thought about it seriously, which she liked. He did not reach for the answer that would make her feel useful. "Depends on the person. With people who need something from me when I'm under, it's worse. With someone who can just be present without needing anything back." He paused. "It helps. It's the difference between being witnessed and being monitored."

"I want to be the first one," she said.

"I know," he said. "You already are."

She sat with that for a moment. Then: "Okay."

"Okay."

They spent the afternoon in the apartment. They made lunch from what was in the refrigerator, which was not much but was enough, and they talked about everything and nothing in the easy register that she had only ever found with two or three people in her life and that she was still slightly surprised to find herself in, the conversation that did not need to be anywhere, that was content to just be the conversation. He found a gap in her shelves and spent twenty minutes genuinely affronted by the absence of a specific film in her collection, which became a twenty-minute argument that ended with her ordering it and him writing the title on a receipt he found in his pocket so they would not forget to discuss it properly.

She walked him out at four. In the hallway he paused.

"Thank you for showing me," he said. He meant the stream room. She knew he meant the stream room.

"Thank you for coming," she said. She meant more than the apartment. He knew she meant more than the apartment.

She closed the door and stood in the hallway of her apartment, the apartment that was hers and that she had let someone into, and she was twenty-six years old and the ring light was off in the second bedroom and the grandmother's teapot was warm on the shelf and she was, in this moment, entirely herself and entirely known, and the two things were the same thing, and she stood there with that for a long time before she moved.


## Chapter Nineteen: The Asymmetry

She saw it mid-scroll, already an hour into the morning. Tea made, the good kind, the grandmother's pot still warm on the shelf.

A thread. Someone had done the arithmetic — the photo, the blurry cinema still, February — and laid it out in full. okay so I think I figured out who maya chen has been seeing and you are NOT going to believe this. The kind of thorough that wanted credit for the finding. The replies already in the hundreds.

She closed the app.

Not embarrassed. That was the thing to locate first. Not embarrassed, not ashamed. What she felt was more specific: Derek had not agreed to become a publicly nameable fact. This was her world. Not his. And the world was now pointing at him without his knowledge or consent, and she didn't like it, and she didn't know what to do with the not-liking that wouldn't itself become a kind of management.

She made more tea. She let it get a little too strong.

She texted him at eight-fifteen, after she had eaten something and the tea was gone and she had stopped catastrophizing. She told him about the comment. She said: you don't have to do anything. I am just going to let it be what it is. A blurry picture of two people leaving a cinema.

He responded: okay. Are you okay?

She responded: yes. A little — yeah, I am okay. Are you?

He responded: I am not going to confirm or deny the photo. I am just going to be a person. That seems right.

She read that twice.

She wrote back: that is exactly right.

She put the phone down and smiled and the smile was the real one.

She was going to have to be more careful. Not with him. With herself.

## Chapter Twenty: The Saddest Kind of Careful

It was a Sunday in March and it had rained the night before, which in Los Angeles was enough to make the whole city feel briefly chastened and new, the air carrying the scent of wet eucalyptus and the particular clean that felt borrowed rather than earned. They were on his couch with the remains of an afternoon: two mugs, a book she had been reading and set face-down on the cushion beside her, the Criterion of Chungking Express on the shelf where he had replaced it after the cinema screening, its spine a small fact in the room.

Not: if you want to. Not: you don't have to. Just: tell me. Which was the way she asked things, and which had stopped surprising him, and which he had come to understand as a form of respect, the assumption that he was capable of answering, the refusal to pre-empt him with softening.

He was quiet for a moment. Not resisting. Just locating the right place to begin, the way you find the right entry point into a text before you can teach it.

"Her name was Priya," he said. "We were together for three years and married for two. She was a software architect. She was very good at her job and very organized and she had a quality I mistook, for a long time, for understanding."

"What was it actually?"

"Tolerance." He said it without bitterness, which cost something. "She was tolerant of me. Of the way I talked about films, the way I followed threads, the streaming, the podcast. All of it. He turned his mug in his hands. "It took me a long time to understand that gratitude for being tolerated is not the same as being known."

Maya was quiet. She had the quality, in these moments, of someone who had switched off every competing signal. No phone, no peripheral awareness, just the conversation. He had noticed this about her. The professional listener deploying her full capacity in private. It was different from performance. Performance had a direction, an audience. This was just: here.

"She made me feel," he said slowly, "a specific way about the things I cared about. Not directly. She never said your enthusiasm is exhausting or your fixations are too much. She didn't have to. It was more like the slight hesitation before she agreed to watch something, with me. The way she would ask how much longer the podcast was going to be before she put it on. The patience. The visible, careful patience." He set the mug down. "And the thing is, I understood it intellectually. I knew that not everyone was going to share my investment in the same things. "It is different," Maya said.

"And I knew it was different. Somewhere. I knew. He paused. "I called that love. For five years I called it love."

"How did it end?"

He had told this story before, to Rajan, to his therapist, to the ceiling of his apartment at two in the morning in the weeks after, and each telling had been slightly different, slightly more refined toward accuracy, the way repeated tellings either drift toward mythology or toward truth depending on the teller. He had been trying, for two years now, to tell it toward truth.

"She was sleeping with my best friend," he said. "Arun. We'd been friends since grad school. Maya's breath changed slightly. Not dramatically. Just the small involuntary shift of someone who has been hit by a thing they were braced for but not braced enough.

"It was proof," he said. "That's the only word I have for it. It was proof of the thing my family had been arguing my whole life: that the real version of me, the unedited one, was not something someone could actually want.

That the best I could hope for was tolerance. And when the tolerance ran out." He stopped. Started again. "I know that's not a rational way to read what happened. I know it was about her choices and Arun's choices and things that had nothing to do with my worth as a person. I know all of that.

I've had it explained to me professionally." A small, dry pause. "I still believed the proof."

Silence settled in the room. Not uncomfortable. The kind of silence that a true thing makes when it lands.

"I became very precise after that," he said. "About what I let matter.

About who I let close. I built the podcast and the streaming and the classroom and I put everything I had into those things and I told myself it was because they were meaningful, which they are, but it was also because they were safe. The frame makes the intensity legible. Inside the frame I could be fully myself and it was okay because there was a structure for it and people opted into the structure and nobody had to tolerate me they just had to watch. Or listen. He stopped. He had gone further than he meant to. He could feel it, the slight exposure of having said the last part, the part he usually kept. He waited for the recalibration, the managed warmth, the kind thing said slightly beside the point.

"That's the saddest kind of careful," Maya said.

He looked at her. He had expected the kind thing, the supportive thing. She gave him neither, exactly. She gave him the accurate thing, which was harder and more useful.

"Building a life where people can close the tab when you're too much." She said it without pity, which was the only way it could have been said, the only register in which he could have received it. "You made yourself opt-in because you were afraid of being opted out of. And you called it boundaries."

He didn't say anything.

She was quiet for a moment. Looking at the shelf. Something on the shelf she couldn't identify from where she was sitting. Something moved through her face — a recognition that arrived and turned inward. The way you look when you've said a true thing about someone else and understood, mid-sentence, that it's also true about you.

"I do the same thing." Not quickly. Not to fill the silence. Slowly, the way something comes when it's been found rather than assembled.

She followed them. "It doesn't mean I was too much. It just means the stream ended."

She looked at the shelf for another moment. Then at him.

"I didn't know I was doing that," she said. "Until I heard myself say it."

The room was quiet. The wet eucalyptus smell came in from somewhere outside. He sat with what she had said, and she sat with it too, and it was the first time in the conversation that both of them were sitting with something instead of one person offering it to the other.

"Yes," he said. "That's exactly it."

The rain had stopped sometime in the night but the wet smell was still there, still the borrowed-clean smell of a city that had been briefly washed.

He sat with it, her and the smell and the mug and the Criterion on the shelf, and something in the glass, the permanent fixture of his interior architecture, was very thin today. Almost not there. Not gone. He understood it was not going to be gone. But thin enough that the warmth from her side of it arrived without translation, without the slight delay that was the glass at its usual depth, and he sat with the warmth and did not try to say anything about it because there was nothing to say that would be more accurate than just sitting there.

"I'm sorry about Arun," she said, after a while. "Specifically about him.

The particular cruelty of it being him."

"Yeah," Derek said. "That part still... yeah."

She didn't try to fix it. She just let it be what it was, which was the thing that had cost him the most and the thing he was least interested in performing recovery from. She let it sit in the room, acknowledged and unresolved, the way real things sit.

Later she made tea. She did it without asking, using the grandmother's pot because she had learned it was the right pot for this hour of the afternoon, and she brought both mugs back to the couch and handed him his and it was hot and it was the right thing and he drank it and the room was warm and outside the eucalyptus was still wet and the glass was thin.

He thought: I didn't edit that. I said the true version and I did not edit it and she is still here.

He held the thought carefully, the way you hold something you are not certain is fragile and don't want to find out by dropping it.


## Chapter Twenty-One: What Your Father Used to Do

Her mother arrived on a Tuesday with two suitcases and the specific energy of a woman who had been planning this visit for three months and had approximately forty-seven things she intended to do in five days. Her name was Linda Chen and she was fifty-eight and had the quality, which Maya had tried to describe to Jess once and failed, of being both the warmest and the most accurate person in any room, the combination that made her simultaneously the person you most wanted to talk to and the person you were most careful around.

"That's just my face, Mom."

"Your face has many expressions. That particular one is thinking." She set her bag down and looked at the apartment with the attentive survey she always gave to spaces, cataloguing. "The rug is new."

"I got it in November."

"Mom."

"It's a question."

"Yes. I've been eating. I've been eating very well, actually."

Her mother looked at her over the kitchen island. Maya recognized the look. It was the look that meant: I have more information than you think I do, and I'm deciding when to deploy it.

"I didn't use your name."

## Chapter Twenty-Two: The Machine in the Room

The invitation had come three weeks earlier, a Streamcon gala, the kind of industry event that existed at the intersection of genuine celebration and mandatory visibility, where attendance was itself a form of statement and absence was also a statement, just a different one. She registered all of this in the time it took to cross the threshold.

Someone said her name from across the room. She turned.

She moved through the room with the ease of someone who had mapped it before entering. Dani first, the platform exec, warm and specific — the thing about the Q4 numbers, the kid's recital because she'd mentioned it at the last one. She asked. Dani lit up. She actually liked Dani.

She actually wanted to know. The wanting to know and the strategic value of it were the same action and she had long since stopped being able to tell them apart.

She introduced Derek. This is Derek, he teaches English, he does a film podcast that's really great. She meant this. She was proud of him. She touched his arm. Dani was saying something and she leaned back in.

A sponsor cluster. Three people whose names she knew, whose metrics she knew, whose quarterly review was the following Tuesday. She was warm. She was specific. She said the thing about the new format and watched them relax by increments and felt the clean satisfaction of it.

She glanced back at Derek. He was standing with his drink. He looked good. The dark jacket doing exactly what it was supposed to do. She'd get back to him.

The room moved around her. She moved through it. Someone from a competing platform who was also a friend — genuinely a friend — and the warmth was real and the register slightly elevated, two people who knew what they were each doing and respected it. Someone she didn't know well who wanted to know her better. Gary, who always needed too much time and got it anyway.

She was good at this. The room was a language she was fluent in. The fluency felt good — the pleasure of moving through a complex system without friction, of being exactly what a room needed.

She was having a good night.

* * *

Jess found her near the back bar and they talked for real for three minutes.

Jess said the evening seemed to be running well. Maya said yes. It was. She felt good. The room felt right.

She looked across the room at Derek.

He was by the windows. Looking at the city below. Not performing alone, not doing the thing people did at parties when they wanted to look like they weren't alone. Just standing there, actually looking at the city, with the stillness that was one of his specific qualities.

She knew that stillness. She had been inside it. His apartment, his attention, the full weight of it arriving without agenda. She knew what he looked like when he was present.

He was present. He was entirely present. He was just — somewhere she wasn't.

Something moved through her chest. She didn't reach for it. Gary was back at her elbow, something about a panel, and she turned toward him and her face was warm and the evening continued.

She didn't look at the windows again.

Not because she was avoiding it.

She just didn't look.

* * *

He watched her not look at him.

She kept including him after that. That was the thing he hadn't prepared for. She touched his arm when something was funny, said his name in the natural way of someone making sure a person feels present, looked at him during the parts that were general enough to admit everyone.

She was doing everything right. He could see the doing of it.

And he could not feel her.

That was the precise thing, standing there with her hand on his arm, her voice saying his name. He could not feel her. He knew what it felt like when she was actually there. He had months of knowing. The scallion pancake argument on the freeway forty minutes ago. Her hand warm in the crook of his arm through the lobby. Mid-sentence through the parking structure, the story still finding its ending when they came through the door. He had felt the specific temperature of her presence for months and he knew it exactly, the way you know the weight of something you carry often, and standing in this room with her beside him he could feel the shape of where it wasn't.

She was there. The feeling of her wasn't. And the gap between those two things had a size, and in this room, with six hundred people and the warm light and her name in everyone's mouth, he could feel the size of it clearly for the first time.

He thought: I have felt this before.

Not here. Not at this scale. But the edge of it: in the manager call at the Silver Lake lunch, the warmth recalibrating, pointed somewhere else for two minutes. In the video, the cruise control taking over in the room where he'd been the subject. In the photo, six hours ahead of the text. The slight wrongness he'd routed back to his own terms, his own wall, his own choice.

Each time a small version of this. Each time the shape of the gap, briefly visible, before the warmth came back and he let it go.

He had been feeling the edge of this for months without knowing what the edge was the edge of. Now he was inside it. Now he could see the whole thing.

* * *

The photographer found them near the drinks table forty minutes in.

He saw it coming before she did. The man with the camera, the practiced arc of a professional. He saw Maya register it and do the thing — the chin slightly down, the weight shift, the half-step forward, the body's accumulated knowledge of how to be recorded.

He stepped back.

Not angry. Not performing not-angry. He stepped back because the frame was hers and stepping into someone else's frame without being asked was not something he did. He watched the camera click and thought: she doesn't know I stepped back. She's already somewhere the camera takes her.

She turned afterward, said sorry, I should have — and he said it's fine.

Then Gary appeared across the room and she said one second.

One second was eleven minutes.

He stood by the windows and looked at the city below and he did not think about whether this was fixable or fair or whose fault it was. He thought about the car. Forty-five minutes ago. Her foot almost tucked under her, the window down two inches, mid-sentence and warm and entirely herself. He had been in possession of her completely, and then the door had opened and something had been redistributed, and he was standing here with his drink and the city outside and the outline of her absence arranged precisely in the space where she had been.

She came back. Said sorry. He said it's okay.

Jess arrived and for twenty minutes it was actually good; the two of them talking like people, the machine receding, Maya being Maya, following his thread, looking at him when he said something worth looking at. His body responded to her hand on his arm the way it always did. He let himself be in it.

And then someone arrived she needed to speak to and the thread dropped and the machine came back. He watched it come back, the click of it, and he stood beside her and the warmth was all around him and none of it was for him and he had no name for the feeling except that he had been feeling it, in smaller versions, for months.

He understood now what the feeling was.

She loved him. He was certain of that. And Maya Chen did not know him. And those two things were true at the same time, in the same body, and the distance between them was not a fight or a fracture. It was the seam. The seam nobody had stood at yet and pointed to. And he was standing at it now and he had his hand out and there was nothing there to touch.

* * *

In the car on the way home he was quiet.

She drove. The freeway unspooled ahead of them, the Los Angeles night doing its indifferent thing. She waited for the silence to soften. It didn't.

"Tell me," she said finally.

A pause. "I felt like a prop tonight."

She glanced at him. "You wanted to come. You asked to come."

"I know I did."

"So I don't know what you expected." She was not being unkind. She was being logical, the way she was logical when something didn't add up and she was working through it. "It's my job, Derek. It's six hundred people and panels I'm on and sponsors I have obligations to. I introduced you. I came back for you. I'm not sure what else I was supposed to do."

"I know what it is," he said.

"Then you knew going in that this was what it would be like."

He looked at the window. The freeway signs came and went.

"You introduced me as Derek-he-teaches-English," he said. "And then you were already three steps away."

"That's how you introduce someone at a networking event. You give them context, you — "

"I know how it works."

"Then you know I wasn't doing anything wrong." She heard herself and the logic of it was complete and correct. "That's just what those rooms are. I've been in those rooms for five years. That's what they are."

He didn't say anything.

"You were alone for eleven minutes," she said, and he heard the concession in it, the slight giving of ground. "I know. I got stuck with Gary.

That happens. It would happen to anyone's —" She stopped. She had been about to say anyone's guest and she heard it before it arrived and stopped herself, but it was already there.

He heard it too.

"I'm not a guest," he said. Quietly.

"I didn't say —" "I know you didn't." He turned from the window and looked at her.

The look was not angry. It was something past anger, further back. "I know what I walked into. I asked to come because I wanted to understand what that part of your life was. And now I understand it."

She pulled up to his building. The engine idled.

"What does that mean?" she said.

He was quiet for a long moment. The glass was starting, he could feel it, the early cold of it at the edges of everything.

"Nothing," he said. "It's late. I'm tired."

"Derek —" "I don't want to do this in the car." He said it evenly, without heat.

"I'm tired and you're tired and whatever this is it's not going to be better in a parking spot at midnight."

She sat with the engine running. She had introduced him. She had come back. She had wanted him there. The logic of it was complete and she sat inside the completeness of it and waited.

"Are we okay?" she said.

He looked at her. "Yeah," he said. "We're okay."

She believed it. He could see her believe it. She had heard tiredness and received it as arrival. He did not correct her. He got out of the car.

At the building door he turned and raised a hand and she raised one back and then the door was closed.

She drove home. The freeway was quiet at this hour, the city stripping itself down to the minimum. She parked. She went upstairs. She was tired in the particular way she got tired after a long event, not sleepy, just emptied, the social expenditure sitting in her like a debt not yet tallied.

She did not think about the car. She changed out of the blazer and hung it up and she did not think about the car. She cleaned the kitchen, which did not need cleaning. She wiped the counters and straightened what was already straight and ran a cloth over the grandmother's teapot until it gleamed on the shelf. She went to bed. She was certain they were okay. He had said they were okay.

* * *

He sat on the couch in the dark for a long time.

He did not turn on a light. He sat in the dark the way you sit in the dark when there is too much to look at and the lamp would just make it visible.

He was not angry. Not angry. Clear. Clear in a way that was almost worse, because anger had a shape you could work with and this was just the cold precision of a thing understood.

He had gone to that room to find out if there was a version of her world he could exist in.

The answer was: she doesn't bring people into her world. She brings them adjacent to it. She is warm about it, she is genuine about it, she touches your arm and says your name and comes back after eleven minutes, and none of it is the same as being in it. The version of her that ran that room had never met him. It knew Derek-he-teaches-English, warm and pleasant and slightly decorative, and that was all it had been given.

He thought about the small moments. All of them, laid out now without the explanations he had given them at the time. The manager call and the work mode and the way she had named it like a minor inconvenience. The video, the cruise control, the four days of short texts after. The photo he had found out about six hours after it went live. The asymmetry she had named herself on the freeway, which he had received with a yeah, I've noticed that and let sit. Each one reasonable in isolation.

Each one absorbed and moved on from because none of them individually were worth making a thing of.

Together they were the thing. And he had seen the together of it tonight, in the room, with the six hundred people and the automatic touch and the eleven minutes. He had been right to see it. He did not know yet what to do with being right.

He was in love with her. He was certain of that. And for the first time, sitting in the dark with the glass at two and the city outside, he was not certain that loving her was going to be enough. Whether the seam between them was findable. Whether he was asking for something the machine's architecture didn't have room for.

He thought: she thinks we're okay.

He thought: I'm not okay. And I don't have the right words yet, and the glass is coming up, and I am going to have to find the words before the glass gets too thick to say them through.

He went to bed. Somewhere across the city she was asleep, certain they were okay. He was not certain. The not-certainty sat in him like something that had finally arrived and was not going to be comfortable to house.
