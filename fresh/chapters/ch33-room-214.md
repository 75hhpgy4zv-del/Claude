# Chapter Thirty-Three
## Room 214

The room is small and the air conditioning is loud and set too cold, and there is a desk against one wall with a kettle on it and a folder about the hotel, and one bed, and a chair with his clothes over the back of it, and a window that does not open with the parking lot behind it.

The bag was on the floor by the closet with the top of it open.

She saw the card in it before she saw anything else in the room.

"Come in," said Derek.

She came in and the door shut behind her and the air conditioning was the only sound in there.

"What happened to you," she said again.

"Do you want to sit down?"

"No."

"Okay."

"Don't handle me," said Ha-eun.

He stood in the middle of a hotel room in Bocaue and put his hands in his pockets and took them out again.

"They didn't renew me," he said. "You know that. That's the sixteenth of June. What I didn't tell you is the rest of it, and I'm going to tell you the rest of it in order, and I'd like to do it without stopping, because if you ask me a question in the middle I'll answer the question and then we'll be somewhere else."

"Fine."

"On the nineteenth I took the keys back. There's a woman in the front office called Wendy who wouldn't take them off me for about four minutes." He was not looking at the floor and he was not looking at her. He had found a point on the wall about a foot to the left of her head. "I went up and got my things out of Room 14. There's a chair in that room I bought in 2018 that nobody has ever asked me to justify, and I left it, because taking it out would have meant deciding something."

"Derek—"

"And Ferreira came out to the parking lot and stood four feet away from me because she'd been told to be careful, and she couldn't tell me anything, and that's the worst thing that happened that day and she didn't do anything wrong."

The air conditioning cycled and came back on.

"Then I went home," he said. "And I stopped going out."

He told her the whole of it and it took about twenty minutes and he did not stop once.

The box by the door with the mug still wrapped in a sweater, from the Friday to the Sunday. Hector knocking about the crate wood and saying his name twice through a door and then dragging four sheets of plywood around the side of the building on his own. The milk he opened on a Sunday and smelled and put back in the fridge.

Deepa outside the door with a dish on one hip, talking through it, saying *love you, bye.*

Halina's email on the Tuesday, which he read four times and understood and did not answer, and again on the Friday, and *ok* on the Monday.

The photograph of a theater in London that Rahul sent, and the reply he typed and did not send.

He got to the twenty-third of June and stopped for the first time.

"You called," he said.

"I called."

"You asked me what the number was."

Ha-eun stood by the desk with the kettle on it.

"You said a Three," she said.

"I said a Three and it was a Five."

The air conditioning did the thing it does.

"I said it was a spiteful Three and I said I was a bit distracted with the school thing, and then I gave you every single fact about the school in the right order with the dates in, because that's what a report sounds like." His voice had not changed at all. "You asked me where I was sitting and I said the kitchen. You asked me which light was on and I said the one over the sink."

"Where were you."

"In the chair by the window."

"Which light was on."

"None of them," said Derek.

She did not say anything for about nine seconds.

Then she picked the folder about the hotel up off the desk and put it back down, which is not a thing she decided to do.

"You *promised* me a number."

"I know."

"You gave me a number in *April*, in a parking lot, off the top of your head, and you had the year in your phone since January, and I have taken *every single number you have ever given me* — " she had turned around " — I have taken all of them, at face value, on a phone, in nine countries, because you told me in a kitchen in March that if it moved you'd tell me the day it moved. Those are your words. *The day it moves.*"

"They are."

"So say it."

"I lied to you on the twenty-third of June," said Derek.

"Say the rest."

"And on the twenty-sixth, and on the thirtieth, and on the second of July, because you asked four times and I said a Three four times, and by the second of July I hadn't been outside in eleven days."

Ha-eun put both hands flat on the desk and stood there with her head down.

"Do you know what I did on the twenty-third," she said.

"No."

"I got in a car in Cologne and I sat in it in a loading bay for eleven minutes with the door shut before I let them take me anywhere, because you'd said the word *spiteful* and I didn't like it and I could not work out why." She was not shouting. "And then I decided I was being ridiculous, because you had given me a *number*, and the number was a Three, and you have never in fourteen months got a number wrong."

"No."

"I *checked*, Derek. I checked and you passed."

"I know."

"You passed on purpose."

"Yes," said Derek.

"Say what you did."

"I ran the list."

"*Say it.*"

"You've read it four times," he said. "It's on page two hundred and twelve. Response times, whether the texts get short, whether I stop following the thread, whether I cancel things, and the shelf." He had not moved. "I answered inside the hour every time. I stayed lateral. I did the whole Hamburg thing about the man apologizing in three languages and it took me forty minutes to build and it was funny. And I gave you the shelf without being asked, because giving it to you unprompted was the strongest thing on the list."

Ha-eun stood by a desk in a hotel room and looked at him.

"You used my own—"

"Yes."

"You used the *book*."

"I wrote the list," said Derek. "I wrote it down for a woman in a kitchen in 2021 so that somebody would know what to watch for, and I have spent three years being glad it was in there, and in June I sat in a chair in the dark and went through it item by item and worked out how to pass it."

"How long did that take you."

"About a minute and a half."

"*A minute and a half.*"

"It's my list," said Derek.

She sat down on the end of the bed because her legs did it.

She had been on a stage two hours earlier. There is a specific hour after a show where the body starts telling the truth about what it has just done, and it was arriving now, in a hotel room in Bocaue, at about twenty to one in the morning.

"Why," she said.

"Because you were in Cologne."

"That is not—"

"You were in Cologne, and then Warsaw, and then you had a travel day that wasn't a travel day, and there were fifty-one dates left." He had not moved from the middle of the room. "And if I'd said *five* you would have got on a plane."

"*Yes.*"

"I know. That's why I didn't say it."

"That was not your—" and she stopped, and put a hand over her eyes for a second, and took it away. "You don't get to make that decision for me. You have never got to make that decision for me and you *know* that, you're the one who — you're the person who taught me that. In a kitchen. About a *lamp*."

"I know."

"So why."

Derek stood in a hotel room and did not have his hands in his pockets any more.

"Because there was nothing you could have done," he said, "and you would have done it anyway, and it would have cost you the tour and it would have got out inside a week and then it would have been my fault as well."

Ha-eun sat on the end of a bed and looked at the carpet.

Because she had heard it.

She had heard it while he was still saying it — the shape of it, the sentence underneath the sentence, the arithmetic of a person deciding on somebody else's behalf what they can carry — and she had been running it herself since the sixteenth of April and had never once heard it out loud in a room before.

"When did you last sleep properly," she said.

"That's not—"

"When."

"I don't know. May."

"Right," said Ha-eun, and put her hands in her lap, and did the hardest thing she has done in nine years of doing hard things, which was to keep talking. "My last day off was the sixteenth of April. That's four months and thirteen days. You asked me in July and I told you the date and then I made a joke about Miru's baby and you let me have it, and you shouldn't have let me have it, and I *made* you let me have it, because I have been making people let me have things since I was nineteen and I am extremely good at it."

Derek did not say anything.

"There's nine minutes in a show day where Bo does my eyes and nobody talks to me. That's it. That's the whole of it." She was looking at the carpet. "That's all the time in a day that belongs to me, and I plan the whole day around getting to it, and I have never told anybody that, including Bo, who is *in* it." Her voice did something on the last part. "And tonight somebody came in with a tablet at four minutes and it was nothing, it was two lines of copy, and she was polite and she was quick and she was doing her job. And I sat in that chair with my eyes shut and counted down the four minutes I had left and I got nothing out of them, and then I went and did a show."

"Ha-eun."

"And I didn't tell you any of that either," she said, "because you were in Alhambra with no job and I thought, he cannot hold this, and I will hold it, because I am the one who's *working.*"

Neither of them said anything for a while.

The air conditioning cycled. Somewhere below them a car door shut in the parking lot and a man said something in Tagalog and somebody else laughed.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"Don't."

"I'm going to—"

"Don't apologize to me," said Derek.

He crossed the room and sat down on the floor with his back against the bed, about two feet from her knee, which put him lower than her and not looking at her, and she understood exactly what he had done and why, and it did not stop it working.

"You have been on a stage for four and a half months," he said. "In eleven countries. With no day off since April, and no way to say any of this to anybody, because Ba-da works for you and Miru's got a baby and I was seven thousand miles away being *fine* down a phone." He had one hand flat on the carpet. "You didn't hide anything from me. You did the same thing I did and you did it for the same reason and you did it while doing forty-four shows."

"That's not—"

"I'm not asking you," said Derek. "I'm telling you it's finished. It's done, it's not a thing between us, and I'm not going to talk about it again."

Ha-eun sat on the end of a bed in a hotel in Bocaue.

"You don't get to just—"

"I do, actually," he said. "That's the one thing I've got and I'm using it."

She started crying at about ten to one and it was not the kind she had done on the ramp.

That one had come up and been beautiful and had gone out to twenty thousand people and had cost her about six seconds. This one came out sideways, with no shape to it, in a hotel room with the air conditioning on, and it went on for a while, and she made a sound at one point that she had not made in front of anybody since she was fifteen years old in a stairwell in Ilsan.

Derek did not get up off the floor and did not touch her and did not say anything at all.

When it slowed down he said, "Have you eaten?"

"Don't."

"I've got a thing from the bakery on the corner and it's about nine hours old, and there's a kettle, and that's it, that's the whole of the room."

"I'm not hungry."

"Okay," said Derek, and got up, and put the kettle on anyway, and stood at the desk with his back to her while it went, which took about two minutes, and did not fill any of it.

She watched him do that.

He is thinner and he has gray coming in on one side and he has been awake for something like thirty hours, and he is standing at a desk in a hotel room in a country he has never been to, waiting for a kettle, with his back to her so that she can have two minutes.

"What did you do," she said. "After Lucky."

"Sorry?"

"You said Lucky came on the Thursday and stayed the night and left on the Friday morning. What did you do after that."

Derek turned around with two cups.

"I wrote a chapter," he said.

She did not understand it for a second.

"You wrote—"

"Four pages. On the Monday. Twenty past nine in the morning until about twelve-thirty." He put a cup down on the bedside table next to her. "It's chapter four. It's the leak."

Ha-eun sat very still.

"You haven't written anything since 2022."

"No."

"You've got four hundred pages of—"

"Four hundred and six, and I didn't open them," he said. "I opened a new one."

"Is it good."

"It's the best thing I've done."

He said that without any performance on it at all, in the voice he uses for a fact, and she has known him for sixteen months and has never once heard him say a sentence like that about his own work.

"Say what it's about."

"It's Maya on a Sunday morning in a kitchen with a ring on," said Derek. "She's had it since Saturday and nobody knows and somebody wants to know when they're going to announce it, and she says she wants a few days first. Before it's anyone else's." He sat back down on the floor. "And then on the Monday she wears it to a coffee place and somebody photographs her hand."

Ha-eun put both hands around a cup she had not asked for.

"You wrote that in July."

"I wrote that in July."

"After the school. After the—" She stopped. "After the Five."

"Four days after Lucky left."

She looked at him on the floor with his back against the bed.

"That's horrible," she said.

"Yes."

"That's — you got *that* out of *that*?"

"Yes," said Derek. "And I was pleased about it, and I sat in that room at one in the afternoon and I was pleased, and there was nobody there, and I didn't put it down anywhere."

"How much of it is there."

"Fourteen chapters."

Ha-eun looked up.

"*Fourteen.*"

"A hundred and twenty pages. Thirty thousand words, near enough." He had both hands around his own cup and had not drunk any of it either. "Since the eleventh of July. I've done six days a week and I have not had another thing to do."

"Fourteen chapters is — " She stopped and did the arithmetic and he watched her do it. "That's all of it. That's everything up to now."

"It's everything up to now."

"The leak's in it."

"The leak's in it."

"The school?"

"The school's in it and it's worse in the book," said Derek, "because in the book they come to the building."

She held the cup and did not say the next one for about four seconds.

"Am I in it."

"No," said Derek.

"Derek—"

"Maya's in it. Maya is thirty-one, and she is an only child, and her mother drives over from Alhambra and lets herself in, and she does her own shopping, and she has never in her life had anybody drive her anywhere." He looked at her properly for the first time since she came in. "You're not in it. What's in it is what it costs. That's mine and I paid for it."

Ha-eun sat on the end of a bed in a hotel in Bocaue.

"Can I read it."

"Yes."

"When."

"It's in the bag," said Derek.

She turned her head and looked at the bag by the closet with the top of it open and the card in it, and did not say anything at all for about six seconds.

"You brought it."

"I printed it on the twenty-sixth and it's in a plastic thing with a clip on it," he said. "I nearly took it out at the airport, because there was a version of this where you didn't see the sign and I flew home with a hundred and twenty pages in a bag, which would have been *funny* — " and then his voice did something on the last word and he stopped, and started again. "It's yours. Take it in the car."

"Who else has read it?"

"Nobody."

"Halina?"

"Nobody, Ha-eun."

She looked at him.

"You've got a contract," she said. "You were optioned for a second book in 2022. There is a woman at Aperture whose *job* is this book and she has been sending you emails about it for three years."

"Priyanka. Yes."

"Does she know there's a hundred and twenty pages of it?"

"No."

"Are you going to tell her?"

Derek sat on the floor of a hotel room in Bocaue with his back against the bed.

"I don't know," he said. "I haven't thought about it once. Not one time since the eleventh of July, and I've noticed that I haven't, and I've decided not to look at why."

Ha-eun sat on the end of the bed and did not say anything for a moment.

"Then why did you print it."

"Because you asked me what came next," said Derek.

He did not look up when he said it.

"In 2022. You read a book that stops in the middle of the thing you actually wanted to know about, and you were so annoyed about it that you threw it at an airplane, and then you spent your own money to make somebody finish it." He had one hand flat on the carpet. "Nobody has ever asked me a question like that in my life. And it took me three years to be able to answer it, and I'm sorry it took three years, and that's the answer. It doesn't have to go anywhere. It doesn't have to be a book. You asked and I made you one."

"How does it end?"

Derek sat on the floor with his back against the bed.

"It doesn't," he said. "It stops on the twelfth of August, because that's where I am."

"Say that properly."

"I can't write a thing I haven't had." He said it the way he says a fact. "Not a room, not a name — those I can make up all day. The feeling underneath it has to be one I've actually had, and if it isn't I can hear it, and I stop. That's why the first book ends where it ends. That's what the four hundred and six pages are."

Ha-eun sat on the end of a bed with a cup she was not drinking.

"So the last chapter—"

"There isn't one to write yet."

---

She got there at about one-thirty.

Not deliberately. She had been on a stage at eight and in a hotel room at midnight and had cried at ten to one and had drunk half a cup of something at twenty past, and somewhere after that the last of it came off her — the whole apparatus, the four and a half months of it, the thing she has been holding with both hands since April — and what was left was a person of twenty-seven in a hooded top on the end of a bed who had not slept properly since Osaka.

Derek was on the floor.

She said, "Say it."

"No," said Derek.

She looked at him.

"Say the—"

"You've asked me for that on a phone eleven times since April and it hasn't worked once," he said, "and it's not going to work now either, and I'm not doing it."

"Derek."

"You can't get there on your own any more," he said. "You haven't been able to since Fukuoka. I've heard you try to do it in a parking lot in Warsaw."

He got up off the floor.

And he did not ask her, and he did not say the name, and he did not wait for her to put her arms out.

He crouched down in front of her and took her shoes off.

One and then the other, unhurried, with a hand behind the heel of each one, and he put them side by side against the wall under the window where she would find them in the dark.

He took the cup out of her hand — she was still holding it and had stopped drinking it about ten minutes earlier — and put it on the bedside table out of the way of her arm.

"Derek."

"Mm."

"I've got—"

"I know what you've got," he said.

He got the two pillows off the far side of the bed and put them where he wanted them, one on top of the other and slightly over, and pulled the cover down, and then he put one hand flat between her shoulder blades and moved her.

She resisted for about a second. Not with any weight in it — the amount a person resists when the resisting is a habit and not a decision — and then she went, and lay down on her side, and he pulled the cover up over her and up to the top of her arm and tucked it under once with two fingers.

She said, "I've got a car at six fifteen."

"I know."

"Ji-won's in the—"

"I know," said Derek, and sat down on the floor with his back against the bed where he had been, about a foot from her face, and turned the lamp off.

Then he said, "Gracie."

---

She did not answer for about four seconds and then she made a noise that was not a word.

"Again," she said.

"Gracie."

"*Again.*"

"Gracie," said Derek.

She got one hand out from under the cover and put it flat on the side of his neck, which is a thing she has done twice, and left it there, and he did not move.

"You don't have to be strong any more," he said. "Not tonight and not in this room. There's nobody in here and there's nothing to do and there's a man in the parking lot whose entire job for the next five hours is that nobody comes up those stairs."

"There's a six-fifteen."

"There's a six-fifteen and I'll get you in the car at six fifteen and I'll put your shoes on you myself if I have to," said Derek. "Between now and then you have got nothing to do."

Ha-eun lay on her side in the dark in a hotel room in Bocaue with her hand on a man's neck.

"Say it again," she said.

"Gracie."

"*Good*," said Gracie, and went to sleep in about ninety seconds, and did not move for four hours and ten minutes, and Derek sat on the floor with his back against the bed for the whole of it and did not sleep at all.
