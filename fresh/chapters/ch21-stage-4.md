# Chapter Twenty-One
## Stage 4

They built his kitchen in a warehouse in Sun Valley and nobody told her it was going to be like that.

She had been on sets. She had been on about forty of them — video shoots, ad shoots, the two dramas she had turned down and visited anyway, a thing in Bangkok with eleven hundred extras — and she had understood a soundstage as a large room where a smaller room is pretended.

She came around the corner of the flat at ten past seven in the morning on the first day of principal photography and stopped dead.

The lemon tree was in the pot. Dying, correctly.

---

She stood in the doorway for about a minute.

It was wrong in the way a thing is wrong when it has been built rather than lived in: the counter was four inches too deep, because a camera has to get past it, and the window over the sink looked onto a photograph of a wall, and the fourth side of the room was not there at all, and there were nine people standing in the place where the fourth wall should have been, drinking coffee and talking about a cable.

And everything else was exact.

The hook by the door was on the left. The Polish *Robocop* poster was in the hallway and the frame was the right frame. The kettle was the kettle. There was a ring on the counter where a mug goes, painted on, by a person whose job that is.

She went in.

Nobody stopped her, because she was the lead and the set was hers, and she walked into a reproduction of a two-bedroom apartment in Alhambra at ten past seven in the morning and put her hand flat on a counter that was four inches too deep.

---

The shelf was wrong.

She found it in about nine seconds and she found it because she knew.

It ran the length of the wall the way it ran the length of his wall, and it had the steelbooks on it and the Criterions and the two art books, and somebody had done an enormous amount of work — she recognized eleven of the spines from three feet away — and they had put them in alphabetical order.

Ha-eun stood in front of it with her coat still on.

"Is there a problem?"

The set dresser was about twenty-four and had come up beside her with the specific bright terror of a person whose department has just been looked at.

"It's alphabetical."

"...Yes?"

"It shouldn't be alphabetical." She was already taking them off. "It's in the order he saw them. It's — sorry, it's in the order the *character* saw them. It's in the book, it's chapter nine, he explains it to her and she says it's insane."

"I don't — " The dresser had gone pale. "The continuity script doesn't—"

"It won't be in the script, it's a thing he says on a page." She had four in her hands now. "It's fine, nobody's in trouble, I'll do it. What's your name?"

"Chelsea."

"Chelsea, get the other end and I'll tell you the order."

---

It took nineteen minutes and by the end of it there were four people doing it.

Chelsea, and a props assistant, and a man from the art department who turned up because a small crowd had formed and who then became extremely invested, and Ha-eun in a coat with a coffee going cold on a counter that was four inches too deep.

*Terminator 2* first, hard left, because he was six.

Then a gap of about nine spines that she could not fill and had to guess at, and guessed at out loud, and the art department man wrote them down as provisional.

Then the run she knew: *Robocop*, *Ferris*, the two Lynches in the wrong order — *Blue Velvet* before *Mulholland Drive* because of a bad VHS at fifteen — *Chungking Express*, the Kurosawas in a clump because he went through them all in one summer in 2009, and then a long stretch of things she had heard about in voice memos at four in the morning and could place to within about a year.

At the far right, the Hungarian one. March.

"How do you know all this," said Chelsea.

"I've read it about six times," Ha-eun said, which was true, and which was the answer she had been giving to that question in one form or another for nine months, and which nobody had ever once pushed on.

---

She was in the chair by twenty to eight and it was the worst forty minutes she had had in a year.

That was not the chair's fault. She had been in a chair like it perhaps four thousand times; she could sleep in one, and had; she knew the exact sequence and the exact conversation and the exact point at which a person stops talking to you and starts talking about you over your head to a second person, and none of that had ever cost her anything.

What was wrong was that they were taking things *off.*

"Less," said Marguerite Osei, from the doorway, for the third time.

Mags Osei was forty-four and had directed two films that Derek had a great deal to say about, and she had spent the first week of prep entirely uninterested in Ha-eun's fame and about ninety percent interested in her mouth.

"There's nothing on her," said Bo, who was doing it.

"There's a *lot* on her."

"There's foundation and — "

"Take the line off the eye." Mags came in and stood behind the chair and looked at her in the mirror, which nobody does, because everybody looks at your face when they talk to you and she looked at the mirror. "You've got a thing you do with the eye. It's very good. It's on every album cover you've ever done and I don't want it."

Ha-eun looked at herself in a mirror with a face on it that she had not seen since she was about nineteen.

"I look tired," she said.

"You look *awake*," Mags said. "That's the problem. Everybody's forgotten what it looks like." She put a hand briefly on the back of the chair. "Two things and then I'll leave you alone. One — the eye thing is what you do to a camera when you know it's on you. She doesn't know it's on her, ever, that's the entire part, so you don't get it."

"Okay."

"Two." Mags waited until she had her in the mirror. "You've never done this and you're going to be bad for about a day and a half. That's not a warning, that's a schedule. I've put the kitchen first because it's the easiest thing in the film and there's a hundred and forty people out there who are all going to be very kind to you, and you are going to hate every second of it, and then on Wednesday it'll go."

"That's very direct."

"You're a professional. I'd be insulting you." Mags was already going. "Bo, take the line off the eye."

---

Derek got there at eight because they had told him nine.

She watched him come around the flat and do exactly what she had done, which was stop dead, and she was aware of standing there with her arms folded watching a man look at his own kitchen and being extremely pleased with herself about the shelf.

He did not notice the shelf for four minutes. He noticed the lemon tree, and the hook, and the ring painted on the counter, and he crouched down to look at the baseboard, which nobody has ever done on a set in the history of sets.

Then he stood up and saw the shelf and went completely still.

"Who did that."

"Chelsea," Ha-eun said.

Chelsea, four feet away, made a noise.

"It's chronological."

"Mm."

"It's — " He walked the length of it, slowly, the way you walk along a fence. "That's not — the Lynches are in the wrong order, which is *correct*, because I had a bad tape at fifteen, and there's no way anybody—" He stopped at the gap in the middle. "There's nine missing."

"I couldn't remember. You've never told me the nine."

"You couldn't *remember*—"

"Don't do that. Don't do the — " She had already started laughing. "You're going to be strange about this and I'd like to get ahead of it."

"You did nineteen minutes of my *shelf.*"

"It was going to be wrong in the film, Derek. It would have been wrong forever. It would have been wrong in a *cinema.*"

---

He came and stood next to her at the counter and neither of them said anything for a second.

"They've made it four inches deep," he said.

"For the camera."

"I know it's for the camera. I'm saying it's four inches deep and I can't lean on it properly." He leaned on it improperly to demonstrate, at the wrong angle, and she took his coffee out of his hand and drank some and gave it back without looking. "It's the whole rhythm of the room. That's where I stand when I'm cooking, it's a *four-inch* difference and my entire—"

"You should tell them."

"I'm not going to tell them, there's a hundred and forty people out there and one of them has a *crane*—"

"Then stop leaning on it."

"I can't stop leaning on it, it's a *counter*, that's what it's for—"

She took hold of the front of his coat with one hand and moved him about eight inches to the left, which put him at the corner, where there was a run of about a foot that was the right depth because of where a cabinet had been cheated.

He leaned on it.

"Oh," he said.

"Mm."

"That's it. That's the spot."

"I know it's the spot." She was still holding the coat and did not appear to have registered that she was still holding it. "I've watched you stand in it for eight months. You do a thing with your hip."

"I don't do a thing with my hip—"

"You do a *whole* thing with your hip, Derek."

---

"Sorry," said Rahul Varma.

They both turned around.

He was standing about four feet behind them, at the mouth of the missing fourth wall, in Derek's clothes — the gray henley and the dark jacket, which the costume department had bought in the correct sizes from the correct stores — with a paper cup in one hand and a notebook open in the other.

Neither of them had any idea how long he had been there.

"Don't stop," he said. "Genuinely. I've got about a page and a half."

"How long have you—"

"Since the shelf." He clicked a pen. "Can I ask about the hip?"

Ha-eun let go of the coat.

"No," Derek said.

"Because I've had you standing at the sink for four days in my head and it's completely wrong, I've had you *square*, and you're not square, you're—" He put the cup down on a piece of set dressing, which somebody would move later, and did it: one shoulder down, weight on the far leg, the near hand on the counter with the fingers spread and the thumb tucked. It was extremely good. It was so good that Ha-eun said "*oh*" out loud before she could stop it.

"That's it," she said.

"That's it?"

"That's it. That's exactly it."

"Thank you." Rahul wrote something down. "And she moves you."

"She what," Derek said.

"She moves you. Physically. About eight inches, just now, by the coat, and you went where you were put." He was writing. "She's done it twice this morning and once in the casting session with a water bottle, and I need to know whether that's a *him* thing or a *her* thing, because it completely changes what I'm doing with the arms."

Nobody said anything for a second.

"It's a her thing," Ha-eun said.

"It is *absolutely* a her thing—"

"Great." Rahul shut the notebook. "That's the morning. Thanks."

And he picked up his coffee and walked off across the missing fourth wall toward a woman with a headset, entirely unbothered, at ten to nine in the morning on the first day of principal photography.

Derek looked at Ha-eun.

"He's been standing there since the *shelf.*"

"He's been standing there since the shelf," she agreed, and picked up the coffee she had already taken twice, and drank the rest of it.

---

They turned over at eleven-twenty, which was two hours late, which she was told repeatedly was extremely early.

The scene was four lines long. Maya Chen comes into a kitchen carrying two containers of food and puts them on a counter and says a thing about scallion pancakes, and a man says *don't*, and she says *I haven't said anything*, and he says *you have a face.*

That is the whole scene. It is nine seconds of film.

Ha-eun stood outside the flat in the dark part of the stage with a container in each hand and a hundred and forty people being quiet on her account, and heard a man she had never spoken to shout *last looks*, and had, for the first time since she was about seventeen, absolutely no idea what she was doing.

---

She was bad.

Mags had said a day and a half and it turned out to be more like four hours, and Ha-eun spent all four of them discovering things about herself that she had never had cause to discover, because nobody had ever asked her to walk into a room and *not perform.*

Take one, she hit the mark. She hit it exactly, the way you hit a mark, and got the line out clean and on the beat and gave the camera about nine percent of a face.

"Lovely," said Mags. "Again."

Take two she did it better and it was worse.

Take four, Mags came out from behind the monitor and walked across the set and stood in front of her.

"You're arriving," she said.

"I'm—"

"You're *arriving.* You come through that door like a woman entering a room, and Maya isn't entering a room, she's been in this apartment nine hundred times and she's carrying dinner and she's got a thing in her shoe." Mags put a hand on the doorframe. "Come in like the room is boring."

"Okay."

"Do you know what I mean?"

"No," Ha-eun said.

"Good, that's honest, that'll get us there faster." Mags looked past her at the crew. "Take five, and I want the food *heavy.* Give her a real bag, not a dressed one."

---

It went on for three hours and she got worse before she got better and the entire building was extremely kind to her about it, which was, as promised, unbearable.

At take nine she caught her own foot on the door bar and said a word in Korean that made the boom operator laugh, and Mags said "*that*, keep that, put that in eleven," and she could not reproduce it.

At take fourteen Rahul, standing at a counter with his back to her, said his line about the face and put a thing on the end of it that was not in the script — a small tired downward thing, the sound of a man who has had this argument two hundred times and is enjoying number two hundred and one — and she heard it and *answered* it before she had decided to, and it came out of her sideways and slightly annoyed and completely unperformed.

"Cut," said Mags, in an entirely different voice. "That's it. That's her. Do that again."

She could not do it again for four takes.

Then she did it again.

---

Derek watched all of it from behind a monitor with a headset on, in a chair he had been given without asking, and did not say one word for three hours.

He had a note at take three. He had a better one at take six. He wrote both of them on the back of a call sheet and did not give them to anybody, because there were a hundred and forty people in that building and exactly one of them was allowed to talk to an actor, and it was not the author, and he had worked that out in about eleven minutes on his first day and had decided to be a professional about it for the rest of his life.

What he had not expected — what nobody had warned him about, and what he would try to explain to Lucky in April and fail — was what it did to him to watch her be *bad.*

Not the badness. The badness was fine. Everybody is bad on a Monday.

It was that he had known her for a year and had never once watched her fail at anything in public. Not once. Not a note, not a room, not a press line, not a board. She was the most competent person he had ever met and she had been competent at him since March, and here she was at eleven-forty in the morning on a soundstage in Sun Valley getting a nine-second scene wrong for the ninth time in front of a hundred and forty strangers, with her face scrubbed of the thing she does with her eye, being told to come into a room as though it were boring.

And she kept going.

That was the whole of it. She did not get smaller, she did not get sharp with anybody, she did not manage the room — she said *okay* and *say that again* and *give me one more* about nine times, and at take fourteen she got it.

He sat behind a monitor with a headset on and thought, with a clarity that embarrassed him:

*Oh. There you are.*

---

They wrapped the setup at two-thirty and Mags said "that's a scene" and the room did the thing rooms do, which is nothing much, because it was nine seconds of film and there were forty-one more days.

Ha-eun came off the set and past his chair and did not stop, because there were people, and because she was going to the other side of the stage where a woman with a clipboard was waiting for her.

She got about four feet past him and turned around and came back.

"Was it any good?"

"Fourteen was good."

"Fourteen was the only good one."

"Fourteen was good," Derek said, "and eleven had a thing in it that they'll use, and everything before nine is unusable and everybody knows it including you, and by Thursday you won't be able to remember why any of this was hard."

She stood there for a second with her arms folded.

"That's not what people say to me."

"No."

"People say *that was great.*"

"I know what people say to you," he said. "Go on, there's a woman with a clipboard."

Ha-eun went.

At the far side of the stage, about ninety seconds later, she turned around and looked back at him across a hundred and forty people, for about a second, and then went into the corridor.
